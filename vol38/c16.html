Chapter 16: 禁猟区 [1053]

禁猟区〔1053〕

§ 1

The Baiu season is hazy and the day remains dark; moisture fills the houses, and all things sprout mold. On the twenty-first of Rokuguwatsu, when the mountain colors are obscured and the mist is thick, the Saotome, with water bubbles on their Tanahiji and mud smeared on their Mukamomo, form groups of three and five. Clad in the armor of Minokasa, wearing Teohikiyahan and Kotesuneate, they sing Tauta with courageous voices as they plant the wealth of the nation. In this fierce battlefield of farming where even the hands of dogs are needed, the three ruffians—Ankanbau Kiraku, Umeda Riugetsu, and Ōtsuki Denkichi—have just departed from the main temple. The road they tread through the Honmachi and Nishimachi is a narrow yet wide path; they dash toward the horse-riding ground, their six legs moving, and run to the Ayabe Station. Where are they headed? To the Wachi River. The Onumase railway bridge resounds loudly, and braving the Baiu, they reach Umesako Station. Soon after the train stops, they pass through the dark cave of Makura as if they own the place, roaring as they emerge. The mountain princess waves her blue sleeves, momentarily covering the three faces that are as black as charcoal; such is the atmosphere of the moment. Though still on the train, their hearts are brave and their spirits leap. In their great joy, they dance their way to Maizuru Station and flow into the shipping agent Nishikawa at the end of the Shinbashi bridge.

Perhaps struck by the large, heavy raindrops falling incessantly, the reserved boatman does not show his face even though the appointed time has arrived. Perhaps it is the will of the Lord God to thwart the three men's sinful plot to visit the islands, for the weather is spiteful, relentless, and unreservedly sends the god of water. No matter how long they wait, the sky shows no sign of clearing, but they were prepared for rain from the start. However, they are greatly distressed that the essential boatman has not appeared. Yet, the three, who would rather die than return halfway, urge him on, saying, 'Even if one stays on the tatami, one dies when the time comes; life and death are of Heaven, and it is Kannagara. Please, go and call the boatman.' The widow of Nishikawa has no choice but to send someone to summon Tanaka and Hashimoto. They are the boatmen who served as attendants when the Kyōso of Deguchi first opened the way to Kutsushima. Among thousands of fishermen, they are the most courageous and renowned for their skill. The three were overjoyed, thinking that with them, it would be fine and they would surely agree immediately, but to their disappointment, the men speak with cowardice.

Ankanbau Kiraku, Umeda Riugetsu, and Ōtsuki Denkichi: 'Even if you are on a pilgrimage of faith and have the protection of the gods, you cannot go in this weather, for it is not a matter of demons. Well, please play and wait here for two or three days. When the weather settles, I shall accompany you. Tomorrow is the once-a-year festival of the deity of Kanmurijima, and tonight the deity of Kanmurijima will ride a divine boat to cross the sea to the Araizaki Shrine on the opposite shore, so it is a night of terror. I cannot possibly put the boat out; it would be a disaster if I were to offend the will of the gods. Unless you are Kamezō of Kuwana, no one will put a boat out tonight.'

Bewitched by a wind of cowardice, the boatman refuses to give a favorable answer. The three, even more so, wish to go on a day like tonight when it is said one cannot. 'By all means, we do not care about the fare, please do it...' they beg as if weeping. The boatman, driven further by fear, tries to sneak away in cowardice. Fearing they will be abandoned, the three take turns coaxing and persuading him, holding up the divine oracle of the Kyōso—that if one proceeds straight with courage and determination, even demons will avoid one—as their shield. Their argument reaches no conclusion, and finally, the boatman closes his mouth and stands dumbfounded, refusing them entirely. Like a boat in the offing caught by the waves, there is no island to cling to. Though they are as gentle as sheep in ordinary times, they are like fierce tigers when it comes to carrying out their purpose; they advance straight ahead, seeing neither wind, rain, nor ocean in their eyes. Their courage is like a raging fire, and they are prepared to throw themselves into the fray as if death were a homecoming. However, what can they do? The three do not know how to steer a boat, and once abandoned by the essential engineer, they are like stone Buddhas rather than gods, unable to advance even an inch upon the sea. 'Then we shall wait until tomorrow morning at one o'clock; it is a certainty like a watermelon that the rain will stop and it will become clear weather, so please go with peace of mind, for there will surely be divine help for our departure.'

Speaking such nonsense, the two laughed at the unreliable prophecy, and with looks as if they thought the others were fools, they reluctantly went home.

The three curled up like insects in the room of the wholesale merchant and went to sleep. They were likely rowing a boat on the river of dreams. Just when they thought they had taken a nap, someone knocked on the gate of the Ōnifuya and shouted,

'Customers, the daytime boatman has come.'

Thinking, 'Now, it is time,' they jumped up at once, and fearing the divine will might change, they immediately began their preparations.

'Boatman-san, the weather will be zero, won't it?'

When they teased him thus, he gave a reply that was still not quite clear,

'Well, the signs seem very good, but one cannot know until one goes as far as one can.'

The time of arrival came, and it was exactly two o'clock in the morning on the twenty-second when they left the harbor. As expected, the sky was completely overcast, and not a single star or a patch of blue cloud could be seen, but a fine rain that would raise the price of rice was stinging the faces of the party as if to frighten them. When they rowed out to the vicinity of the usual Minamitomari, they crossed paths with a large steamship speeding along, illuminating the sea surface with its lights. That they were tossed about at will by its wake was truly irritating and unbearable. After a while, the sky shed its robe of rain clouds here and there, revealing the skin of blue clouds, and though the celestial script appeared with flickering dots, perhaps because the water in the inner sea was muddy from the continuous rainfall, the pure stars were not borrowing a lodging in the waves tonight. As they rowed on, leaving Bakigasaki behind, the eastern sky began to blush, and the morning sun emerged majestically from the distant mountain peaks; Kanmurijima and Kutsujima lay before their eyes. Feeling an indescribable sense of vastness in their hearts and unable to suppress their joy, they bowed to the sacred islands, and each offered up prayers of gratitude.

The sea was extremely calm, and waves like crepe were flowing beautifully. The boatmen were drenched in sweat, rowing with all their might from both stern and bow. It was exactly five minutes to six when the small boat arrived at Kanmurijima, as if shooting an arrow or a bird in flight.

Although it usually takes more than ten to twelve hours for a one-way trip, it was truly unexpected that it took less than four hours this time. Kiraku was overflowing with triumph and said,

Kiraku: 'This is what happens when a person with light sins like Ankanbō comes to worship; the gods are impartial and selfless.'

He was getting carried away all by himself. With formal attire, they slowly advanced to the front of the shrine, offered gifts, recited prayers, and after finishing their worship, they respectfully withdrew from the shrine building. For the sake of a memento, I received one round stone from in front of the shrine. Of course, since I had brought a stone for exchange, I did not just take it. Today being the festival day of the Myōjin, several parishioners had been coming and going to the shrine office since the previous day, cleaning the grounds.

'You have come to worship from early on; well, have a cup of tea,'

While deeply thanking them for their kindness in offering a seat, they returned to the boat landing on the beach. The famous mulberry tree of Kanmurijima had been illegally felled for someone's sake last summer and left no trace; only a mulberry tree with a circumference of about three shaku was lying on the shore, uprooted and withered. It was truly a matter beyond indignation.

'Since around the year before last, groups of sixty or seventy people have been coming from Yokohama and Kobe, poaching five or six hundred thousand shearwaters, so lately the birds have decreased significantly, and it is hindering the fishing, which is troubling everyone,'

The two boatmen said sadly as they rowed out toward Kutsujima.

On the Nakatsukami rock between Kanmurijima and Kutsujima, dozens of seabirds were watching the party's boat pass by, their figures reflected in the water, their wings flapping as if to say this was fine. Gazing with interest at the rocky cliffs of Asakuri and Tana-no-shita on their left, they rowed to the spot directly below the Kane-no-iwa, where the voices of various birds were chirping. It was a marvelous and magnificent sight, and they felt a sense of purity in their hearts.

In the thirty-fourth year of Meiji, as one looked out, the mountains and fields were hazy, fragrant with the scent of flowers; the spring mist, like thinned paste poured out, harmonized the panoramic composition of the scenery, birds sang in the branches of fresh green, and butterflies danced among the golden rape blossoms—a favorable season indeed. The sea of Mahizuru saw white waves rolling in gently, yellow in the distance and white as they drew near, reflecting the sunlight and bordering the spring sea, which was heavy with water vapor; in the midst of this indescribable, magnificent scenery of peace under heaven, Kyōso Deguchi led thirty-five disciples, climbed to the summit of this Tsuriganeiha, drew the birth-water of the Heavenly Rock Cave and the pure water of the Dragon Palace from the upper reaches of the Miyagawa in Tango Province, and looking down upon the expanse of the sea, reverently sprinkled it, and prayed, saying:

Kyōso: 'Three years from now, there shall surely be a war between Japan and Russia. At that time, a great nation like a giant and a small nation like a child will engage in a great war of trial of strength upon a grand stage, under the watchful eyes of the nations of the world; should it happen, by some misfortune, that our nation ends the war at a disadvantage, that would be a catastrophe, and the Japanese Empire would never be able to lift its head for ten thousand generations to come. Therefore, the divine spirit of Kunitokotachi-no-Ōkami greatly worries over this, and has sent this old body here to pray for world peace and for the exaltation of the national prestige of the Japanese Empire. Ah, Lord Ushitora no Konjin, Kunitokotachi-no-Mikoto, I beseech Thee, bestow upon our divine land of Japan Thy protection, as vast as the Pacific and as deep as the Sea of Japan, so that this pure birth-water and beautiful clear water may circle the ocean, become clouds, become rain, or become snow and hail, to moisten the five continents universally, sweep away the evil spirits of the world, wash away the filth, construct heaven upon earth, make the Toyoashihara-no-Mizuho-no-Kuni a true paradise, and manifest a golden world.'

This is the very precipice where he prayed with such great volume that it seemed as if heaven and earth would crumble, pouring into it the full sincerity and faith of his heart; hearing this tale of joy, the desire to climb the rock at least once welled up in the party unexpectedly, and they could no longer restrain themselves.

They asked the boatman to moor the boat as best he could, and when they climbed up, they felt a trembling in their hands and feet, and could not help but marvel anew at the courage with which Kyōso was filled. The renowned Maitreya Bodhisattva revealed his heroic figure solemnly upon the natural rock, as if a giant were looking down upon humans, who appeared like beans, and one could feel a sense of sacred inviolability everywhere. When they cast their eyes far to the northeast, the waves of the Sea of Japan were like a series of silver screens, a great mass of gold shone in the eastern sky, and the sea at their feet was like a quilt of emerald silk; the beauty and magnificence were beyond compare. After admiring it for a long time, they returned to the boat and inspected the Wani-no-Su protruding rock; they clapped their hands at the wonders, calling them strange and mysterious, and felt as if their spirits were entranced and they had ascended to heaven.

The boat escaped from beneath the Oniiwa without mercy and barely managed to row to Togakushiiwa. What irritated them immediately upon arrival was that, most insolently, poachers had entered even such a sacred divine island, chosen a small patch of flat land to build a straw hut, and while shielding themselves from the rain and dew, were day and night spreading bird nets and carrying clubs; a fifty-year-old man with a terrifying appearance, with a dirty face and a thick mustache, who looked as if he would not hesitate to make a human his prey if he were hungry, appeared to be the ringleader, and he was employing many laborers to capture albatrosses in the midst of their work. However, upon seeing our party in religious attire from afar, they were for some reason flustered and ran about in confusion, some running up toward the mountain, others dashing recklessly along the precipices, to the point where one wondered what had happened. The shrine built by Deguchikyōso ten years ago had been exposed to the wind and rain of many years, falling into a state of semi-ruin, and it was so awe-inspiring that we wished to rebuild it as soon as possible, vowing before the gods to complete the construction by next spring without fail. The tens of thousands of albatrosses left behind flew unreservedly over the heads of us members, raining down excrement upon the sleeves of our sacred vestments and ruining our only robes. Looking, the upper part was separated by a sheer cliff, and below our eyes, the seawater drifted blue in the depths of a deep valley, creating a terrifying sight.
