Chapter 19: The Episode of 鞍 Horse Mountain (Part II) [1056]

鞍馬山（二）〔1056〕

§ 1

As it happened, that very night was the day appointed for the ceremony of the Saijin and the relocation of the Kuwaigafushiyo at Yagi, and as many Kuwaiin had gathered, they were taken by surprise and filled with joyous excitement upon hearing of the unexpected Otachiyo of the Kyōso and his Itsukau, causing a great Ōsaudō that turned everything upside down; unable to watch this any longer, the Kyōso restrained them and offered an Ingin Aisatsu. It was truly pitiable that the Kuwaiin, all of whom had been inattentive in failing to provide detailed Haukoku or applications in advance, were now reaping the consequences of their negligence before their very eyes, having carelessly performed the Taisetsu Oshiki of relocating the Hōsaishiyo of the Ōkami, thereby compounding the sin of disrespect toward the Shinrei. Fortunately, they entreated the Kyōso to act as the Saishu to complete the Itenshiki, and subsequently, there were lectures by the Kyōso and Kaichō; at eleven o'clock in the evening, the Kuwaiin all departed, having been blessed with the twelve-fold divine virtues. The Indian monk considered his sutras to be of the utmost importance, while we considered the morrow to be vital; fearing that staying up late would be detrimental to our health, we slept together in the cramped room, and on the following day, the ninth, when the morning sun revealed its round face at the edge of the eastern mountains, we used the clear water of a flowing stream to wash our hands and rinse our mouths, then reverently focused our prayers before the gods, divining the nature of our journey ahead. At that time, the Imperial God taught and admonished us through the hand of Kaichō:

'The Kurama Mountain of the human heart in this world, Opens this path through the divine spirit-fire.'

Having discerned from this divine poem that our destination was Kurama Mountain in the province of Jōshū, my heart leaped with courage like that of Ushiwakamaru at Gojō Bridge, and at one o'clock in the afternoon, escorted by Mr. Fukushima, we made our way to the Yagi station. Just then, the up-train from Sonobe arrived; seizing the opportunity, we jumped aboard, and after a busy two-minute stop, the train crossed the iron bridge with a thunderous sound over the Ōi River, passed the castle ruins of Yagi, and sped along the double tracks, quickly approaching Kameoka. The mountain range of Sogabe, the hometown of Kaichō, stood out remarkably, and thinking that the sacred peak of Mt. Takakuma must be over there, I found myself clapping my hands in prayer before I knew it. The sacred peak of Mt. Atago towered majestically in the center of the overlapping mountains, and I felt as though it were watching the departure of the Kyōso and his Itsukau, silently praying for the safety and success of our journey. Inside the train, I happened to see a certain villager from Sogabe. When I tried to speak to him, he deliberately turned his back as if he did not know me, and from time to time, he would glance sideways at us; it was not a very pleasant feeling. He was a Magatsukami who, having once lived in Kaichō's hometown, had considered the state of the nation and, out of a sense of patriotism, had sacrificed his own person and family to fly the flag of the Imperial Way spiritual learning—the great path of Kannagara—only to obstruct us with all his might, both overtly and covertly; I wondered if he had turned his back because his conscience was pricked by his own brazen face, feeling ashamed now. But to my surprise, that was not the case; he was staring at the travel attire of our group, misidentifying us as beggars and pilgrims, and upon returning home, he laughed and said:

'Ueda has drowned himself in strange teachings and has finally fallen into the status of a beggar. Those who are enthusiastic about Shinto should take this as a warning and never perform the foolish act of abandoning the Buddhist path that our ancestors have followed and revered, only to be deluded by Shinto. That man Ueda has been disowned by his relatives, shunned by his friends, abandoned by his younger brothers and sisters, and has been forced to wander to other places, having left his beloved hometown where he was accustomed to living; now, he is carrying his foster mother and wife with him, living the life of a floating cloud and flowing water.'

He went to the trouble of spreading such malicious words and ugly rumors far and wide, and it is said that my relatives in my hometown also felt no small amount of trouble because of it.

Born in the divine land of Japan, eating the rice of the divine land, and bathing in divine grace, there is nothing more foolish, stubborn, or poisonous to this path than the words of an Indian-spirited petty person who, having forgotten the fundamental principle of 'repaying the origin and returning to the beginning' (hōhon-hanshi), has been bewitched by a heretical religion.

The train proceeded relentlessly through Yamamoto and Utsuda, and as it passed through the first tunnel and then the second, third, and fourth, the clear rapids of the Hozugawa River, with its strange stones and grotesque rocks scattered in the swift current, were fascinating: the 'Book Rock' that can never be finished reading, the 'Abacus Rock' that can never be counted, the 'Folding Screen Rock' standing amidst the rapids and waterfalls, and the 'Lotus Rock'—which Buddhist followers gaze upon with admiration and longing—all seen down in the riverbed. As we crossed the iron bridge over the ravine like an arrow, we quickly reached the hot spring resort of Arashikyōkan. Before we could fully appreciate it, we passed through the Kameyama Tunnel, which celebrates the reign of our sovereign for ten thousand generations, and upon emerging, we arrived at Saga station, where 'dumplings are preferred over flowers.' During the five-minute stop, looking to the right, one could see the Arashiyama of moon, snow, and flowers; although there were no flowers in the autumn season, the brocade coloring the spaces between the pine trees, the moon shining over the Togetsukyō Bridge, the Katsura River with its flowing rafts, and the Namidagawa River where Ohan and Chōemon left their famous names—all were visible. The great temple of Tenryū, with its clear stream, had its roof tiles rising high into the clouds, just as depicted in the painting by the master artist Matsunen, and the Zen spirit of Gasan was fragrant in the air. Looking at the Chiba of Kadono, one could see the garden of the Momochitariya and the bravery of the country. To the left was Mt. Ogura, famous for the anthology of Lord Teika, the Sonō-in temple with its flowers and autumn leaves, the Shaka-dō hall where the Buddha and ancestors are enshrined, and to the north was the Ninna-ji temple of Omuro, its five-storied pagoda piercing the clouds; the majority of the passengers boarding and alighting here must be believers visiting these sites. At the sound of the whistle, the train began to move. Soon the train reached Hanazono station, and the Itsukau jumped off, unable to wait for the conductor to open the doors. While looking at the Myōshin-ji temple, the head temple of the Zen sect, to the side, the Kyōso walked at the head of the group, not at all like an old man, and with a staff of plum, pine, and bamboo, he stood firmly before the torii gate of Kitano. Today, the twenty-fifth of the solar calendar, was the festival of the shrine, and it was the very height of the portable shrine procession; the mounted Shinto priests, in their solemn crowns and vestments, marched to the sound of resonant music, and the sight of them parading before and after the portable shrine was most magnificent. Tens of thousands of worshippers simultaneously changed their demeanor, straightened their collars, and clapped their hands. At the shrine entrance, the thousand-year-old pine trees, plum groves, and maple and miscellaneous trees were covered in moss, creating a divine atmosphere of antiquity. The Kyōso stopped here, and after clapping his hands and bowing twice, he turned to us, our Itsukau, and...

His clear voice became increasingly clouded, and his shining eyes were seen to blink incessantly... Swayed by the true feelings expressed while receiving the drizzling rain of the village, which one dislikes on a journey, upon his sleeves, even Kaichō and Sumiko could only voice their emotions, and hiding their faces, which were weeping in sympathy, under their sedge hats, the five companions dragged their staves and hurried toward Kurama.

Upon finally arriving at Kurama, they decided to hold an all-night vigil in front of the shrine that night. Shikata Haruzō received an Omikuji prepared in front of the temple, but it appeared to be quite bad, and unexpectedly,

Haruzō: 'Oh, I failed.'

He spoke out. That night, Fukurin fell into a deep sleep due to the fatigue of the journey, but when he suddenly woke up around one o'clock in the middle of the night and looked around, he was surprised to see that the figure of Shikata Haruzō, who had been by his side, was gone, and as he searched, he heard a voice calling repeatedly, 'Please wake up,' coming from outside, and when he strained his ears, it was certainly the voice of Shikata. When Fukurin hurried outside, he saw a large fireball running back and forth in front of the shrine, and he recognized the figure of Shikata Haruzō, whom he was looking for, in the tail of that fireball, and for the first time, he understood the location of the voice he had just heard. While watching with an eerie feeling, the fireball gradually moved ahead, so he fearfully went in that direction and found that Shikata was making a large bonfire. Fukurin approached and,

Fukurin: 'What on earth happened?'

When he asked, Shikata, with a pale face and trembling, replied,

Shikata: 'Oh, scary, scary, there is nothing as scary as this; if you had seen what I just saw, you would have nothing to say.'

Shikata Haruzō leaked sighs continuously from beginning to end, but a messenger arrived from his home in Unegadani to fetch him, and after returning home, he suffered from illness for about one month and passed away. Prior to that time, a messenger arrived saying,

Shikata: 'Unless Sensei comes to visit me at least once while I yet live, I cannot die even though I am to die,'

whereupon Kaichō went to visit him, and when he said,

Kaichō: 'I forgive you,'

Shikata passed away at peace. Haruzō was eighteen years of age at the time, a man who had truly mastered spiritual science, yet it was a deeply regrettable thing that, as a result of pride and misapprehension, he incurred divine punishment and ended his single life.

One night, a great wind suddenly blew, and the cedar trees of the grand hall roared with a mighty gō-gō sound. When they later inquired of the Kyōso, the Kyōso recounted that the Great Bishop of Mount Kurama had arrived and settled upon Mount Hongū, while his retainers went to the great cedar of the parade ground, and thereafter, countless retainers could be seen upon the great cedar like a swarm of bees.

原文（近代文語）

と云ふのみにて打ち明けもせず泣いて居る。それから連れ立ちて御宮へ戻り再び寝に就き、夜明けてから更めて四方に夜半の出来事を尋ねたけれど、四方は何も知らぬと云ふ。念の為め昨夜焚火せる処へ行つて見たが其跡さへ無き不思議に福林は只驚くばかりであつた。海潮は教祖に向ひ今度鞍馬参りの神慮を伺ひしに、教祖は只、

教祖『先に行つたら分りませう』

と云はれしのみであつた。

帰途は京都より亀岡へ出で八木にて一泊せしが四方は終日蒼白な顔して悄気込み居たりし様見るも憐れであつた。同人は其夜園部まで二里の行程を走つて友人に会ひ、

四方『今度は死ぬやも知れぬ』

とて暇乞ひを成して帰れる由、教祖は此事を聞きて叱つてゐられた。

翌日綾部の役員信者は途中迄出迎ひに出て無事大広前に帰り着く。四方春三は始終太息を洩らし居たが上谷の宅より迎ひに来り、帰宅して後一ケ月ほど煩ひて帰幽して了つた。其より前、

四方『生前是非先生に一度来て貰はねば死ぬにも死なれぬ』

とて使ひが来たから海潮は見舞に行き、

海潮『許してやる』

と言へば安心して帰幽した。春三時に十八才、実に霊学に達したる男であつたが慢心取違ひの末、神罰を蒙りて一命を終はつたのは遺憾の事であつた。

或夜俄に大風吹きて広前の杉の樹、ゴウゴウと唸りし事がある。後教祖に伺ひしに、鞍馬山の大僧正来りて本宮山へ鎮まり又其眷族は馬場の大杉へ行つたが其後大杉には蜂の如く沢山の眷族が見えたと教祖は物語られた。
