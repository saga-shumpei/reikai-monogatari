The group opposed to the Kyōso and the Kuwaichiyō gathered their heads together in whispers, mocking and scoffing with the assumption that while the five members of the Kyōso's party had fortunately reached Oshima safely, it was impossible for an old woman like the Kyōso to reach Meshima; they were certain that she would surely incur the wrath of Kami, causing their boat to capsize and become mere seaweed in the ocean, or that the attempt would end in failure and they would be forced to turn back halfway. However, upon hearing the success story that the Meshimabira had been accomplished once again, they became competitive and twenty stubborn members of the group plotted a pilgrimage to Meshima, only to encounter a great storm and, barely escaping with their lives to Oshima, narrowly survived; this is the story of that event.

In the Qing dynasty across the sea, the Boxer Rebellion was underway, and the allied forces of various nations were marching toward Beijing to protect their residents. Here, the Kyōso of Deguchi, concerned for the future of the Orient, crossed to the uninhabited islands twice in accordance with the divine command of Kami, and despite enduring hardships and suffering in these adventurous undertakings, the believers who had been deluded by Adachi and other officials leaned entirely toward selfishness. Driven by their own base hearts, they criticized the adventurous sea crossings of the Kyōso's party one after another, and there were only those who loudly indulged in heated abuse, claiming that the Kyōso was merely driven by curiosity or was employing these means to gather more believers. As the judge Haliburton said, 'Where there is authority, there is a natural tendency toward disobedience.' How true this is; those who were jealous of the growing fame of the Kyōso and Ueda in recent years, and who were preoccupied with various movements of exclusion and obstruction, saw the success of the two pilgrimages to the isolated island and increased their spirit of jealousy and resistance. This time, several individuals, including so-and-so, held a secret meeting and insisted that they must take the Kuwaichiyō as their guide to Meshima, climb the cliffs of Kaneiwa, and fulfill their various ill-intentioned desires, refusing to stop their demands that the Kuwaichiyō, who knew the way, accompany them once more. If he were to refuse even slightly, they would surely slander him as a coward; for him, whose memory was still fresh of the times he had been led into their treacherous schemes and encountered life-or-death crises, this was akin to walking with wolves and tigers. It was a pilgrimage of extreme danger, where one could not know when or how one might be bitten to death. Nevertheless, thinking that folding one's flag out of fear of the enemy was not the true intent of one who propagates the way of Kami, he firmly resolved to purify his body and mind through daily bathing and abstinence, and relying on the protection of Kami and the divine virtue of the Kyōso, he consented to set sail and make the pilgrimage with them.

Considering the possibility of any contingency, he took along the usually fervent believers—Fusatarō Takehara, Keitarō Kinoshita, Yumatsu Moritsu, Yasunosuke Fukubari, Tokita, Yasuzō Shikata, Jinnojō, and others—and it was decided that the twenty-one people, including both friend and foe who were not of one mind, would finally carry out the plan on the twentieth day of the seventh month of the thirty-third year of Meiji.

At dawn on the twenty-first, four fishing boats carrying the sin-laden party rowed out of Maizuru Port, and after about seven ri at sea, Oshima came into view as if it could be grasped by hand. Just when they thought they were one step away, the eastern sky suddenly changed to a strange color. The four boatmen were flustered and, speaking in unison, cried out:

Boatmen: 'Now, everyone, prepare yourselves, for something terrible has begun. Judging by the look of those clouds, it is a great hurricane.'

Before their eyes, black, ominous clouds welled up in the northeastern sky. Before they could even think that the wind had grown rougher, the hurricane struck; the tide was kicked up, the waves roared in fury, and the sound of the sea wind, surging and violent, howled as it raged to the south, north, east, and west. Amidst the tumultuous noise, waves like mountains and waves like solid, jagged rocks approached; the horror of it was beyond what words could describe. One moment the small boats were tossed into the mid-heaven like leaves, and the next they were plunged into the valleys of thousand-fathom waves, tossed about at will, without regard for the eyes of the Funadama-no-Kami. The party's boats, hidden between the waves, were nowhere to be seen, and they all trembled in their hearts. It was as if they were traveling through hell; all were gasping for breath, like leeches sprinkled with salt or mice encountering a cat, clinging to the bottom of the boats and cowering. Everyone was so weakened that they could not even utter a sound; among them, a Kyoto believer named Mototoshi Matsui, perhaps having accepted his fate, remained as unmoved as a rock, transcending life and death, and while being drenched in salt water from head to toe, he remained calm and composed, gazing only at one part of the sky. Kintarō Tokita, saying that his bladder was about to burst, urinated timidly toward the raging waves. The boatman shouted in a stern, scolding tone, '...Such a big man standing up is dangerous...' Fearing for their lives with all their might, the Ichidō—men who would not even bother to pick up a fallen broom in ordinary times—were desperately bailing out the Chōsui with buckets, tea bowls, and ladles, waiting for the Mimahi of the next wave. The rain fell Paizen as if covering a tub, pouring down as if flowing from an axle, striking them mercilessly from above their heads. The vast, boundless sea was now wrapped in darkness so deep that one could not distinguish even a few inches. The raging waves were churning and tossing, with a fierce power that would not cease until they had pulverized even the rocks of the rough shore. Human beings are truly weak creatures when it comes to such a pass. Perhaps each of them felt they had incurred the divine reprimand of God, for they vomited out all the filth they had been harboring in their bellies and repented of their past wrongs. Everyone, in the manner of 'praying to God only when in trouble,' bowed with single-minded devotion, chanting divine texts with their mouths, and apologizing to God. 'Sensei must be a Kami,' they exclaimed, clicking their tongues in admiration. Indeed, our country is the Mikuni of Kami; it possesses the way of Kamunagara, the Iugenbimeu Shinkyō, and the Kototama of infinite Shinriki, making it a truly noble Tengoku and Jiyōdo. The boat was finally able to take refuge at Oshima. First, they offered gifts at the Oitojima Jinjiya and recited Norito to express gratitude for saving their lives; next, they performed Euhai toward the Ayabe Hongū. Believing that for those among the Itsukau burdened with heavy sins, a Sanpai to Meshima would Tautei never be acceptable to the divine will, they prayed once more for the safety of their return journey. Under the thick protection of Kami, they finally arrived back at Maizuru and decided to stay there for one night.

As the Kaichō lay resting from his fatigue in a room on the second floor of the Ōniuya, a man named Sugiura Mankichi appeared at his bedside. Placing his hands on the floor and showing signs of repentance on his face, he let tears fall as he spoke.

Sugiura: 'There is something for which I must apologize to you, Sensei. It is a fearful thing to even mention, but the truth is that several of us consulted together, intending to take the scroll in your possession, and furthermore, we plotted to do such and such. We wished to clear away the clouds and mist for the sake of our association and install Shikata Haruzō-san in your place to carry out our own designs. As a means to that end, we forcibly requested that you perform the Sanpai to Meshima. However, the fact that we met with such a life-threatening ordeal due to the severe judgment of Kami must surely be a divine warning for us to repent. Please, I beg you to forgive our sins.'

He bowed his head low and told this terrifying story. Having already sensed something of it since the previous day, the Kaichō was not overly surprised and laughed, forgiving him.

After a while, Shikata Heizō came from the Ayabe headquarters by order of the Kyōso, accompanied by Shikata Haruzō, to meet the group returning from Meshima. They celebrated the safety of everyone and danced with joy, as if they had returned from a journey to the Meido.

Shikata Haruzō, hearing of Sugiura's repentance, could no longer keep it hidden and confessed the conspiracy in detail.

Shikata: 'Ah, an evil Kami must have been lurking in my heart. From now on, I will surely repent.'

Seeing him shed tears of sincerity and apologize so frankly, the Kaichō actually began to feel pity for him. As the saying goes, 'after rain, the earth hardens'; that everyone, one and all, repented at once through this disaster and came to recognize the dawn of the Way was entirely due to the profound intentions of Kami, and the Kaichō made his faith all the more firm.

On the evening of the twenty-second, they returned safely to the building, paid their respects before the Kami, and each returned to their own home.
