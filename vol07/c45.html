Chapter 45: The Spirit of Wine [345]

第45章　酒魂〔345〕

§ 1

Having crouched down for a moment, Katoriwake breathed a sigh of relief as the crowd dispersed and vanished, and, as if to say he was the king of the castle, he shook his bald kettle-like head, squared his elbows before the bonfire, and strutted proudly.

There, Hinode-no-kami, one of the four missionaries who had appeared composedly, gently tapped Katoriwake on the shoulder and said,

"Excuse me, Missionary Katoriwake, I have met you in an unexpected place. Hearing just now from behind the shade of the trees, it seems you walk about singing the missionary songs of Urarihiko. That is truly hard work. I, whom you met at the council of Tokoyo-no-kuni, am Daidōwake—or rather, the false Tokoyohiko-no-mikoto."

Upon hearing this, Katoriwake turned around, glared fixedly at the face of Hinode-no-kami, stuck his buttocks out backward, and was already poised to flee.

Hinode-no-kami said, "You are carrying a great many gourds, are you not?"

Takateruhiko said, "I thought it was a gourd, but what is this, it is a shiny face. He is a missionary who looks very much like a gourd."

Hinode-no-kami said, "Again you talk about others. Please stop criticizing people's faces."

Takateruhiko said, "Ha-ha-yes."

Katoriwake said, "Ah, after that I was cast out of Tokoyo-no-kuni, drifted and drifted, and spent long months and years on Mount Kijō at the headwaters of Niagara Falls, but I did bad things and was finally driven out of Mount Kijō as well, and after that, hearing the missionary songs of Lord Urarihiko, I was so roused that I was ready to leap into the air, and now, as you see, I carry gourds and am carrying out mission work in the world. Are you also, after all, missionaries of Urarihiko?"

Hinode-no-kami said, "We are missionaries of the Ananaikyō."

Hearing that they were missionaries, Katoriwake tried to run away.

Hinode-no-kami said, "Now, now, please wait. Although I walk about like this and outwardly teach not to drink sake, not to drink, in reality, if the fellows of the world drink sake, the sake we drink will run out and cause trouble, so I go around stopping the fellows of the world, saying, 'Do not drink; if you drink, it is bad,' while secretly drinking all by myself. Even if you tell people to quit the sake they love, it will never stop. In this world, you see, there are always an obverse and a reverse. It is fine for you to walk about saying drink sake, drink, but if you make the people of the world drink only sake too much, will not the sake you drink run out entirely? Lord Urarihiko is truly benevolent. You too must be admiring that benevolence and preaching it. And getting your head struck here and there, having your gourds broken—being a missionary in this world must be no ordinary hardship; I sympathize with you. Will you not reconsider things at this point, become a missionary of the Ananaikyō, and have the mind to drink delicious sake all by yourself? In the world, everything has a reverse if it has an obverse. If there is day, there is night. Even a single sheet of paper has a front and a back. However great Lord Urarihiko may be, having only a reverse will not do. From now on, making the Ural religion the obverse and the Ananaikyō the reverse, both front and back being complete, will you not come out with us to preach together?"

§ 2

Katoriwake: "Ah, you understand well. Until now, I have met many missionaries of Ananaikyō, but they were ever so rigid and uncompromising—true sticklers—enduring even the sake they wished to drink by saying that the gods would be angered and so forth, holding back their rumbling throats, swallowing their saliva, and straining with wide-open eyes in their agony. In such a state, death would be better; it is truly a case of 'what good are cherry blossoms without sake?' If it is a teaching such as yours, I will gladly accompany you from this very moment. Truth be told, I also wished to do so, but since my wits do not work well, I have lived with absolute honesty. You are truly a deity who understands well. If it were not so, one could not make one's way through the world."

Thus changing his expression, he shook his shoulders and rejoiced.

The three missionaries, not understanding, pulled at the straw raincoat of Hinode-no-Kami and said,

"Excuse me, is not what you say somewhat mistaken?"

Hinode-no-Kami: "It is not mistaken. The craftsmanship is intricate; please watch the final result."

The whole company bore dubious expressions.

As the night deepened profoundly, and it reached the hour of the ox when even the waters sleep, Hinode-no-Kami offered some prayer. Instantly, from whence none knew, a fireball like a mirror that illuminated the four directions descended upon that spot.

All were astonished and gazed intently at the fireball. This was indeed the Kushimitama of Hinode-no-Kami, which had just descended from heaven. Before their very eyes, it turned into a large jar of light. An indescribably fragrant sake was filled almost to overflowing. Katoriwake twitched his nose,

Katoriwake: "Oh, my, what splendid sacred sake; shall I partake of it?"

and already tried to leap toward the jar.

Hinode-no-Kami: "Now, wait. If you lay your hands on this sake or ladle it out with a dipper, divine punishment will strike. This splendid sacred sake granted from heaven will have no flavor unless you quietly put your mouth to it and drink."

Katoriwake: "Without even touching my hands to such a large jar, and without using a ladle, how can I drink it?"

Hinode-no-Kami: "I shall let you drink it now."

Katoriwake: "Thank you; please let me drink it quickly."

Hinode-no-Kami once again recited some divine prayer toward the jar. Instantly, the jar became flat sideways, and in turn, its height grew lower. It became just right for putting one's mouth to it.

Katoriwake: "Ah, while I was watching, the jar entered into the ground. It has come to just the right height compared to the stature of my body."

§ 3

Hinodenokami said, "Now, I shall give you a drink."

So saying, he cast a spiritual binding upon Katoriwake's body. From the neck down, Katoriwake became as stiff as timber.

Katoriwake was so captivated by the sake that he did not even notice his body had stiffened, and merely shook his head. Hinodenokami firmly grasped Katoriwake's two legs and thrust him out over the jar, whereupon Katoriwake stuck out his tongue and said,

Katoriwake said, "Excuse me, please lower me a little more, about an inch; I cannot reach."

Hinodenokami said, "All right, all right."

So saying, he lowered him by seven or eight-tenths of an inch. Katoriwake stuck out his tongue with all his might and said,

Katoriwake said, "Could you not lower me just one-tenth more? I cannot reach."

Hinodenokami said, "All right, all right."

Katoriwake said, "Look, that is the opposite; you have raised me. A little lower."

Hinodenokami raised and lowered Katoriwake, never letting him reach the sake. Katoriwake squeezed out his voice and said,

"Ah, what a clumsy person; please lower me a little more."

In this manner, about an hour passed. Katoriwake,

"Ka, ka, ka,"

cleared his throat with this sound, and at the same moment three heated stones flew out; the instant they fell into the jar with a sizzling sound, the jar vanished like smoke.

The entire area returned to the dark night as before, with only the shapes of twinkling stars in the sky and Katoriwake's head dimly glowing through the darkness on the ground.

From this time on, Katoriwake came to completely dislike sake, came to believe in Ananai-kyō, traveled various regions as an attendant to Omonagitsukasa, and unawares became a living deity.
