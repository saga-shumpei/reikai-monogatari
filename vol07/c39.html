Chapter 39: The Camel Caravan [339]

第39章　駱駝隊〔339〕

§ 1

The four missionaries, who had come down the slope of Mount Kirishima toward the southwest, were descending at their own pace, led by Kumakō, a large man.

Kumakō said, "Excuse me, if we go a little further this way, there is a great desert. Unless we cross this desert, we cannot go to the capital of the land of Toyo. Fortunately, there are many camels in the forest yonder. Shall we cross the desert straddling their backs?"

Hinodegami said, "That would be interesting, and it is convenient. Could you bring seven or eight camels here?"

"Understood,"

and Kumakō ran off toward the herd of camels. The four missionaries sat down on the grass by the roadside and rested. After a while, Kumakō, accustomed to handling camels, returned leading several dozen of them.

Hinodegami said, "Well, there are so many of them."

Kumakō said, "Please mount whichever one you like. I will bring the others along as reserves. Since there is no water in the desert, when the water runs out, we will break open the round humps on their backs to draw out water and drink it as we go."

Omonagi said, "What a useful creature!"

in admiration. Hinodegami nimbly mounted a camel, and following him, the three missionaries and Kumakō likewise came to ride atop the camels.

Hinodegami said, "This is far less trouble than walking through a scorching, blistering desert. Truly, it is easy [rakuda]."

While chattering idle words in this manner, they lined up their camels and advanced through the barren desert where the spring wind was cool.

Just then, a whirlwind blew up, stirring up clouds of sand and dust, rendering the surroundings so dark and gloomy that one could not discern anything close at hand; in addition, as it was unfortunately a headwind, the party from time to time turned the camels' heads around, taking the wind upon their backs to rest. After a while, the violent whirlwind abruptly ceased, and the blistering spring sun began to shine fiercely. Kumakō stood at the front and began to sing:

"The sun has risen, the sun has risen in the desert sky, Hinode has appeared, Hinode has appeared riding upon a camel. This is the missionary of Hinode, The head is hot, the face is hot too, Bound by deep affection, In an intimate bond that lets no water slip between, Departing from the hot land of Hi, Missionaries of Toyohinobetsu, Advancing toward the capital of Toyo. Shine, shine bright, O Hinodegami! Blow, blow strong, O wind of Shinato! Rise, rise up, clouds of sand! As long as the camels' legs hold out, The camel may find it hard, but I am at ease; Rain, fall and fall, fall in abundance! Missionaries who have appeared out of nowhere, Descending from the heavenly stars, Across the sandy plains of the land of Toyo, How valiant is our advancing form! The capital of Toyo is just a little further."

"Though we say a little, it is still a hundred ri; What of a hundred ri or a thousand ri? Advance, advance, camel corps! Advance, advance, camel corps!"

Without minding their surroundings, they pressed forward, singing this improvised song. Staving off their hunger with the fruit loaded upon the backs of many camels, days passed, and at last they arrived at the capital of Toyo.

The giant man Kumakō, his spirits rising as the capital drew near, struck his camel's rump recklessly and rushed headlong toward the capital with the fierce vigor of a raging lion. Next, the four missionaries, unwilling to be outdone, whipped their camels' rumps and sped along like lightning. At the entrance to the capital of Toyo, a great crowd raised their voices in unison, shouting "Uro! Uro!" to celebrate Kumakō's return.

This Kumakō was the Great Chieftain of the land of Toyo. His real name was Yasokumabake. Yasokumabake possessed supernatural powers. Foreknowing that the missionaries of the God of the Rising Sun would cross over to this Tsukushi Island in Africa, land in the land of Kumaso, cross the land of Hi, and come down to the land of Toyo, he had led many camels to rescue them from the perils of this desert and had come to the foot of Mount Kirishima to welcome the party. He had thus intentionally called himself by the commoner's name of Kumakō. As soon as Yasokumabake arrived at the capital of Toyo, the people of the land danced wildly to celebrate his safe return. Surrounded by the cheers of his countrymen, Yasokumabake rode alongside the four missionaries to his own residence and calmly retired deep within.

The crowd left behind engaged in animated talk among themselves.

Person A: "Hey, what could our Great Chieftain Lord Yasokumabake have been thinking, dragging those people like bagworms, like beggars—one white, one red, one black—across that vast desert? Surely curiosity has its limits. If he brings such fellows here, the land of Toyo might be completely overrun, and in the very end, he himself might even get kicked out."

Person B: "If it were only Lord Yasokumabake, it would be fine, but who knows where we too might be driven out to? This is truly a troubling state of affairs."

Person C: "What do you fellows know? How could a man as strong, wise, and magnificent as Lord Yasokumabake make a blunder? 'How could a swallow or a sparrow know the ambitions of the giant Peng bird?' What does it matter even if swallows and sparrows chirp 'cheep, cheep'?"

Otsu said, "What do you mean by swallows and sparrows? If we are swallows and sparrows, you are dung beetles."

Hei said, "What do you mean by dung beetles? Even dung would be disgusted! You are the dung beetle, spawned from the dung of swallows and sparrows!"

Tei said, "You know nothing at all; listen respectfully to what I say. The other day, five-colored clouds rose in the northeastern sky; surely you saw them too."

Hei said, "Oh, I saw them, I saw them; what on earth could that be?"

Tei said, "Be silent and listen to what I say. In the heavens there are strange shifts of wind and clouds, and among humanity there is the suffering of disease. In any case, the world is changing. Therefore, the wise Lord Yasokumabetsu observed this, declared that a splendid deity had appeared in the direction of the northeast, and set out leading the camels. Look, that deity who has now followed behind the Grand Chieftain must surely be an incarnation of the five-colored clouds. If you utter such idle grumbles, divine punishment will strike you."

Kō said, "No wonder a beggar-like vagabond came falling from the heavens; I cannot make heads or tails of it at all. It is said that some god named Yaso-tenyake or Kumo-tenyaki or something of the sort is mixed in."

Tei said, "What an uncomprehending fellow; one cannot speak to you. You utter nothing but elusive nonsense."

Kō said, "When no one is even holding on, how can there be any speaking or letting go, you fool!"

The blockheads gathered together, hazarding their own irresponsible speculations. At the mansion of Yasokumabetsu, five-colored clouds once again rose, piercing the heavens. The crowd merely cried out, collapsed on the spot, and pressed their palms together in prayer.
