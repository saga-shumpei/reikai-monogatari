Chapter 18: The Maiden Battle [411]

第18章　初陣〔411〕

§ 1

Uzuyamahiko, along with the three persons Matsu, Take, and Ume, repeated nights of bedding upon grass and resting on stone pillows, and gradually arrived at the southern port of Hara in the land of Teru. The night boat was on the very verge of setting sail. The moon of the thirteenth night was shrouded in dark clouds covering the whole sky, hiding its light, and it was pitch darkness without a single glimmer of starlight. The name of this ship was the Ataru-maru; it had carried a great quantity of fruits and passengers from the port of Ataru to the port of Hara, and it was now time to return to its home country. From within the darkness, the boatman,

"The boat is leaving now! Those who are boarding, get on quickly—!"

bellowed at the top of his lungs, and putting a bamboo tube to his mouth, blew upon it to announce the departure with a loud blare. In the south wind blowing at that moment, the ship's sails caught the wind fully, and the vessel began to advance swiftly.

Uzuyamahiko's party was aboard in a corner of the ship. At this time, it was so dark inside the boat that one could hardly discern anyone's face. Setting his eyes upon the lighthouse as a landmark, the boatman,

"Setting sail from the land of Teru, / Returning home to nightless Peru; / Even the risk of capsizing upon the waves, / Until the port of Ataru where winds also blow,"

sang fluently in tune. Inside the boat, casual talk began here and there.

Kumakō said, "Hey, Torakō, when you were returning from the land of Tsukushi some time ago, didn't you rip off a massive amount of sake money from a passenger on the Mikasa-maru? You truly are a shrewd fellow when it comes to doing bad things."

Torakō replied, "We are among people here; are you going to scatter disgrace in public? Hey, Kumakō, what do you take me for? On this island of Takasago-jima, I am the famous Oni no Tora-sama whom nobody does not know!"

"Whether among people, inside the ship, in the dead of night, or inside the belly, what do I care? You are quite a villain, but if one goes to such an extreme as a villain, it is rather admirable."

"It was a sure thing. There were magnificent beauties beyond comparison to Benten-sama or Otohime-sama of the Dragon Palace, and three of them at that. Accompanying them was one beautiful, strong-looking retainer. Then that fellow Kamekō, boiling with vexation and abandoning himself to despair, began to sip his sake, and after talking about various matters, brought up the story that Momogamihiko no Mikoto of the land of Uzu was killed in the land of Paru. Thereupon, that retainer came forward saying, 'I would like to make a slight inquiry.' So I snatched Kamekō's tale away and said, 'Yes, what might it be?' That man appeared to be an underling retainer of Momogamihiko no Mikoto and said, 'I am one who is going to see him due to a certain matter.' Thinking, 'Here it is, I have got him,' and waiting in eager anticipation, just like catching a whale in a sardine net, he was caught on the tip of Lord Tora's tongue, and brought out magnificent, clinking coins the likes of which I had never seen before. Realizing, 'This guy has quite a lot; if I tell him everything at once it won't pay off, so let me ask for another round,' I spoke vaguely, and he said, 'What you say is hard to understand; please tell me a little more firmly and clearly.' So this Lord Tora shot back that even the judgment of hell depends on money, and that greenhorn from the capital, completely naive, smiled broadly and said, 'Ah, is that so? You want sake money, what a troublesome fellow,' and gave me another heavy, thudding load. Such an easy haul rarely ever happens. I wonder if someone would bring up such a story for me again."

The three maidens, Matsu, Take, and Ume, were at their side, listening attentively to the conversation between the bear and the tiger.

Uzuyamahiko said in a low voice:

"Princess Matsushiro, they are having a strange conversation. "Is that so? Would even such a person repent?"

"This is also your maiden battle as a missionary. If you cannot cause that man to repent, you cannot possibly serve as a missionary."

"Ah, then let me try it."

The Way of God, which teaches and guides that it purifies and cleanses the sins of the self wandering in darkness; the Ananai teachings, which instruct the way of connection unto God and Lord; boarded upon the ship of teachings, to proceed through the dark world of darkness unto the land of Teru, the land of daylight shining brightly, unto the port of Ataru where the morning sun shines—this indeed is joyful. Ah, Torakō, Torakō! I am an evangelist of the august teachings. The stolen money is not to be regretted; there is merely one thing that should be regretted: it is that your pure spirit, Torakō, granted by God, had its mind clouded by Magatsumi-no-Kami, and the soul that protects your body was stolen away. Without thinking that the stolen soul cannot be helped,

from this moment on, re-erect the true pillar of the spirit; set up the pillar of kotodama with the chigi rafters high above the lowest bedrock; take back the jewel of the heart which Amatsu-mikami bestowed! Take back the jewel of the heart! What is repeatedly sorrowful is the circumstance, the future of you, Torakō, whose soul was snatched away. Ah, Torakō, Torakō! As soon as even a single day, as soon as a single moment, return to the way of sincerity! The human body born of God is a being equal to God; it is a being equal to God! Manifesting as a divine spirit, reflect upon good and evil; look anew upon your crooked deeds up to now with a straight spirit, and re-proclaim them.

Separated spirit of Naohi-no-Kami, especially precious child of God! Praise and extol the grace of God; wash your soul before God! O this ship speeding through the darkness! Leaving behind the port of Teru, where the divine spirit shines across, and crossing the vast blue sea where the great blessing is ever deeper, just as it proceeds toward the port of Ataru, the divine land of day without night, so must you very swiftly, swiftly sweep away the dust of your heart; you must wash the jewel of your heart!'

The people aboard the ship listened intently to this voice, tilting their heads and listening in silence. Inside the dark ship, voices expressing admiration arose continuously upon hearing this song. Uncharacteristic of his former vigor, Torakō raised a loud voice, cried out in tears, and prostrated himself. With the lighthouse as its mark, the Ataru-maru proceeded steadily onward, breaking through the darkness and cutting through the rough waves.
