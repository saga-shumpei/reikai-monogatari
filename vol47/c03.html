Chapter 3: 寒迎 [1236]

寒迎〔1236〕

§ 1

Harukuniwake, accompanied by Tatsukō and Tāru, treads upon the dew of the desolate moor and advances directly toward the encampment of General Ranch, who is stationed in the village of Ukiki.

Tatsukō, in high spirits, leads the way, and while gazing at the scenery in all directions, he begins to chant in a new-style tone with faltering rhythm:

Entering not the mountain of the moon, Dawn breaks not yet in the heavens, Yet by the busy voices Of skylarks and myriad birds, Encouraged, rubbing sleep-laden eyes, Early now upon the wild moor I raise my footsteps ○ When I tread upon the grass leaves Bearing dew with my waraji, The morning wind blowing against my sleeves Fragrantly scents, and wiping away sweat, I wash my breast, The delight of the journey! The moon of Mount Sakateruyama is clear, And the sound of the Shō accompanying the pine wind Is heard coming to me ever more endearing.

Tāru says:

Tāru: 'Oi, Tatsukō-sama, there is no Shō, nor is the sound of a Shō or anything of the sort audible at all, is there? Eh, is it permissible for a poet to tell such lies?'

Tatsukō: 'That is precisely what makes a poet. For the character for poetry is written with the radical for speech and the character for temple. A temple is a place where dead men go. Is it not fine so long as the flavor is interesting even if the Shō is somewhat mistaken? In any case, it is not something made by living human beings; it is what half-poets and half-Shō players, or old men and three-penny scribblers of this present world speak of. I too merely tried to imitate a poet.'

Tāru: 'It is not flavor, it is taste, you mean.'

Tatsukō: 'Even if the true Shō is off by about that much, poetry talent is not particularly lacking, is it? Ahahaha.'

Tāru: 'Moshi Sensei, that moon yonder, is it likewise a poet? Is it not trembling and shivering in mid-air? If there were even the sun, the moon would be an unnecessary thing, would it not? Overwhelmed by the light of the sun, its light gradually weakens, and has it not come to look almost dead?'

Tatsukō: 'Un, does it look that way? Then, following the example of Tatsukō-sama, shall I, Harukuniwake, also compose and chant a poem? From the remote past of millions of years, A cold and dead-seeming, Silent moon Shines alone in the vast sky. That is To cast an auspicious light Upon all things of the earth, To let fall the dew of benevolent mercy Upon the common people, And to bestow the pure water of life, In order to manifest That warm figure of harmony with the dust and equality with the world. The moon waxes or wanes, Or sets, To display the divine mechanism of light and darkness In the worldly realm, And because it causes the divine work of benevolent mercy To be conducted eternally and infinitely. That it appears to human eyes As a cold moon Is in order to conceal its spiritual light Within the providence of encompassing warm affection. By the way, let us chant the sound of the wind blowing now. The sound of the wind Blowing softly, If we listen to the echo of footsteps And the voice of grass leaves, All things Possess a heart, And it is as though there is something Whispering a mystery To my ears, whose heart is dark.'

Harukuniwake is filled with the love, faith, and wisdom-enlightenment of God, and even as he advances without possessing weapons toward the camp of the formidable enemy, his valiant spirit is almost like that of a maidservant returning to her parent's home for the Spring and Autumn Yabuiri, journeying on while chanting poems along the way. Both Tatsukō and Tāru, before they know it, are influenced by Harukuniwake's unhurried and composed attitude, and completely find themselves in the mood of a heavenly journey. Tāru says:

Tāru: 'Moshi, Sensei-sama, where do universal love and differential love differ? Do we advance from differential love to universal love, or do we separate from universal love into differential love? I have heard of differentially-unified love and uniformly-differentiated love; from which should we find the starting point?'

Harukuniwake: 'Differential love is like a narrow romantic love. Universal love is a universal love. It is so-called divine love. Let me try composing another poor verse.'

So saying, Harukuniwake chants:

From inborn differential love, The path to advance To divine universal love Must truly be walked Upon a path of harrowing blood and tears. Herein is concealed the gracious message Of the cessation of earthly desires and the attainment of Nirvana. By the way, let us compose another poor verse concerning faith and the bliss of joyful trust. When by faith the stubborn wall of our faithless selves experiences body-and-mind shedding and collapses away, by the bellows of the spiritual wind blowing from the treasure seat of God, it performs the joy of emancipation and newborn creation

§ 2

Even the Maukuwa cannot burn it, nor can the Harau submerge it; the Kongaufue Hotsushin, which is Hongu and Gonjiyō, I have come to realize, is inherent within me. At that very moment, even if a thousand Kajitsu were to rise and shine with Shiyakuyaku intensity all at once, they would be nothing more than the seven treasures (Shitsupau) adorning the Jishiyōhofukai, and the Manihotsushiyu reflecting the Shiyōjiyōmeushin.

The Shinraku of Hofuetsu that we possess is not some Kūsauteki thing that would suddenly meet the Hiun of Genmetsu due to the saws of Gendai cold Tetsugaku or the axes of clever Kuwagaku. It is a great rock-like heart standing upon Chie and Shiyōkaku, through the love of goodness and the truth of faith held by the Lord God.

Taaru: 'Through your poem just now, I too have tasted the Shinraku of Hofuetsu. It seems the sun of today has finally risen, and the summit of Mount Sakateru has become very bright and radiant. Let me compose a poem as well.'

Taaru: 'With a heart filled with burning hope, I have worshipped the rising morning sun. The peaks of the distant mountains are pure white, and even now, they are glowing with clouds as the sun prepares to rise. How magnificent is the rising sun before me, as I think of living more strongly.'

Ryūkō: 'The fields where the wind sweeps through the desolate mountains are lost to sight, darkened by the snow.'

Taaru: 'Haha! Hey Ryūkō, don't be sleepy-headed. What is this about "darkened by the snow"? Why do you not say "dawned by the snow"?'

Ryūkō: 'This is stock from last night. If I keep it in storage for too long, it will become stale, so I must sell the old, rough products first, and then bring out the new ones. It would not be economical to let a fine verse rot in my belly. Now, here is a fresh start: The mountains brighten, and the wind blows softly along the field path; the surroundings are pure, and my heart is calm. I declare it anew. Ahem.'

Taaru: 'What a splendid poem that is.'

Ryūkō: 'There is more; I shall throw out the best one now. Ahem. Bathed solemnly in the morning sun, the high peaks of Mount Sakateru tower above the clouds. How is that?'

Taaru: 'Are you trying to live solemnly? How noble, the brocade-covered mountain towers into the sky.'

Ryūkō: 'Nay, both are excellent. If I stay in the company of such a fine poet, even Chikokuwake-sama will have no face left. Come, let us be on our way.'

As they said this, the three of them walked along the path through the withered grass, treading upon the morning dew. From ahead, General Ranchi led a cavalry troop of several dozen men, and the force with which they came rushing toward them seemed as if it would kick away the very mountains. Standing at the head was Aaku, who had been saved by Chikokuwake-sama and fled earlier.

Aaku jumped off his horse, approached before Chikokuwake-sama, and bowing politely, said: 'I am deeply grateful for the great trouble you took for me earlier. Following that, I returned immediately to the main camp and reported about you to the General. The General was also very pleased and said that he must come out to welcome you, so he has marched out here now. Please, ride my horse and come to the main camp.'

Chikokuwake: 'Oh, that was a lot of trouble. And is General Ranchi-dono here?'

Aaku: 'Yes, the one wearing that golden, brilliant military cap is the General.'

Chikokuwake: 'My, what a splendid outfit. In that case, I must offer my greetings.'

Just as he said this, General Ranchi jumped nimbly off his horse and appeared before Chikokuwake-sama, rubbing his hands together, and said: 'I am General Ranchi, who serves the Kami-tsukasa of Ōkuninushi-no-Kami. On this occasion, by the order of my lord, while we were on our way to attack the mansion of Iso, our vanguard, General Katahiko, was thoroughly defeated by your Kototama and the like, and it seems he fled in a fragile state. Since it is impossible for those of us who are powerless and without virtue to oppose the propaganda envoys of the San-kyō, who possess matchless divine power, we set up camp in the forest of Ukiki and consulted with the staff officers. Since it has been decided by the council that there is no choice but to lead the entire army and surrender at your feet, we shall no longer take any hostile action against you. It would be a happiness beyond our expectations if you would kindly come to our camp and let us hear your noble words.'

§ 3

He stated this with an air of sincerity. Although Harukuniwake did not believe every word of Ranch, he determined in his heart that this was the perfect opportunity to lead him onto the Seidau, and with an expression of feigned ignorance, he said:

Harukuniwake: 'If that is the case, I shall follow your instructions and accompany you to your army's Jinei.'

General Ranch had Harukuniwake mount the Meiba he had ridden in on, while he himself mounted a spare horse, and they returned toward the Jinei in high spirits.

Stopping before the gate, General Ranch looked back at Harukuniwake and said:

General Ranch: 'It is a makeshift Jinei with nothing worth seeing, and it is most Futsugafu to receive a guest from afar in such a place, but I beg you to rest at your leisure.'

He offered this greeting with great Ingin. Harukuniwake replied with a slight Tafurei:

Harukuniwake: 'Yes, thank you.'

He then proceeded deeper and deeper into the camp.

Many Gunsotsu were whiling away their boredom by building a Doheu and engaging in Shirōtozumafu. Ryūkō and Tar, both of them, were so captivated by the sumo that they did not notice Harukuniwake advancing deep into the camp, and while criticizing the techniques of the forty-eight moves such as throwing, waist-throwing, pushing out, and the Kawazu, they said:

Ryūkō and Tar: 'Aha-ha-ha-ha!'

They laughed, and finally began to clap their hands and cheer them on. The strongest man among them, Ekisu, was stomping his feet and standing in the center of the Doheu like a Nio, saying:

Ekisu: 'Come on, whoever you are, bring it on! I am ready for you.'

He threw down every man who came at him, one after another; such was his skill. Ryūkō, feeling indignant at Ekisu's attitude and the spinelessness of the weak warriors, found his hands moving to his waist before he knew it, untied his belt with a slide, and jumped into the center of the Doheu completely naked. He then began to stomp his feet with a thudding sound. Seeing this, Ekisu seemed to be offended and said:

Ekisu: 'Hey, aren't you Ryūkō? Where have you been hiding since the other day? This is no place for a weakling like you to appear. Do not harbor the ambition to wrestle with us. Do you intend to face me, this Ekisu-sama, who could be called the reincarnation of Nomi no Sukune? Hey, stop it, stop it, you will only bring shame upon yourself.'

Ryūkō: 'Hmph, do not make a fool of me. Even I, in my younger days, reached the Makuuchi division. With my silk loincloth, my Barents-style sumo topknot, I have sat on a large camp stool even in front of Daikokushu-sama, stood to drink water, wiped my nose, and once I stepped into the sixteen-straw-bale Doheu, I was Ryūkō, the lion-hearted, who never touched the earth. I have never lain down on the Doheu; I am called Ryūkō because I always remain standing. My other name is Shōkō. Come, let us have a bout.'

Ekisu: 'Hey, you life-ignoring fool, I shall plant you in the earth. Do not come crying to me later.'

Ryūkō: 'That is what I should be saying to you. Drink your final water and brace yourself.'

While saying this, he scattered the salt tied to the four pillars to the left and right, and without even drinking water, he began to stomp his feet. Ekisu, not wanting to be outdone, grabbed a handful of salt and threw it at Ryūkō, and without drinking water, he stomped the ground until it shook, and they locked together in a four-way grip. For about half an hour, the battle between the dragon and the tiger continued, with no end in sight. Tar became fully absorbed, grabbed a war fan, jumped into the Doheu pretending to be a referee, and said:

Tar: 'Hatsukeyoi, hatsukeyoi, nokotta, nokotta, there is no retreat, hatsukeyoiya!'

He circled the Doheu to the right and left. The crowd held their breath, watching to see how the match would turn out. Even the formidable Ekisu, exhausted, let out a gasp, and sensing the timing of his breath, Ryūkō pulled his right hand away and struck the three-way knot of Ekisu's loincloth, causing him to spin around the Doheu three or four times before he tumbled into the western area like a collapsing avalanche. Ekisu, having greatly lost face, disappeared deep into the Jinei while still completely naked. The cries of praise and the sound of applause were enough to shake the surroundings.

『さア誰なつと来い、消しかかりだ』

といきりきつて居る。来る奴来る奴片つ端から投げつける、其手際のよさ。竜公はエキスの態度と弱武者の腑甲斐なさに憤慨し、何時の間にか両の手が腰へまはり、帯をスルスルと解いて了ひ、真裸となつて土俵の真中へ飛び出した。さうしてドンドンと四股を踏み鳴らしてゐる。エキスは之を見て癪に触つたと見え、

『おい、貴公は竜公ぢやないか。此間から何処へ逃げて居つたのだ。そんな弱虫の出る所ぢやない。俺達と相撲をとるなんぞと云ふ野心を起すものぢやないぞ。野見の宿弥の再来とも云ふべき此エキスさまに相手にならうと思ふのか。エー、措け措け、恥をかく様なものだから』

『ヘン、馬鹿にすない。俺でも若い時や幕の内まで入つたものだ。襦子の締込み、バレンツの相撲束ねの櫓鬢、大黒主の前でも大胡床をかき、立つて水のみ、手鼻汁をかむ、十と六俵の土俵に出たら、獅子奮迅、土つかずの竜公さまだ。いつも土俵の上で横になつた事はない、いつも立ちつづけだから竜公さまだ。又の名を勝公さまだ。さあ一つ揉んでやらう』

『エー、生命知らず奴、土の中へ植ゑてやらう。吠え面かわくな』

『そりや俺の云ふ事だ。末期の水でも飲んでしつかりせい』

と云ひ乍ら四本柱に括りつけた塩をポツポツと左右に打振り、水をも飲まずに四股を踏み出した。エキスも負けぬ気になり塩を一掴みグツと握つて竜公にぶちかけ、水をも飲まずドンドンと地響きさせながらペタペタと四つに組んで了つた。半時ばかり竜虎の争ひ、いつ勝負の果つべしとも見えない。タールは一生懸命になり、軍扇を握り土俵に行司気取りに飛び出し、

『はつけよい　はつけよい　のこつた　のこつた、後がないぞ、はつけよいや』

と土俵の周囲を右左に廻つてゐる。大勢は固唾を呑んで此勝負如何にと見つめて居る。流石のエキスも力尽きハツと吐く息の気合を窺ひ、ポンと右の手をぬいて褌の三辻を竜公がたたくとコロコロコロと土俵の中を三つ四つ廻つて西の溜へドスンと雪崩が落ちた様に転げ込んで了つた。エキスは大に面目を失し、真裸の儘スタスタと陣中奥深く姿を隠した。ワーイワーイと称讃の声、拍手の音、四辺も揺ぐばかりであつた。
