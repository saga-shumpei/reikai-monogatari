Chapter 4: 茶湯の艶 [1728]

茶湯の艶〔1728〕

§ 1

On the outskirts of the city of Tarahan, at the back, flows a fairly wide ditch of slightly murky water. Amidst a dense and not very large compound of evergreen trees stands a semblance of a thatched hut belonging to Tarchin, a master of the tea ceremony. Though the house is old and narrow, it is fairly spacious for the bachelor life of the master alone. Moreover, standing apart from the main house, no annoying sounds are heard, and when one regards the lush garden shrubberies as one's own, it far surpasses the villas with their painful outward displays maintained by gentlemen and wealthy merchants who decorate themselves for the sake of societal appearances, being carefree and comfortable to live in. The forest facing them enveloped in spring rain, the country road looked out upon on a hazy moonlit night, the swaying of young leaves close to the eaves, the croaking of frogs in the small paddy fields audible from the windows—it is a homestead that, at a glance, looks as if a master of the tea ceremony might dwell there.

The fluttering of a sparrow chick's wings is preparation for soon soaring across the skies. The playful tumbling of a kitten is practice for catching its favorite mice. A maiden of marriageable age facing the mirror to tidy her face and hair is preparation for perfectly fulfilling the supremacy of romance.

Tarchin, the master of the tea ceremony, from early in the morning, with a shaved head and a twisted headband tied around it, and his lower garment rolled up high, sweeps the tatami rooms, cleans the gate, looking as though some rare guest is about to appear. And with an expression as if he had unexpectedly found a treasure worth ten thousand gold in some secret place, he smiles broadly. They say every fool has his one trick, and this tea-booth priest also seems quite well-versed at least in the way of the tea ceremony. In the direction of the main house lives a single Hotei-like woman who is the master's wife, of a young age and leaning excessively toward physical robustness. Her strength in effortlessly carrying a five-peck rice bale shows not a single trace of anything womanly anywhere. With a dark complexion and tea-brown hair, she is a large woman, a creature utterly unlikely to become material for a novelist who values hysteria. If viewed from the fashionable wit's perspective of the present day, she has no meaning whatsoever in any circumstance, scarcely any necessity for existence, and is merely a single, pitiful in the extreme, physical object. Bookworm literary men, rice-devouring politicians, gramophone educators, and rice-pounding-insect minor officials come visiting the tea-booth priest's thatched hut from time to time, as if to make a fuss about boasting of the tea ceremony's procedure and wishing it to be an aid to their social intercourse, making him a master who is not very popular. His worldly fortune is emaciated, even the walls expose their bones, the eaves are slanted, and it is a prized dwelling from whose upper privy roof one can view the moon. At the time of a squall, he bustles about carrying buckets, washbasins, and hand-pails into the middle of the rooms, making the sound of an untimely rain drum.

Lately, rare guests quite unsuited to the house and its master sometimes poke their faces in and out of the windows of this tea room. Glossy hair color, a beauty who seemed to have slipped out of a celestial being's painting drawn by a master, came from nowhere, innocent yet neither looking like a country-bred woman nor a wild mountain-monkey's daughter, with graceful deportment and manners, so much so that from the tip of her head to the tips of her fingers, one felt that a single movement would cause the surrounding air to undulate away to ten thousand miles distant, capturing the souls of sentient men. The master Tarchin began a pompous lecture concerning his proud Way of Tea toward the young beauty. The beautiful maiden was, needless to say, none other than Subaruhime, who had been captured alive from the deep mountains of Arina, and who could be described as the metamorphosis of a mountain spirit and water deity.

Tarchin: 'Hinomiya-sama, since the most important thing for a woman to learn is the tea ceremony, today, by the gracious and venerable command of Taishi-sama, I, humble as I am, shall respectfully impart the procedure of the tea ceremony to you. First, since you must understand the subjects of the tea ceremony, I shall speak of the general outline.'

Subaruhime: 'Hai, thank you very much for taking care of me in every possible way. No matter what, having been taken away to the deep mountains since I was a child of about ten years old, and being an innocent maiden who knows nothing of the world as if she had not even been touched by the winds of this floating world, I beg for your guidance in all things, not limited to the tea ceremony.'

原文（近代文語）

タラハン市の町外れ、裏は薄濁つた可なり広い溝が流れてゐる。常磐木のこんもりとした余り広からぬ屋敷の中に茶湯の宗匠タルチンの形ばかりの茅屋が建つてゐる。家は古く狭けれども宗匠一人が独身生活には可なり広い。しかも母屋と離れて煩き物音も聞えず、生い茂れる庭の植込みを吾物と見れば、世間体を飾つた紳士紳商の苦しい外観を飾る別荘よりも遥に勝り、呑気で住心地もよい。春雨に包まるる向日の森、朧月夜に見渡す田圃道、軒端に近い若葉の揺るぎ、窓に聞ゆる小田の蛙の泣き声、見るからに茶人の住みさうな家構へである。

雀の子が羽ばたきをするのは、やがて天空をかける準備だ。猫の子がじやれるのは大好物の鼠をとらむとする下稽古だ。年頃の女が鏡に向つて、顔面や頭髪の整理をするのは恋愛至上主義を完全に達せむ為の準備である。

茶の湯の宗匠タルチンは朝早うから、坊主頭に捻鉢巻、腰衣を高くまき上げ座敷を掃いたり、門を掃除したり、何か珍客の出て来る様子。さうして何となく万金の宝を人知れぬ処で拾つたやうな顔付してニコニコと笑つて居る。愚者の一芸とか云つて、此茶坊主も茶の湯の道丈けは可なり覚つて居るやうである。母屋の方には宗匠の女房として年の若い体格美に傾き過ぎた布袋女が一人住まつてゐる。五斗俵を軽々と持ち運ぶその力、どこに一点の女らしい処も見えない。顔色黒く頭髪は茶褐色の大女、到底ヒステリ性を尊ぶ小説家の材料になりさうもない奴。もし当世流の才子風より見れば一切の境遇に何等の意味もなく殆ど生存の要もなく、只一個の哀れ至極なる肉体物に過ぎないのだ。鞋虫の文学者や、穀潰しの政治家や、蓄音器の教育家や、米搗螽斯の小役人共が、仔細らしく茶の湯の手前を誇り、交際場裡の補助にもがなと、茶坊主の茅屋を折々訪ねて来るのみで、余り流行らない宗匠である。身代は痩せて壁迄が骨を出し軒は傾き、上雪隠の屋根から月を見る重宝な住居である。夕立の時にはバケツや、盥、手桶等を慌てまはして座敷中に持ち運び、時ならぬ雨太鼓の音をさせてゐる。

此頃此茶室に家と主人に不相当な珍客が、チヨコチヨコ窓の内外から顔を出す事がある。艶々した髪の色、名人の描いた天人の絵から抜け出したやうな美人が、何処とはなしに初心々々しいけれども、さりとて田舎出の女とも見えず、山猿の娘とも見えず、起居振舞しとやかに、頭の先から指の先まで、一寸動けば四辺の空気は千万里の彼方迄波動するかと思はるる位、有情男子の肝魂を奪つた。宗匠のタルチンは妙齢の美人に向つて得意の茶道に就いて鹿爪らしき講義を初め出した。美しき乙女は云ふ迄もなくアリナが山奥から生捕つて来た、山霊水伯の精の変化と云ふべき、スバール姫たる事は云ふ迄もない。

タル『姫様、女の最も習つておかねばならない事は茶の湯で厶いますから、今日は太子様の有難き尊き御命令によりまして、卑しき私が茶の湯の御手前を恐れ乍ら伝授さして頂きませう。先ず茶の湯の講目から心得て居つて貰はなくてはなりませぬから、あらましの事を申上げます』

ス『ハイ、何から何までお世話になりまして有難う厶います。何と云つても十年許りも子供の時から山奥に連れ行かれ、此世の風にも当つてゐないやうな、おぼこ娘の世間知らずで厶いますから、茶の湯に限らず何事も御指導をお願ひ申します』

Taruchin floated a smile across his entire face, twitched his low nose, rolled his Sanpaujiro eyes, went 'Humph' and stroked his nose from left to right with the back of his right hand, rubbed it restlessly against his own buttocks, and even made his kotoba look solemn as he began,

Taruchin: 'Now, the Way of tea is regarded as having three main principles. And what is called the central practice of the tea ceremony means illuminating bright virtue, which is the path of leading one's nature based on heavenly decree. Well then, if you exhaust the great origin of the tea ceremony, any and all temahe will have the thirteen temahe as their father and mother. From this, three hundred and eighty temahe also branch out. "All things have a root and a branch, and affairs have a beginning and an end; when one knows what comes first and what comes last, one is close to the Way. Is it not taught by the seijin that those whose root is in disorder yet whose branches are brought to order do not exist?" Therefore, the tea ceremony takes the thirteen temahe as its root, and all manner of temahe lie within this. And furthermore, when your keiko advances to the point called the permitted temahe, your kotowaza becomes broad and branches out in various ways. This is the realm of the new people, and there are changes in various things. Temahe means selecting that mind. From the ancient Higashiyamadono down to Rikiu and the gendai, its mei is ever renewed; what I teach is the true daisu of seven tiers, which is the utmost in authorization. Because illuminating virtue is made the root and illuminating the people is made the branch, what is called the tea ceremony takes the central practice as the root and the temahe as the branch. Making the true daisu the root is regarded as supreme goodness. Therefore, the seven tiers are those whose scope is great. Hereupon, when one exhausts their refinement and all know the place where they stop, there is not the slightest doubt. Hence, what I teach is called the true daisu of the tea ceremony. First of all, in the tea room there are such things as this four-and-a-half mat space and the junsulate. And the jun circular style refers to moving to the left from the seated mat as jun, and further, in a four-and-a-half mat room there are practices for jun and gyaku hands. Taking the jun circular style, cutting the hearth in the middle half-mat, hanging a free-hanging chain, or placing a kettle upon an iron ring—this is called jun-gote. Now I shall compose a song, so please write it down and keep it.'

Su: 'Yes, thank you very much for bestowing upon us various lofty teachings. Being a young girl unaccustomed to the ways of the world, no doubt the shishō-sama finds it tedious as well.'

So saying, she took out an inkstone, brush, ink, rolled paper, and the like from the paper box, and began to record the poem chanted by Taruchin.

Taruchin: '1. If upon entering the gate there is a guest room to the right, Know beforehand that this is the jun-gote. 1. The host being present and moving to the left is called jun, This is precisely the spirit of the character for righteousness (masu). 1. House-building is good when deliberated upon beforehand, That which lacks proper completion is an evil thing. 1. The less visible the inside of the hearth, the more troublesome it is, When arranging charcoal, one desires a lamp. 1. Even a withered tree wraps wisteria around itself like incense, Truly one knows this is the skillful hand of the host. 1. Fields, mountains, flowers, and fragrance all while beholding them, Those who know the living spirit are they who know. 1. The dust of the tea master who sweeps away dust from time to time, Ah, what of the dust within his heart? 1. That which has a guest room to the left upon entering the gate, It is best to know it as gyaku-gote. 1. The host being present and moving to the right is called gyaku, This is precisely the spirit of the character for following (jū).'

Taruchin: 'Now, now, by means of this poem you will roughly understand the arrangement of the hearth and the appearance of the interior. If I teach too much at once, it will not do if you forget, so I shall teach just a bit more and rest with this. Tomorrow onwards I will show you the actual temahe. Now I shall state the practices of the seven tiers of tea ceremony curriculum.

First tier: Daibon, kobon, Karatsu ware, tea caddy stand, tenmoku.

Second tier: Daibon, large tea caddy, placement of incense container, tray presentation.

Third tier: Daibon bag, tenmoku tea whisk holder.

Fourth tier: Daibon umi nagao, thin tea stand, three sets of tenmoku.

Fifth tier: Daibon stand, tenmoku tea bowl, double-eye presentation.'

Sudarman: 'Yes, thank you, I kindly ask for your guidance.'

At such a place, wrapping his face and muffling his footsteps, entering with the demeanor of a professional housebreaker peering into someone's residence and glancing around cautiously in all directions, was none other than Prince Sudarman.

Upon catching sight of the Prince's figure, Taruchin was both startled and overjoyed; like a rice-pounding grasshopper, time and again she pounded the tatami with her bald head like a pestle, retreated smoothly toward the entrance with five or six steps, slipped her feet into garden clogs worn down like setta sandals, and affecting a swagger toward the main house with a brown cap pulled deep over her brows and her head slightly lowered, she wiggled her buttocks as she threaded her way through the grove of trees in the garden and departed.

Tigers, lions, bears, and wolves roaring in the wild mountains, the myriad birds chirping in the forests, and even down to the insect tribes and earthworms—all living things between heaven and earth without exception sing of love and are immersed in passion. How much more so for the young Prince of Tarahan Castle, a fine youth burning with the blood of youth, who, when standing before a natural beauty who shames even the flowers, could not stem the tumultuous beating of his heart. Princess Subaru, clad in the robes of love with the same thoughts, dyed her cheeks a crimson red, wrapped her enchanting face in one sleeve, and for a while sustained a curtain of silence. Being novices at love, both the Prince and the maiden faltered and stammered over the things they wished to say to each other, and feeling somewhat overwhelmed and defeated by the uncanny nature of love,

"I wanted to meet you, I wanted to see you, you adorable thing,"

they had become so timid that even opening the conversation with a single word was beyond them. Nevertheless, having been knocked about together with numerous petty thieves even though it was deep in the mountains, Princess Subaru's mind was relatively open and she had grown somewhat bold. Resolving to take the plunge, Princess Subaru leapt onto the Prince's shoulders, embraced him so tightly it felt as though her arms might be torn off, and pressed their hot cheeks firmly together. Their four eyes were moistened with tears of joy that their love had been fulfilled.

Princess Subaru resolutely expressed her feelings in a thirty-one-syllable waka poem:

From that very day when my lord graced us with your visit, Oh, the agony of waiting for this auspicious day. How joyous to be welcomed by Lord Arina, And the joy of meeting the Prince. May our path of love continue on forever and ever, Even as I have prayed since the days gone by.

Taru: 'The figure of the maiden I first beheld upon Mount Asakura Is truly my very life. My robes of love for you, pondering you, drenched and soaked, Wept tears without a moment to dry. Protected by the grace of the gods of heaven and earth, How joyously I meet you today, even now. Let others plot against my person however they may, Once I am laid to sleep, what is there to fear?'

Suba: 'Most precious are the words of my beloved lord, They are the very life of this humble maiden. I pray that they remain unchanged for eternity, This beautiful bond between you and my own body.'
