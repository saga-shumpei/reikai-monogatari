Chapter 2: 懐 Valley [1153]

懐谷〔1153〕

§ 1

The rocks of Kajikatauge and the trees alike, as if to say they would scatter to the winds, were buffeted by the Ōyamaarashi; unable to advance, the party of Tamakuniwake firmly grasped the roots of the trees by the roadside, and prostrating themselves, waited for the wind to pass. One hour, two hours, and then three; though they waited, the Baufū did not cease, but roared and blew like the howling of wolves and tigers. The sun tilted toward Seizan, and in the pitch darkness where even Ayame could not be distinguished, the four-person party, having no other means, had no choice but to pass the night.

When the time past Ushimitsu arrived, the Shinadobe Kami, who had been so fierce, finally blew the Ratsuba of Kiusen and calmed down. The party of Tamakuniwake finally felt their hearts ease, and having composed their minds, they fell into a peaceful sleep until Yoa.

The party of Tamakuniwake, feeling their hearts ease as the Retsupū finally subsided, looked at the Atari and saw that the curtain of Shinkoku darkness was wrapping closely around their Ganzen. Having no choice, they steeled their Dokiyō, spread their straw raincoats on the Michibata, and decided to wait for the dawn. When they suddenly awoke, the surroundings were faintly brightening. Michikō, while rubbing his eyes, said:

Michikō: 'Sendenshi-sama, it seems the night has broken. Although yesterday was the very day of our departure, the arrival of the Baufū took us quite by surprise, did it not? I wish such an evil day would not happen again. Shall we all offer our prayers sufficiently when the new Hiten-sama appears today?'

Tamakuniwake: 'It seems it will be windy again today. Because you were chattering about such useless things, we encountered the admonition of the Ōkami. From now on, each of you must restrain your mouth. Since this is a land where the Kototama flourishes, the darkening of heaven and earth, the blowing of the wind, and the occurrence of great floods are all because the Kototama of human beings is evil. We possess the great mission to use the Kototama to pacify all Magatsukami, so you must not say anything that brings bad luck, even in jest.'

Michikō: 'That is true. That fellow Itakō, because he was blowing his trumpet with a prideful air, singing his nonsensical, derailment-filled propaganda songs against the gale, he received divine punishment; at the blood sacrifice of our departure, he scraped his shin and shed red blood. It would have been fine if it were a pair or a trio, but since it is a party of four, there is nothing very auspicious about it.'

Tamakuniwake: 'Hey, hey, Michikō, is it not your own Kototama that is evil? Do not say such useless things as "four people"; why do you not say "Zehi"?'

Michikō: 'If I say "four people," it sounds like there is an extra person, which is even more inauspicious, is it not? Even I am by no means an extra human being. I am, after all, a full-fledged, hundred-kan handsome man in the raw.'

Tamakuniwake: 'Look at that; a suspicious black cloud has appeared in the Ushitora sky. That is a Kazedama. If that thing comes blowing in, it will be far worse than last night's Retsupū. Let us quickly continue our march, arrive at the Futokoro-dani of Kajikatauge, and take refuge for a while. Come, let us go.'

So saying, he stood up straight, hurriedly put on his straw raincoat, grasped his hat firmly in his hand, and while leaning on his staff, he descended the steep slope, taking out bread from his bosom and eating it as he went. At last, they arrived at a south-facing, lush, and sunny valley called Futokoro-dani, located halfway down the slope of Kajikatauge. This spot was about one chō above the mountain path. The party of Tamakuniwake finally arrived here, spread their straw raincoats, and decided to wait for the wind to pass.

The many long-tailed monkeys that inhabit Kajikatauge had foreseen the arrival of the Baufū and, having gathered in numbers beyond counting, had all made this Futokoro-dani their ultimate refuge. Seeing the figure of the party of Tamakuniwake, they seemed surprised; they approached about four or five ken, surrounded them in an atlas-like formation, and were crying out, peeling their eyes, and gnashing their fangs.
