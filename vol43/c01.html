Chapter 1: 烈 Wind [1152]

烈風〔1152〕

§ 1

Clearing away the clouds and mists that obstruct the Tenchi, to save the world, the Kamubashira of Ananai-kyō, the Lord Kamususanōnōokami, whose Mikoto he reveres, Otohiko changed his name to Tamakuniwake and, leading three Shinto, Michikō, Itakō, and Sumikō, departed from the Iso mansion.

In the autumn sky where the cold wind rages, climbing up and down the steep slopes of Kajikatauge, he proceeds toward his goal, the moon-land of Indo.

To turn the Yamataorochi, the Shikogitsune, the Magaoni, and the evil Magatama that dwell in the capital of Haruna toward the path of sincerity, to construct a Shinkoku of supreme benevolence and supreme love upon this land, and to illuminate the divine majesty of the Lord.

Advancing with courage, he looks back at the Iso mansion and departs.

Ah, Yorokamigami, may your divine spirits be blessed.

Protect the journey of the propagandist Tamakuniwake and his three followers completely and in every detail.

May you allow us to perform our thousand-fold and ten-thousand-fold activities, without omission or failure, and in a flourishing manner, Yorokamigami.

Before the presence of the Lord, Mizutsuki reverently and humbly offers this prayer.

The propagandist Tamakuniwake, together with his three companions, following the party of Yoshikohime and Terukuniwake, fell into the valley of Kotoeriwake-no-mikoto and explored the First Heaven; whipping their legs like horses on the steep slopes of Kajikatauge, avoiding the dangers of stone wheels, and singing songs of propaganda with clear voices, they crossed over the mountain ridges.

The gale blowing at that moment roared like hundreds of lions howling at once, and it blew without restraint, as if to say that rocks should fly and trees should be uprooted from their very foundations.

Tamakuniwake encouraged his courage, saying, 'How could I be daunted by such a gale? What is a storm? Why should I fear thunder and heavy rain?' The bravery and heroism with which he climbed the steep slope while defying the headwind seemed enough to startle even the gods.

At last, they climbed up to a winding, narrow path that was somewhat level on the mountain top.

Michikō: 'Tamakuniwake-sama, right in the middle of climbing this chest-striking slope that stands like a board, that rascal of a wind god is taking advantage of our weakness, exerting all his might to blow us up into the mid-air, is he not?'

Tamakuniwake: 'Hahaha, how could I utter such weak words now? You must steel your nerves a little more.'

Michikō: 'It is not that I am uttering weak words at all. Because that rascal of a wind god is blowing so recklessly, I just blew a little myself. Has he not blown away the leaves of the trees so mercilessly, and wiped the place as clean as if a servant had wiped the dust from the edge of the veranda with a rag?'

Itakō: 'Hey Michikō, instead of blowing weak words, why don't you blow a brag?'

Michikō: 'Eh, Itakō, your nose is flapping like a blacksmith's bellows, and you are even blowing bubbles with your huffing and puffing, are you not? You are in such a weakened state, with your breath failing, your breathing labored, and your body temperature at forty-three degrees. Is this a time to be saying such trivial things? You should sweep the dust from your own doorstep first.'

Itakō: 'This is indeed a painful greeting; I, Itakō, have also taken my medicine.'

Michikō: 'What do you mean by medicine? You are spouting things as if you had caught the influenza and taken quinine, are you not?'

Tamakuniwake: 'Hey, hey, you two, is it appropriate to start an argument at the beginning of a journey to conquer demons, which is already an ill omen? Will you not be silent for a moment?'

Michikō: 'Yes, even the record-breaking gale has fallen silent, so when the time comes, the power supply to our vocal organs will naturally be cut off. Since the voice has already come out, if I stop it halfway, I will catch a disease again, hahaha.'

Tamakuniwake: 'Look at that Sumikō. He does not clatter like a rattle or a bell as you do, but keeps silence all the time, does he not? A man should not chatter about trivial things so glibly, nor should he show his white teeth to others so easily. There is nothing that looks more refined than a person who remains silent... How like a frog that opens its mouth to show its entrails... you would do well not to forget that.'

§ 4

Yotahiko: 'I have become lonely at heart. Had I known it would come to this, I would not have accompanied you. Hey, hey, Michikō, Sumikō! Have you become mutes? It is too much that you make only me speak and offer no reply. Oh, wait, wait! This is wrong. The Sendenshi of Tamakuniwake, the Kankōrei that was imposed, I had completely forgotten about it. O Kamunahohi of the expansive heart, look upon me with Ōnahohi, and please, I pray you, forgive me. Ah, Kamunagara, Kamunagara. A prayer to the gods when all else fails; surely you will listen to me a little. This is another steep slope, is it not? Ouch, ouch, ouch, I have fallen again.'

Saying this, he advanced into the mountain bosom of the great bend at Kajikatauge. A Kiyōfū, as if to tear the Tenchi asunder, blew up again with Mauzen force; even the mighty Tamakuniwake could no longer advance a single Itsupo, so he clung to the roots of a tree and, while reciting the Kamigoto, decided to wait for the wind to pass. The Sannin also followed the example of Tamakuniwake, clinging to the roots of trees, trembling and trembling, closing their eyes, and waiting for the wind to pass.

と言ひながら、河鹿峠の大曲りの山の懐に進んだ。天地もわるる許りの強風、又もや猛然として吹起り、流石の玉国別も最早一歩も進む能はず、木の根にしがみつき、神言を奏上し乍ら、風の渡り行くを待つ事とした。三人も玉国別に傚つて、木の根にしがみつき、ふるひふるひ目をつぶつて、風の過ぐるのを待つてゐる。
