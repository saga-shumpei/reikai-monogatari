Chapter 7: Forest 議 [1158]

森議〔1158〕

§ 1

Leaving the mansion of Iso, / ascending to subjugate the demons, / the party of Tamakuniwake / encountered them at Kajikatauge.

Seeking to escape / the record-breaking fierce winds, / they concealed their bodies / in the bosom of a valley.

Though their two eyes / had been gouged out by mountain monkeys / and for a while they brought down / the curtain of tragedy,

at last, by the aid of the Kami, / only a single eye was completely restored, / and they bravely advanced, descending / to the south of the pass of Kajikayama.

The sun sank into Seizan, / and relying upon the moonlight / shining in the late autumn sky / close to twilight as their strength,

the party, resting their breath before / a time-worn shrine, stood up,

exchanging poems with one another, / and at the moment they thought of leaving this place once more to advance, / there came to be heard from below the slope / the neighing of steeds and the voices of men.

Tamakuniwake: 'Do not be so hot-headed and hasty. Rather than rashly pushing forward and thereby giving the enemy an advantage, it is better to remain perfectly calm, consider the situation from all angles, and, if you slowly recognize the necessity of the Kototama, concentrate your full strength and strike all at once. First of all, you had better calm down. Please become a little more cool-headed.'

Michikō: 'As a man of hot-blooded valor and fiery loyalty, my entire body's blood is already boiling and surging, and I can no longer sit still. In that case, Sensei, since you surely have your own ideas, please grant me independent action. No matter how much of a teacher you may be, you cannot possibly suppress and obstruct our actions taken for the sake of the Kamisama.'

Tamakuniwake calmly shook his head from side to side, looked around at the faces of the three men,

Tamakuniwake: 'Michikō, what you say is plausible enough at first glance. However, among our party of four... I am the leader. No matter how much of a genius you may claim to be, you do not understand the ultimate secrets of the Six Secret Teachings and Three Strategies. The path of the Kamisama is never monotonous; there are ever-changing secret tactics, so you had better hold back for a while.'

Putting strength into the end of his words, he spoke as if glaring at him. Michikō muttered an unpleasant 'Hē' in response and fell silent. In front of the shrine, the vanguard commanders Katahiko and Kumehiko were calling the roll, and their voices could be heard counting in order: one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve... Every single voice sounded as if the ends of the words were pitched upward steeply like a slope. Tamakuniwake spoke in a small, perfectly calm voice,

Tamakuniwake: 'Those are clearly the vanguard troops of Brahmanism, as expected. It seems they are coming to attack the Saiin Mansion. When you consider the divine administration of the Kamisama up to now, it is truly the height of ingenuity. Fascinating, fascinating; only through this can we manifest the glory of our departure.'

Michikō opened his mouth once more, crept closer to Tamakuniwake's knees, peered into the face of the propagandist hidden by the tree shade, and breathed slightly out of breath,

'Sensei, by your recent words, it seems you too acknowledge them as the enemy army attacking the Saiin Mansion. Knowing that, why do you remain so unconcerned and fail to initiate activity? You said it is time to manifest the glory of our departure, but if you let the enemy slip away unmolested and allow them to enter the mansion, how can that possibly be considered a feat of arms? Have you too perhaps had one eye taken from you, your mind and spirit suddenly plunged into discouragement, and become enveloped in the clouds of cowardice and irresolution?'

Tamakuniwake calmly replied,

'Now wait; there is a deep strategy behind this.'

Michikō: 'Hē, what a strange strategy indeed. Do you suppose it is the only strategy, like Kuropatkin's planned retreat?'

Itakō: 'Hey Michikō, that is the final resort of the thirty-six stratagems.'

Michikō: 'That is outrageous. How can our faces be saved by that, and above all, Sensei's face will not be saved either. There is a saying that beneath a brave general there are no weak soldiers. However, since the reverse is that beneath a weak general there are brave soldiers, the rules of the game are a bit different. Still, no matter how weak the commander may be, if the subordinates are strong, the commander's name is elevated. Hey, Itakō, Junkō, why don't you ally with me to force Sensei into retirement, carry out a major reform, and let me become the commander to try out great activities?'

Junkō: 'What are you talking about, you lowly commoner? How could we ever fathom Sensei's inner mind? If Sensei were someone whose depth we could measure, wouldn't his stature be pretty much fixed? Sensei's great personality leaps and bounds precisely in that vague and obscure realm where he cannot be grasped. Our duty is simply to follow commands and orders in all things. As a lowly person, do you have any right to criticize even Sensei's official authority? Besides, since you are a reckless fighter without an ounce of forethought, you even bring up the theory of banishing Sensei—what on earth are you thinking? Why don't you calm down a bit? How can petty warriors like fallen leaves, fluttering noisily at the slightest breeze, ever hope to accomplish this divine work?'

Michikō: 'That may well be, but look yonder—does it not make one's blood boil with irrepressible fury? Before the shrine enemies gather like a black mountain, even conducting a roll call, and marching on with flying banners and magnificent dignity; how can one possibly overlook this sight? If I had a three-foot shiusui, I would flash it in the moonlight and, with the vigor of a lion emerging from the thicket, cut down and mow them all down one by one, making them blow a single bubble of defeat—such are my heartfelt thoughts, but whatever one may say, Ananai-kyō is of the principle of non-resistance; if we speak of our weapons, they are merely the launching of Kototama, or failing that, weapons of fist-like rocks. Ah, what a vexing matter!'

Itakō: 'I too feel something within me, my whole body throbbing so that I cannot help it. In contrast to this, the Sensei, while facing a formidable enemy, remains as cold as a rock, silent as a mute. Now, whatever shall we do about this?'

Tamakuniwake quietly opened both his hands with a snap, making a gesture as if to hold down the three men, pressing the air down two or three times,

Tamakuniwake: 'Wait for a while, Until the divine edict descends, On the workings of heaven and earth Deeper than the sea.'

Michikō: 'Can this again be calmly looked over and ignored, With the enemy plainly confronting us right before our eyes?'

Tamakuniwake: 'To leave everything to the will of God alone, Know that this is the duty of you three.'

Michikō: 'This is astonishing, an unexpected teaching from our master's lips; I am simply struck dumb by such idle talk.'

Sumikō: 'Michikō, you speak of things you do not understand. Would you turn your back on the teachings of Tamakuniwake?'

Itakō: 'Whether this turning away is the path or truth, I know not, Yet somehow my heart is filled with impatience.'

Michikō: 'Itakō's heart is the very same as mine; Come, come, then, shall the two of us stand up?'

Tamakuniwake: 'Casting aside the life received from the imperial gods as a discarded bowl, How they clamor, these foolish pranks of the ignoble ones!'

Michikō: 'That indeed is an unexpected command; The foolish pranks belong to you, our master!'

Sumikō: 'Hey, you two, do not utter such rude things! What could blockheads possibly understand?'

Michikō: 'Two cowards and two heroes, Here at last we stand and part ways.'

Tamakuniwake: 'A pair of fellows with interesting words of bravado, Do not act against truth and commit a blunder.'

Tamakuniwake was by no means intimidated by the force of Katahiko's and Kumehiko's troops, nor had he hidden in the shadows of this forest for such a reason. His divine calculation and strategy were to let Katahiko's and Kumehiko's troop pass safely, and by the time they climbed as far as the direction of Kaikoku, Chiguniwake's party would surely arrive. If he did so, he intended to let the enemy pass ahead, shoot forth Kototama simultaneously from front and rear, and make the enemy rats trapped in a bag submit. However, it seemed to him of little value to reveal this secret strategy to his three subordinates. Indignant at Tamakuniwake's attitude, Michikō spoke in strong words,

Michikō: 'Hark, Sensei, as of this moment I take my leave.'

Tamakuniwake: 'Humph, if it is your desire, I shall grant you leave. It appears the enemy has frightened you greatly. What is that demeanor and action? You are altogether like konnyaku.'

Michikō: 'Yes, I have become frightened. It is because your hips are too weak that I find it frightening. We must consider the relative weight of attaching ourselves to God or following a master. I have not the slightest particle of a heart to abandon you, but for the sake of God, I swallow my tears and go against you. They say one must not step even three feet within the shadow of one's master, and so my feeling of heart in turning against you is more painful than swallowing red-hot iron, but please forgive me. This Michikō makes his life a target and charges toward the enemy. Even if by good fortune I obtain victory, I shall never make it my own achievement. It will still remain Sensei's great achievement, you see...'

Shedding tears, Tamakuniwake said in a small voice,

Tamakuniwake: 'Michikō, draw near, I have something to tell you from my ear.'

Feeling as though he had been brought back to life, Michikō gave two or three bounds before he knew it, leaping upon him and clinging to him just as a dog meeting a master it had not seen for a while, and pressed his ear to Tamakuniwake's lips.

A marching song could be heard coming from in front of the shrine. The four of them strained their ears, listening so as not to miss a single word.

With voices full of valor, the evil gods sang the marching song without shame before heaven and earth, as if they firmly believed their own side to be righteous. Itakō became frantic, and without uttering another word, running with the speed of Idaten, he pointed straight toward the dense formation, swinging his kongō-staff about and raging like a yaksha.

The army led by Katahiko and Kumehiko suddenly raised a clamor, running hither and thither through the steep and narrow valley paths, and their shouting voices echoed through the echoes of the valley.
