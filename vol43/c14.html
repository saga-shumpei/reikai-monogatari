§ 1

Before the shrine, the four of them, friend and foe alike, had untied the cords of their chin-straps and were laughing and making merry with utter artlessness.

Isokō: 'Hey, how amazingly interesting fellows have mixed into Brahmanism too! Today I really stretched out the wrinkles of my kintama. Ah, travelling is indeed such a pleasant thing.'

Matsukō: 'Even for me, today is the very first time I have encountered such an interesting dōbutsu. Not a bit of the Sensō mood is felt. It was just as if I were watching a kigeki for free. I've even come to feel like quitting my post as secretary, becoming a kigekishi, and trying to wield my arm to the full at the daigekijiyō in the capital of Haruna.'

Isokō: 'Well now, speaking of your style, your zeuzetsu, and from the point of view of your kiten, blowing incessantly into kotsukei and kaigiyaku is really something to mia. You will surely be able to become a top-tier actor, perhaps.'

Matsukō: 'Nah, no good. When I set out toward the mansion of Saien as a thousand-ryō (occupied) actor, I was blown about by such a storm and suffered a miserable defeat, you see.'

Isokō: 'That is precisely why you are unsuited for a military man. You are meant to be an actor through and through, no matter what.'

Matsukō: 'I wonder if that's so? Somehow, hearing you say that makes me want to apply to be an actor. I've grown sick of such a bleak business as this, so full of dangerous peril where one's head might fly off at any moment.'

Isokō: 'If that's the case, since meeting me here is also some sort of karmic bond, why don't you rouse yourself, make up your mind to reform, and become an attendant to a missionary of Misokyo? Once your training is complete, it is not outside the realm of possibility that you too could become a missionary.'

Matsukō: 'My, being an attendant to a missionary of Misokyo is no good either. If it comes to things like having one's eyes scratched by monkeys or being made a prisoner and driven into a cavern, it becomes utterly useless, ahahahaha.'

Junkō slid his knees closer, grasped Matsukō's right hand firmly, and with a trembling voice,

Junkō: 'What, you—from whom did you hear such a thing? Isn't it a bit funny? Confess everything right here.'

Matsukō: 'I didn't hear it from anybody at all. When I captured and interrogated Itakō, who had been accompanying the missionary, he confessed that my teacher, Tamakuniwake-sama, had his eyes scratched by a monkey.'

Junkō: 'Where on earth is that Itakō located? You must know, don't you? Come on, won't you confess quickly?'

Matsukō: 'As for that, I know. However, since I have been told by General Katahiko to keep it a secret, no matter what, I cannot tell you his whereabouts. But you may rest assured, as there is no danger to his life. Whatever the case, that fellow chatters away endlessly whenever anyone asks him, which makes him extremely convenient, so the fellows of Brahmanism put him in a cavern, make him drink sake every day to get him drunk, and have decided to hear all the secrets of the Saien Mansion. It's really a troublesome thing to have such a loose-mouthed fellow. Even without being asked, he blurted out the fact that he was scratched by a monkey. That is why General Katahiko and General Kumehiko also heard that Tamakuniwake was injured and went up Kajika Pass with a great sense of relief. And then, just as they thought they had weakened the wolf at the front gate, they encountered the tiger at the back gate—hence yesterday's defeat.'

Junkō: 'What, aren't you too blabbing secrets without even being asked? You're even worse than Itakō.'

Matsukō: 'Nonsense, since we've become like brothers with you fellows this way, isn't it fine to chat about a secret to that extent? After all, you're the kind ones who healed my uninjured (or rather, injured) legs, ahahahaha.'

Isokō: 'Since you've opened up to that extent, wouldn't it be fine to let us know Itakō's whereabouts? Won't you make a grand effort and memorialize Itakō's whereabouts, thinking of it as a donation to the god of the shrine? Unto the sacred hall... een.'

Matsukō: 'Hey, Tatsukō, what should we do? Somehow, don't you feel like it's a bit unfair to General Katahiko?'

'In the awe-inspiring forest of the shrine where the central pillars are stoutly built and the chigi rise high into Takamanohara, O Great Deity of Infinite Freedom, Great Oukunihiko, settled everlastingly and firmly, I, Matsukō, the commander of the Brahman army where the green leaves of a thousand years flourish in prosperity, humbly, reverently, and with utmost awe proclaim this. Receiving the august words of the Great Deity Oukunushi, General Hinodewake, General Kumehiko, and General Katahiko, leading a hundred troops for the pacification, ascended to suppress and harmonize the divine-elemental Sueharu and Kamusamu. As the vanguard, both generals advanced before the shrine where the moon shone brilliantly at the dusk of the fifteenth day, resting their breath for a while, checking the number of soldiers, and singing the august song of advancement, when from the shade of the forest trees appeared Itaikou of the Misoshiro teaching, who without a word struck into the midst of the troops, raging wildly in all directions, and regretfully struck down General Katahiko, whereupon the horses startled, pranced and leaped up, and the General fell down by the roadside onto Sukanatari. Crying out that a mighty warrior had appeared, I, Matsukō, grappled with that warrior, bound him with hands and arms tied, made three soldiers guard him, and hid him away in the rock cave of Mount Kiyoharu of the teaching. O August and Great Deities, awe-inspiring to name, illuminating us with your fierce august soul, protect and favor the captured Itaikou so that he may never return to the hands of the enemy. Moreover, protect all the soldiers of Oukunushi safely and peacefully without a single error, let them make their return report before the august presence of Oukunushi, thus praying with knees bent like the shika-no-jimono and throats pierced like the u-no-jimono, with heavenly awe, reverence, and trembling, we humbly offer our prayer. If it cannot be otherwise, may tamachi flourish, pon-pon.'
