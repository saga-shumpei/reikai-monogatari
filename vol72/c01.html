Chapter 1: 老の High 砂 [1810]

老の高砂〔1810〕

§ 1

Possessed by the power of Kami, the Niyoi Hotsushiyu stole the cord of the Murakimo heart, and starting from Onokorojima, it raced through the entire Sekai. At the Ageku, like a Suteobune on the shore of Karatama, it floated on the Biha Kosui. Using the Sankakuishi under the floor of Benten-sama as a Shirube in the dark night, it clawed until its Tsumasaki bled, searching for the Hitorigo it had dropped in its Dannen. Just as it searched for the Arika of the child, Tōsuke of Sumoto in Ahaji, upon meeting his old Kohibito, was instantly wrapped in a Kohigumo, and once again performed a Kiyōtai.

Banished from the Seichi of Aya, and having been built again at the foot of the mountain under a pretense, the Kamidate of the Ikuta Forest—having become its Tsukasa, he served the Ōkami with great diligence for a while. As the cold winter nights passed and spring arrived with budding leaves, the old woman's spirit rose again. Unable to extinguish the flames of love, she crossed the great ocean, traversed many unfamiliar mountains and fields, and through the May and June grass-pillow travel, the fatigue of the journey finally faded. She climbed up to the Ubusuna-yama where the Saien-kan, which had been revived, was located, and with the tip of the tongue of Furuna, she sought to meet the Tōsuke who served as the Sōmu.

Though she wept, pleaded, and pressed him, the Chibiki-iwa would not budge. There was no way to capture the iron-willed Tōsuke. Enduring the shame, she trudged along, her Ashura-like figure looking fierce as she glared at the Saien-kan. She kicked up sand with her hind legs, puffed out her shoulders and elbows, and shook her hips. 'Just you watch, Tōsuke. This is the heart of a woman who has set her mind on something, as firm as a bamboo arrow.' There is a precedent for an arrow piercing a rock, and pines grow even on the Chibiki-iwa. With her stubborn love that would not move for any lever or rod, she vowed to make the many officials lined up blow bubbles, and she climbed the wind-swept slope, dangling her Tokkuri-cobra.

With her worn-out sandals on her feet, her breath rough, her mouth twisted, and her eyes glaring, she shook her shoulders in the air. Stamping her feet, she climbed upward. 'Ah, Kannagara-kamu-nagara. May the spirits be blessed, and please grant that I may fulfill this love I have set my heart upon,' she prayed. When she arrived at the forest of the shrine, she saw an unexpected Kami-no-miya. Standing tall with its Chigi reaching high, the solemn and incomparable Kamitoku left Takahime speechless, and she stood there for a while. But the mischievous old woman Takahime, transforming into a golden-furred nine-tailed fox, brazenly advanced to the reception desk, waving her hands, and spoke in a stern voice.

The Kami-tsukasa, who had not been serving in the forest of the shrine for many days, believed her to be a messenger from the Saien-kan and let her pass into the inner sanctum. Takahime sat herself down there, and stopped the believers who were coming and going to the Saien-kan, plotting to lead them astray into her own path by killing their lice. She settled into the seat of the Kyōshu and sat deep within the inner hall. After a short while, hearing at the reception desk that the visitor, a Mahito named Memoaru, was a Tsukasa with the character 'To' attached to his name, Takahime slapped her knee. 'The person with the character 'To' at the sacred grounds of Ubusuna-yama must be Tōsuke-sama.'

Ashamed of the eyes of others, he treated her coldly, but in his heart, he surely wished to meet his wife. He must have climbed and descended the steep slopes of Kajikayama, longing for his old acquaintance Takahime. 'Ah, how grateful, how grateful. There is a proverb that says even a single strand of a woman's hair can pull a great elephant. Even though I am old, this body of mine is better than others in its flesh. My physical curves and the fragrance enveloping my whole body—perhaps he is a widowed bird who cannot forget and cannot abandon me, and comes longing for me. Tōsuke-sama is also a person who can talk a little about the path of love. This is interesting, this is interesting. In this hall where there are few eyes, I will plead to my heart's content.'

I will expose the shortcomings of the past and dye his face red like autumn leaves. Though I say so, even for a concubine like me, when I am made to wear the love-robe, my face turns red even though I am old. With a heart of red sincerity, we will treat each other with kindness. The branches of the old tree are in full bloom, small birds sing, and butterflies dance. Like a Kiraku-tonbo flying leisurely with its wings spread, extending its wings over the world, she boasted at the Saien-kan. 'If things go well, I will resume the path of Uranai.' As she roared with ambition, the man who entered, guided by the receptionist Iru, was, surprisingly, not Tōsuke, but the Tokioki-shi who always got in her way.

§ 2

At the sight of Mokusuke Sōmu, Takahime was startled, as if she might collapse, yet encouraged by the support of Fukushiyu, she straightened her knees, adjusted her collar, and planted her thick buttocks firmly. Narrowing her Dongurimanako, she offered all the coquetry she could muster, and her style, with her mouth—missing its front teeth—forced into a pucker, resembled nothing so much as a mouse on a shelf nibbling at a rice cake. Takahime thought to herself, 'Even my sworn enemy Mokusuke is a creature of flesh, not wood or stone; surely he must know a little of passion. Since they say the rules of love are first, wealth; second, money; and third, talent, then surely this Ekubo of mine, belonging to the peerless and beautiful Takahime, could swallow even a demon or a serpent, and there is no way he could fail to be taken captive.' Nodding to herself, thinking, 'Yes, indeed, yes, indeed,' she spoke: 'Now, now, Tokiokashi, Mokusuketsukasa Sōmu-sama, you have come to visit at last. You, who are known as one of the Sanbagarasu, are an Eiketsu many times greater than Azumanowake. Pray, tell me in detail the reason for your visit.' With such loathsome, clinging behavior, the great Yōmi of Ōkumoyama, Yōgenbō, who had disguised himself as Mokusuke Sōmu, raised his face, twitched his nose, and with a haughty air, opened his red mouth, squeezing out a rasping voice and laughing with a cackle that shook the very hall.

'Now, now, Takahime-sama, Ikumiya-sama, you fleshly palace of the Sun God, your intense will for love is so enviable that even to see it from the side makes me yearn for you, so I have come following in your footsteps. It is a pity for Azumasuke-sama, but though it is said to be in the presence of others, to pick up the flower of love once discarded is also an act of charity. I have come with courage, thinking we should consult on this, as a secret maneuver of love, having crossed over the steep mountain passes to reach you, my lovely man,' he said. At the sacred site in the forest of the shrine, they began their negotiations: 'The second and third generations are trivial; let us pledge our vows here for five hundred lives to come, knotted together like kelp, an old couple sleeping and acting like dried cuttlefish.'

'Our fame shall be like the Takasago, and just as the boat of the Urafune you possess, with its full or partial sails hoisted, drifted and split the boat at the shadow of the island of Awaji, so shall we row out our precious boat. Shall we live happily and amusingly as a married couple, like the moon and flowers of Suminoe, with peaceful hearts?' When he said this, Takahime clicked her throat, thinking she had caught a fine duck. Celebrating with a drink of 'chin-chin-kamo-kamo,' she filled a precious cup with the dew of jewels poured from the sake bottle of Mokusuke-sama, and they spent the night in talk of seedless tales, pledging their deep love like autumn eggplants, intending to play at night-boating. To such a place, the messenger of propaganda, Hatsuwakahime, appeared.

While pretending not to see the disgraceful behavior of Takahime-sama, she halted her horse for a brief moment. To dispel the Magatsumi in the forest of the shrine, she cleverly planted a suggestion under the floor, causing Yōgenbō, who had been lurking there, to have his heart shattered into a thousand pieces as she sought to save Takahime-tsukasa, who was in the presence of the gods, and return her to Takamanohara. Just as she paused, Yōgenbō swiftly took Takahime with him and vanished into the clouds and mist, no one knew where. Ah, Kamunagara Kamunagara, illuminated by the grace of the gods, the whereabouts of the ugly Takahime are being clarified in complete detail.

The Mizuki of the Exit of the Gods, which is said to be one of the three most scenic spots in Japan, is where I, on the boat of the oral narrative platform, in the inn's annex, with a pure and clear heart, go on to convey in detail the circumstances of how Yōgenbō and Takahime were led astray by love and desire, standing against the backdrop of Amanohashidate, which rises on the beach of white sand and scenic old pines. Ah, Kamunagara Kamunagara, may your spirits be blessed.
