Chapter 14: New 宅入 [1823]

新宅入〔1823〕

§ 1

As they crossed the Kosui of Haru, a sudden Baufū blew, causing the Takasagomaru to Chinbotsu. Eugenbau's Mokusuke, carrying Takahime upon his back, drifted amidst the waves. At last, they arrived at the shore of Itefu on the island of Tama, where bamboo grew thick and which floated in the Kochiu. There, the two finished doing various evil deeds, stole a boat from the shore, and Mokusuke steered the oar. Being unaccustomed to the sea from the start, they were tossed about by the waves, drifting hither and thither. They wandered for a Shiujitsu and a Shiuya upon the water, suffering from Sukibara, until they finally reached the port of Suga, barely saving their Inochi.

Takahime and Mokusuke, the Riyōnin, stopped briefly at an Udonya along the Kohen to soothe their Sukibara. Just then, hearing the rumors of passersby that a grand Ōmiya of the Ananai-kyō had been newly built on Mount Suga with high Chigi, their Shiyokushi began to stir greatly. Thinking to themselves how they might devise a Kufū to seize that sacred place, they were indeed presumptuous. As the day turned to dusk, they checked into the Taruya Inn in the Nakano-machi, a prominent location, and while seeking lodging for the night, the two easily fell into a sweet slumber there.

Takahime and Mokusuke woke up early in the morning and, while considering the state of the inn, noticed that it was a wider estate than it had appeared, with a new annex built upon it. Furthermore, that annex faced the highway of Kitamachi, making it an extremely suitable structure for missionary work and propaganda. Eugenbau used the art of the Kuruma, picking up seven or eight leaves from the garden, and after chanting some mumbling words, they instantly transformed into hundred-yen bills. He secretly hid them in his bosom, and with an indifferent face, sat down heavily before Takahime.

Mokusuke: 'Oi, Chigusa no Taka-chan, is this not a fine structure? It would be interesting if you were to conduct your specialty, missionary propaganda, right here.'

Takahime: 'Indeed, as expected of Mokusuke-sama. You are very observant. I too have been thinking that since the Ananai-kyō fellows have built a magnificent palace on Mount Suga and are propagating with such great force, I feel a sense of unease that I cannot bear. I had considered storming Mount Suga immediately, peeling the skin off the faces of the Shinji, and breaking their Dōjō. However, that would be too childish, and I was just now worrying about how it would affect my future credibility if the townspeople thought I had occupied it by force. But even if it is a convenient house, once we were trapped in the lake and left stark naked, having lost all our travel funds, we cannot even rent a house, can we? Even though we are staying at an inn looking so important, I wonder what we shall do when the time comes to pay the bill. When I remember that, the inn's food does not even go down my throat sweetly. If we do not slip away tonight, we will be dragged off to the Brahman's office for something like dining without payment.'

Mokusuke: 'Ha ha ha, no need for worry. I am not a Mokusuke who would be careless about such things. Why not consult with the master and buy that annex?'

Takahime: 'Even if you say buy it, it is impossible if we have no money. If we had at least a deposit, we could talk, but with us not even having the money for last night's lodging, how could we do such a thing? Ah, how I wish I had money.'

Eugenbau: 'I want money too, but although I have bills, I have not a single bit of hard currency. As they say, "Though I have many bills and promissory notes, I struggle with copper coins (dōka) and hard currency (kōka)." If there is no hard currency (kōka), there is no effect (kōka) to the talk. Still, I wish to obtain that house by some means (dōka).'

Takahime: 'Even if there is no hard currency, it would be fine if there were paper money, but we do not even have a single piece of nose-paper, so there is nothing to be done.'

§ 2

Mokusuke smiled broadly and, while patting his breast pocket three or four times, said:

Mokusuke: 'Hey, Taka-chan, put your hand in here for a moment and take a look. Your favorite thing is staring right at you.'

Takahime, feeling suspicious, immediately thrust her right hand into Eugenbau's breast pocket, and her fingers touched seven or eight crisp hundred-yen bills. Startled, she fell backward onto her bottom and said:

Takahime: 'Oh my, oh my, Mokusuke-chan, do not do such dangerous things. You must have thought about the time I was asleep last night and gone somewhere to do something or other; you are truly a frightening person.'

Eugenbau: 'Ha, ha, ha, do not be so surprised. This Mokusuke would never do anything like stealing. By using the art of Magawa, I simply picked up some fallen leaves from the garden and turned them into banknotes for a moment.'

Takahime had a habit of believing anything without question whenever the art of Magawa was mentioned.

Takahime: 'Oh my, what a great person you are. That is exactly why you are the husband of the Ikimiya of Hinodenokami. If we have this money, why don't we negotiate with the owner and try to acquire that house? There is also a detached building standing in the back, and it would be very convenient for you to rest there.'

Eugenbau: 'Hmm, that is true. I cannot rest properly unless I have a separate building, so how about it? Won't you negotiate with the owner for me?'

Takahime: 'Yes, I understand.'

Saying this, she clapped her hands together. After a while, a maid opened the sliding door softly, knelt down politely, and said:

Maid: 'Is it you who summoned me?'

Takahime: 'Ah, yes. You appear to be the maid of this house, but I have no business with a maid. Please go and call the master for a moment, and while you are at it, bring the bill from last night as well.'

The maid said, 'Yes, certainly,' and hurried away. Takahime turned the banknotes she had taken from Mokusuke's pocket over and over, but she could not see them as fakes no matter how hard she looked. With her courage increased a hundredfold, she waited impatiently for the master to arrive. Soon, an old man of about fifty with a face full of pockmarks poked his shiny, bald head out and said:

Master: 'Yes, I am the owner of this house. I have come as you requested.'

Takahime: 'How much is the bill?'

Master: 'Yes, for the two of you, it will be one yen and fifty sen.'

Takahime: 'In that case, this includes the tip.'

As she said this and threw out a hundred-yen note, the master looked at their faces in surprise and said:

Master: 'Even if I receive such a large amount of money, I have no change. Please, I ask that you provide something smaller.'

Takahime: 'No, if you have no change, that is fine. One yen and fifty sen is the lodging fee for last night, and ninety-eight yen and fifty sen is the tip.'

Master: 'In this day and age, when the innkeepers' association has abolished tips, if I were to accept something like this, I would be ostracized by my peers, so please take it back.'

Takahime: 'Ah, if a tip is no good, I shall give it to you as a souvenir. That should be fine, right?'

Master: 'Yes, if it is a souvenir, I will gladly accept any amount. Thank you very much. Please stay as long as you like. It is quite inconvenient, but I ask for your patience for the time being.'

Takahime: 'By the way, Master-dono, it is my husband's wish to know if that detached building standing across the garden belongs to this house.'

Master: 'Yes, that is correct. It has just been completed, and although the tatami mats and sliding doors have been installed, no one has moved in yet. It is truly a brand-new place.'

Takahime: 'I will pay any amount of money, so would you allow us to use that house?'

Master: 'Yes, since you are a customer who has favored us so often, I will do as you say, whether it be selling it or renting it to you.'

Takahime: 'If it is all the same, I would prefer to have it sold to me, as I would have to ask for permission every time I wanted to make any repairs to a rented house.'

§ 3

Teishiyu: 'Everything you say is perfectly reasonable; if you wish, I shall sell it to you.'

Takahime: 'For how much will you sell it? The price does not matter to me at all.'

Teishiyu: 'It may be a little expensive, but I would like to ask for five hundred yen.'

Takahime: 'Very well, then please accept these five hundred yen. And since I shall have to trouble you for various things, I will give you this one hundred yen as a token of my appreciation.'

The landlord had been at his wit's end because a tenant had hanged himself in the annex, leading to rumors that ghosts or monsters appeared there every night, so no one would rent it and even his own family was too creeped out to enter. Since someone was offering to buy it for the large sum of five hundred yen in cash, it was as if a botamochi had fallen from the shelf; he agreed immediately and wrote out the bill of sale on the spot. From this day, Mokusuke and Takahime moved into the annex, hung up a large sign for the Uranai-kyō, and began preparations for their propaganda.

Takahime: 'Now, Moku-chan, we have our own comfortable nest, so please rest well. From tomorrow, we must work hard to gather many believers and give the Sangokyo of Mount Suga a taste of their own medicine.'

Yōgenbō: 'Ah, to think that from tomorrow I will have to hear those names of good-for-nothing gods—the Heavenly Being of the First Spirit Kingdom, the Living Palace of the Hinodewake, the Great Miroku of the Sototsu-iwane, the Savior of the Three Thousand Worlds, the Hegre-Hegre-Hegre Warrior, the Great God of the Hegre Shrine, the Great God of Rintōbiten, the Life of Kiso Yoshikohime, Jōdō Yukinari, Chijōmaru, Chijōhime, Minister Tagaya, the Life of Sadakohime, Kizukihime, Kotouehime, and so on—until my ears grow calloused; just thinking about it makes my head ache.'

Takahime: 'Now Moku-chan, I am working with all my might to open the path of the gods, yet you always ridicule me. People who say their heads ache or their eyes swim just from hearing the names of the gods are inviting divine punishment.'

Yōgenbō: 'That is exactly why I am leaving the Uranai-kyō to you, my lady, while I, this Mokubē-sama, shut myself in a room in the detached building to rest and stretch my legs. I would not want to get in the way of your propaganda, you see.'

Takahime: 'You are too great a personage, so it is inappropriate for you to preach directly to the people; please rest in the detached building during the day. In return, I will have tasks for you to perform at night; you will be very happy, won't you? You are so cute.'

Yōgenbō: 'It is as if you have mistaken me for a stud horse. Well then, while the mountain goddess is in a good mood, I shall head to the detached building. Now, go ahead and sell the Living Palace of the Hinodewake and the Great Miroku-sama.'

Saying this, he carried his massive frame to the detached room, making the floorboards creak with every heavy step, locked the door from the inside, reverted to his original monster form, and fell asleep with a loud snoring sound. Yōgenbō found it extremely painful to remain in human form, so he always required a room where he could not be seen from the outside. Takahime, feeling that the spring sun had finally returned, began to shout her propaganda on the main road while swinging her thick hips. Though her hips were large, she possessed the body of Chigusahime, and she had an air of nobility and grace; if she did not speak, one might mistake her for a high-ranking lady or the reincarnation of Benten-sama. Takahime's desperate propaganda seemed to take effect immediately, and from the following day, men and women of all ages flocked to her, resulting in such a crowded, bustling success that the number of pilgrims far exceeded those at the shrine on Mount Suga.
