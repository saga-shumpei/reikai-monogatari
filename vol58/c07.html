Chapter 7: Kami Ship [1482]

神船〔1482〕

§ 1

Tamakuniwake and his party arrived at the shore of Lake Telmon. With wings spread in the shape of the character for eight, the Amboina glided over the vast, purple surface of the water—now high, now low—displaying a majestic air as if to say, 'The great bird does not rush its wings.' Flocks of Ahaudori and cormorants flew in a straight line, busy with their wings, gathered in groups of one hundred or two hundred to the east, west, south, and north. Although the surface was calm, as befits a great lake of such renown, it was stirred by a faint north wind, and waves of Shichigosan were beating like drums against the shore. Gazing into the distance, the beach of white sand and green pines stood in rows, their outlines perfectly aligned as if carved, presenting a scene so magnificent and breathtaking that it seemed to wash the very soul; it was enough to make one think of the lake of Suha with its single island.

Tamakuniwake gazed out over the vast, rippling surface of the lake.

Tamakuniwake: 'The sound of the drums of the waves washing ashore also rings clearly in the ears of Tamakuniwake. Are those flocks of cormorant birds fishing, flying back and forth over the lake surface to the right and left?'

Michihiko: 'The irises blooming on the shore of the lake seem to mimic the white waves that strike it.'

Masumihiko: 'The vast sky and the surface of the lake are both clear; is this the mirror reflecting the tide of Michihiko?'

Itahiko: 'Looking out, I see the pine forest of Chin, visible beyond the white waves that look like clouds or mist.'

Princess Davis: 'My heart thunders as I gaze upon the figures of the mandarin ducks floating and playing on the surface of the water.'

Masumihiko: 'The words pledging love until a thousand generations are held back in the mouth; they are a young couple of mandarin ducks.'

Tamakuniwake: 'Since leaving the Saien-kan, my eyes, which have only traversed mountains and fields, find this rare. The wide, deep, and clear appearance of this lake must be the spirit of Mizu. I wish to show this scenery once more to the Lord God Susanoo. If it were to follow the desires of my heart, I would make this lake a souvenir for my home. The grace of the Imperial Gods is deep; this is a lake whose bottom is unknown, even at a depth of eight thousand fathoms.'

Princess Davis: 'How shall we cross this lake? We are on a journey today with no boat to rely upon.'

Masumihiko: 'Alas, Princess Davis, a goddess who appears while holding the jewel boat all by herself.'

Princess Davis: 'This boat is not a vessel to carry people of the world; it is the exclusive property of my husband. I think to pick up the crow shells gathered on the shore of the lake and return them to the boat.'

Michihiko: 'If we ride on the wings of the Amboina, we could easily pass over, following the will of the Gods.'

Itahiko: 'No matter how long we suffer and worry, there is no way to cross this distant path of waves.'

Thus, the five members of the party stood on the lakeshore, composing mediocre poems while feeling somewhat perplexed as to how they might cross the water. Just then, a fishing boat came running as if shot by an arrow. The party, holding onto the hope that this was a rescue ship, waited for the boat to arrive at their side. The boatman, in a listless voice, spoke:

Boatman: 'Hey, you lot look like missionaries of the San-go-kyo, but do you intend to cross this lake? We have received many favors from the Lord Daikokunushi of the capital of Haruna... and have received strict orders that if the missionaries of the San-go-kyo come here, we must never ferry them across. Even if I wanted to ferry you, I cannot. But having said that, even a single sheet of paper has a front and a back. There are boats for the sea, air for the water, women for men, hammers for chisels, and ink for inkstones; these things have been properly determined since ancient times. Depending on how you present yourself, it is not impossible that I might ferry you. What do you intend to do? Do you intend to stand there forever, gazing at the lake like a stone Jizo statue meant to ward off drowning? If there is no reply, I will take this boat back to the other side, so you had better think of something.'

Itahiko: 'Hey, boatman, even if you say that, I don't understand the point, do I? You mean you can't ferry us openly, but if we give you a lot of money, you will ferry us, right? If that is the case, I understand. I will give you whatever you want, so please ferry us to the opposite shore quickly.'

Boatman: 'No matter what you say, this lake is two hundred ri from north to south, so it is not a matter of just crossing it in a moment. Once I have you on board, I must feed you, and no matter how much we hurry, it will take ten days, so it will not pay unless I receive a considerable amount. I must settle the potential for future quarrels now, for it will be of no use if you complain about the price being high or low after we arrive on the other side.'

Tamakuniwake: 'I will pay you any amount, so please set sail quickly.'

Sendō: 'In that case, will you pay me one hundred ryō? Since there are five of you and one dog, that does not even average twenty ryō each. It is a cheap price, is it not?'

Tamakuniwake: 'If one pays one ryō, one can get one koku of rice, so is one hundred ryō not a bit expensive?'

Sendō: 'If you think it is expensive, shall we stop? Goodbye then.'

He showed an intention to leave quickly by rowing the scull. Princess Debisu, fearing it would be a disaster if he escaped, became anxious.

Debisu: 'Sendō-sama, I will pay the one hundred ryō as you wish. Please, I beg you, take us across to the other side quickly.'

Sendō: 'Ah, how grateful I am. You are a princess from the Kamuyakata, are you not? You must be eloping somewhere with such a handsome man. One hundred ryō is cheap. Pay me another one hundred ryō. If you do so, I will keep secret the fact that you crossed this lake to elope. One hundred ryō is a cheap price for keeping one's mouth shut, is it not?'

Debisu: 'Oh, I have no choice. I will pay as you wish, so please let us board quickly.'

The Sendō brought the boat alongside with a grin. The five of them, along with Smart, jumped aboard quickly.

Just then, they raised the white sail to the gently blowing Kitakaze, and for a short while, they slid smoothly across the Komen. The Sendō stood at the stern, gripping the scull in his hand, and from his thick throat, which had been broken by the wind, he began to sing a Funauta in a voice that seemed to ring clear.

'Here is the, ah-eh, Lake Terumon of Amagi, Crossing is, ah, a joy, a married couple, eh, In the land of, ah-oh, The moon, there are famous places, ah, Haruna's, ah, eh, Capital's, oh, lotus pond, The waves are, ah, echoing, is it the sound of a drum? Or perhaps, ah, the Princess of the Dragon Palace?'

As he sang, the boat gradually left the shore and proceeded in a straight line toward the south. Little by little, the old pine trees at the boarding place became smaller in appearance, and the summit of Mount Terumon began to look taller instead. At this moment, four rough men appeared from the bottom of the boat, wearing net shirts and net trousers over their entire bodies, and brandishing long swords, they advanced toward the five, wearing disgusting smiles as they glared at them. This was a plot by the four men—Wax, Exis, Helman, and El—who intended to settle their grudge on this lake, having intentionally given the Sendō a large sum of money to lure the five men and women to the center of the lake and cut them down.

Wax: 'Ah, how rare, Sanchihiko, and the other sorcerers of the Sangokyō, as well as the Amatsu-onna, Princess Debisu, who brought shame upon us. How well you have been lured by our scheme. Now that we have lured you this far, you will not be able to escape, no matter what kind of mysterious magic you use. Come, we four shall make you the rust of our dragon-slaying swords. Even this dog-beast will be able to do nothing on the lake. You had all better resign yourselves to your fate. Since several dozen rough men are hidden in the bottom of this boat, it is useless even if you struggle. Will you gracefully hand over Princess Debisu to us and throw yourselves into this lake to meet your end? If not, I am sorry, but we will make you the rust of our swords. What a dull-witted fool you are, anyway.'

Tamakuniwake remained calm and did not panic in the slightest, beginning to chant the sacred number songs. For some reason, a violent storm suddenly blew in, waves like mountains raged, and the boat began to sway back and forth and side to side as if it were a leaf being scattered. The villains, including the formidable Wax, began to roll about—rolling to the right and rolling to the left—as the boat swayed, leaving them with nowhere to stand, tumbling about like dung mixed into rice bran. Even the formidable Tamakuniwake began to feel dizzy and nauseated from the excessive swaying of the boat. Regardless of friend or foe, they all prayed in the depths of their hearts, a prayer to the gods for when all else fails. At the moment the boat swayed, the Sendō was struck by the handle of the scull, tumbled over, and was thrown into the raging waves with a splash; like a stone Jizō statue being thrown in, he sank deep to the bottom of the lake without a sound or a struggle. Just then, a boat appeared, with seven or eight young men rowing with all their might, aiming for this spot. As it turned out, Hatsuwakahime, a missionary of the Sangokyō, had anticipated that this would happen and had been waiting, hidden in the shadow of an island. Hatsuwakahime appeared, standing at the bow.
