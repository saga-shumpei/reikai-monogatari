Chapter 5: 復命 [1279]

復命〔1279〕

§ 1

At last, the Ishitsuki was completed, and Michikō, Harukō, Itakō, Sumikō, and all the others spent over a hundred days building a magnificent shrine. The ceremony of the transfer of the deity was set to take place on the night of Setsubun. Believers gathered from all directions, and the wide valley where the forest of the shrine stood became so crowded that there was not even room to stand a needle, as the believers assembled to participate in this ceremony. A vast amount of offerings was piled up like a mountain. The group of Brahmins, including Iru, Iku, Sāru, Yoru, Teru, and Haru, led by Michikō, also joined the ritual officials, wearing the splendid, newly tailored ritual garments bestowed from the mansion of Iso, and entered the Shinsenjiyo to begin preparing the Shinsen, which numbered in the hundreds of trays.

Yoru: 'Hey, today's festival is thanks to me. You should worship me before you worship the gods.'

He was slurring his words with a face bright red from stealing sips of Miki.

Iru: 'Hey, you Yoru, what do you think you are doing? Wearing ritual garments and drinking the Miki before even offering it to the gods? Do you have no sense of propriety?'

Yoru: 'Hey, Iru, what are you spitting out? Did Tamakuniwake-sama not say that tonight is Yoru's festival? It is not Iru's festival. That is why I am tasting it first to offer it to the gods of my throat, and then I will hold the festival for the god of Yoru. Since my spirit is clearly good, they are enshrining the spirit of Yoru in such a magnificent shrine, so I am truly great, am I not?'

Iku: 'But even if we offer this many money and goods, I wonder if the gods will accept them. It might even be a nuisance; since the gods are all formless, they should not be eating these tangible offerings that humans consume. As proof, no matter how long we leave them out, not a single piece of fruit has decreased. Instead of offering so many things, wouldn't it be fine to just offer two or three trays to the three of them as representatives? It looks just like a greengrocer's shop. Hey, Yoru, what do you think?'

Yoru: 'It seems you still do not understand the things of God. When the gods descend to the earth, they use the human fleshly palace as an engine, so must we not serve them based on the laws of the natural world? To say that the gods who accept the offerings of believers are formless and therefore material food is unnecessary is the act of a false believer who does not reside in the love of God and cannot bathe in the blessings of God, and thus does not provide money or goods for this magnificent festival. A festival is a method of worship. To worship is to wait for a person; it is the same as inviting a guest. It is a divine ritual that weighs good and truth in the balance and harmonizes human love with the love of God. That is why it is called "matsuri" (to wait/to serve). Even if you say the offerings you have given to the gods do not decrease, the spirits of the essential offerings are all being consumed. The taste of a daikon radish, the taste of wasabi, the taste of fish—that each has its own distinct flavor is all created by the gods, and it is neither the doing of humans nor something that the daikon or wasabi themselves have achieved. Even if you plant them in the same soil and use the same fertilizer, if you sow chili peppers, they become spicy; if you grow watermelons, they become sweet; if you grow Japanese pepper, it becomes spicy again; and each has its own characteristics in its sweetness or spiciness. These are all things created by the inflow of divine virtue. That is why humans on earth, having been given magnificent things by the gods, cannot help but be grateful. Therefore, we offer offerings with all our hearts to thank God for His blessings. The reason such a large amount of offerings has gathered like this is that, even if it was a little bit at a time, this many people each brought something, and thus dust piled up to form a mountain. Since God accepts the sincere heart of humans, if someone brings even a single leaf of greens saying they want to offer it, we must offer it all. That is the reason why a festival is a festival, so...'

Teru: 'Even so, are there not some who rejoice and feel grateful without even carrying a single ichimon for Saisen, or offering a single leaf of greens? What becomes of them?'

Yoru: 'Such people are like those who rejoice while dreaming. Since this Yoru-sama is the festival of Yoru, I remember all the humans who have made offerings, but those who perform karamai are karamitama, so I do not grant them blessings. Because it is a world under the shiyugo of Yoru, you see. If you do not bring a tegata of some sort, the Kamisama of Yoru cannot understand your shinkau.'

Teru: 'Your Kamisama is not the Kamisama of Yoru, but a Kamisama of greed, is it not?'

Yoru: 'Do not say such baka things. The Kamisama of Yoru is the Kamisama for those humans who love Kami, follow Kami, and possess the magokoro to want to offer even a single leaf of greens to Kamisama. In other words, it is the Kamisama that sorts things out. Is it not said that those who are chosen go to Tengoku? To choose is to yoru. However, because it is a Kamisama that tsugafu to the circumstances of the time, well, sake or anything else will do, so please bring it, please carry it, ahahahaha!'

Haru: 'Are you not saying things that sound just like Brahmanism? To say that if one offers things to Kamisama there will be blessings, and that Kamisama cannot love those who do not offer, does that not slightly teishiyoku with the shinkaku as Kami?'

Yoru: 'Whether it be Brahmanism or Ananai-kyō, there is not more than one festival, is there? Since Kamisama is surely not a human kojiki, He is not a personage who maintains His seimei by means of kiyoshiyutsu, but when the heart of love arises in all things, humans naturally want to offer something to Kamisama. Furthermore, when Kamisama loves humans, He becomes of a mind to give them fields and ridges as well. When an elderly parent has a miyage bought and brought to them by a musuko or daughter, or when they hear that a grandchild has bought and brought something, even if it is a kinshō amount, thinking they want to give this to their grandfather or grandmother, do you not know how much that grandfather or grandmother rejoices, even if it is a mere trifle? They will tell everyone they meet, saying it was something their musuko bought for them, or that their grandchild brought it from harubaru, or sent it to them, and they will rejoice. And when a grandchild gives them something worth only nisanjitsusen, the grandfather or grandmother will take out ten yen from their secret savings and quietly give it to the grandchild. Love corresponds with love, and goodness corresponds with goodness. That is why a festival is called a true matching. Grandfathers and grandmothers never desire to have their children or grandchildren buy and bring them miyage... just as Kamisama never desires offerings. However, there is a tremendous difference in the emotional state of love at that time between the heart of the child or grandchild when they give a miyage and when they do not. That is why those who wish to touch the love of Kami must love Kami. No matter how much one may exert one's heart as a human, there is no other means or method to repay the debt of gratitude to Kamisama than to offer that magokoro to Kami through money and goods.'

Teru: 'Even so, humans should just exert themselves for the sake of Kami with their spirit and love Kami. Hey, Iku, Saru, do you not think so, you?'

Saru: 'That may have a point, but as the Kamisama of Yoru says, if a heart of love arises, it will surely not stop halfway; it will reach the final point. That final point is the actual practice of humans. If it is the truth that things begin from the spirit and settle in the body, then I think that offering rare items from the mountains, fields, rivers, and seas, or heihakuryō to Kamisama, is the manifestation of what is called love and sincerity.'

Haru: 'Indeed, there is no mistake in that. Otherwise, how could Takawakewake-sama and Yoshikohime-sama be holding such a grand festival?'

The place for offerings was bustling. Then, Itakō and Junkō appeared.

The Harahidoshiki and the Shinsendengu were completed, and the Sengūnorito of Tamakuniwake was finished, after which the event moved on to the Mochima. The Norito of the Harahidonushi and the Sengūshiki are omitted.

The small rice cakes, pounded with a rhythmic thud day and night, were said to amount to five koku, six to, and seven shō. The greater part of these had been shaped by the hands of Isokohime and Imakohime. The Shinsen staff, including Iru, Iku, Sāru, Yoru, Teru, and Haru, as well as the Shinsenchiyō Sumikō, climbed onto the high platform and threw the rice cakes in all directions with all their might. The multitude of men and women, young and old, surged like waves toward the place where the rice cakes fell, trampling over people and knocking them down in their effort to pick up even one more. While they usually listen to the teachings of the gods and understand in their hearts that humans must yield to others and be humble—and even preach this to others in a self-important manner—in such situations, they fully display their animal nature without reservation. It is just like throwing a piece of rotten meat into a place where puppies are playing together, seemingly in close intimacy, licking and frolicking with each other. Thinking of this, it appears that as long as humans possess a physical body, they cannot escape from greed and desire. If one were to try to receive even one of these rice cakes to take home and share with family or neighbors to distribute the divine virtue, one would not be able to pick up even half a rice cake if one remained quiet and stayed in the back. Many believers who had taken such an attitude at the beginning were pushed down and trampled because they were hesitant, and since they could not pick up any rice cakes, they were seized by what is called mob psychology; even men who had been so humble and obedient began to slowly tighten their headbands, spit on their hands, push down those who were in their way, and, feeling that it would be a loss not to pick up even one extra to stuff into their bosoms, they began to run wild all at once, which was disastrous. From the edge on that side came the sound of children crying 'Kyaa, kyaa,' and from the corner on this side came 'An, an,' as it suddenly transformed into a miserable scene of the Shuradō. The Mochima finally ended, and the trumpet of truce for the battle over the rice cakes sounded. From there, each believer bowed to the newly built shrine, and with joy, they descended the Kajika Pass, returning home as the sun set. Remaining behind were the propaganda staff, the festival committee members, and a dozen or so enthusiastic believers.

Incidentally, three shrines were built; in the center, Kuninotokotachi-no-Mikoto, the Great God of the Sun, and the Great God of the Moon were enshrined. On the left side, the Great God of the Universe, Ōkunihiko-no-Mikoto, as well as the Great God Pangu, Shionagahiko-no-Kami, were enshrined, and on the right side, the God of Yamaguchi and the eight million gods were enshrined. At the same time as this festival ended, the eye disease of Tamakuniwake was completely cured, and his face had changed slightly, even in its form, appearing more fulfilled than before, and it seemed as if he had suddenly acquired a divine character. Here, Tamakuniwake decided to hold a Naorai banquet in the large hall of the shrine office. Those who sat at this table were all the officials who had participated in this festival and a dozen or so enthusiastic believers. To celebrate the safe completion of the enshrinement, Tamakuniwake began to sing while partaking in the sacred sake.

Shizuko: 'My husband has become an official of the shrine; protect us, Kuniteruhime-no-Kami.'

Kaede: 'My father has now become an official of the Kami; protect my mother and us. I shall firmly keep the Oshihe of Kuniteruhime-no-Kami and serve.'

Kuniteruhime: 'Well then, now that the shrine of the Kami has been completed without incident, I shall return to heaven.'

Thus singing, the Kami ascended, and Isokohime returned to her original physical body.

Isokohime: 'Well then, my husband, fulfill the command of the Kami without incident. Even if Isokohime remains in the mansion of Iso, my spirit shall be by your side, my lord.'

Tamakuniwake: 'Make the sincerity of Tamakuniwake-no-Mikoto your strength and serve the Kami.'

Thus, while exchanging poems with each other, they bowed respectfully before the Ōkami. While praying for the light of their future, Tamakuniwake entrusted the future to Uzuhiko, Shizuko, Kaede, and the other six officials of the Brahman group and the fervent believers. Together with Michiharawake, Masumihiko, Itahiko, and Michihiko, Tamakuniwake descended the Kajika Pass with grace, singing the propaganda song while bathed in the light of the early spring sun.
