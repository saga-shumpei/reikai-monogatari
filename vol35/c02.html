Chapter 2: 出陣 [966]

出陣〔966〕

§ 1

Ananai-kyō's Sendenshi Kurohimetsukasa quietly departed from Takehi's mansion, and proceeded along the Hinokuni highway over the steep, rocky, and undulating slope.

Whether by divine shigumi or by mere gūzen, she unconsciously strayed and lost her footing; taking the right turn instead of the left into the animichi where she should have gone, she presently approached the foot of the Mukafutauge in a deeply lonely state.

Stepping upon the marukibashi over Fukatanigaha, half-decayed as it was, with the feeling of walking on hakuhiyō, she was aided by the divine grace and hurried across to the other side.

As she traced the hosotanimichi where the foliage of the shiyōjiyu blocked out the sky, she encountered a hollow-cheeked, innocent young otome named Oume.

When she asked about the jijiyō, she learned that Torakō, a shiranamiotoko renowned for his gikefu in Takenomura, was Oume's elder brother; halting in her tracks, she comforted Oume and asked why she, a mere otome, was wandering along this yamamichi.

Hardly had she asked when Oume's eyes filled with tears, and she cried: 'A brute known as Daija no Sankō came with a host of kobun to the empty home while my elder brother was away, tied up my elder sister Ane-sama with her arms bound tight behind her, and dragged her to the foot of the Mukafutauge, bringing her into the shade of a camphor tree.'

'There, my elder sister and I were tormented by the underlings of Sankō, until at last my elder sister breathed her last and was buried deep beneath the earth.'

'Together with two men, she met a wretched fate.' Hearing this, Kuroyume was struck with astonishment; carrying Oume upon her back, she rushed down to the roots of the camphor tree, and though she thought to remove the heavy ganseki with all her might, a woman's weak strength was of no avail, leaving her only to weep over her inability to save them. Just as she was at her wits' end, eight small dōji, about three shaku tall, appeared upon the spot; grabbing the heavy ganseki as if they were mere balls, they hurled them hither and thither, and immediately concealed their forms.

Undaunted she hurls reckless parting words, / striking out with stinging Kototama; / then Kanekō and Yosakō, the pair, / drawing close one after another, / address her with vexing words. / Ah, Kamunagara, Kamunagara! / This body entrusted to Kami, / how can a woman with a husband, / frightened by brute strength, / let Sankō have his way with her desire? / As she reviles him with the utmost scorn, / Sankō suddenly flies into a rage, / pressing forward right up before her, / glaring with a blackened face. / A man exuding a foul stench, / felt as though she were standing on her head, / choking her very breast. / The henchmen of Orochi, swarming in throngs, / advance to make an assault upon her. / At this moment comes Kanekō's sudden betrayal:

'That bold-hearted Oai-dono— / to have such an aneki as oyakata, / the henchmen who follow her are fortunate!' / Uttering this with a humorous look, / how comical as he casts a sideways glance! / Orochi, unable to contain it in his breast, / gives a winking signal to Yosakō: 'Bind them!' / Seizing the moment of Kanekō's unguarded slip, / Yosakō casts a rope upon him, / and pulling the neck taut, breaks into a run. / Even the mighty Kanekō is left quite helpless, / bound hand and foot in a pitiful state, / raising cries of weeping—how laughable! / The shadow of the sun gliding across the vast sky / has hidden beneath the horizon. / The voices of birds announcing the twilight, / hour by hour a sense of loneliness / drifting about—at that very moment, / heard close at hand is a propaganda song, / sung by Magokō of Anisakyō.

Appearing suddenly right here: / 'I am a revered propaganda envoy, / high-named in the capital of the Land of Fire, / Takayamahiko,' he boasts proudly, / thinking to crush and shatter / the fierce courage of everyone present. / But his prompt cleverness proves to be mere water bubbles; / with blinding sand hurled straight / into both of his eyes, pitifully indeed, / Magokō crouches right down on the spot. / Without a moment's loss of time, Yosakō / throws a rope around Magokō's neck, / heaves it across his shoulder two or three paces, / and drags him along with all his might. / Magokō, whose faith is strong enough / that he should endure through anything, / finds his body bound fast in a web, / suffering and writhing—how pitiful! / Sankō of Orochi, looking on coldly at the three, / mocks and laughs with a sneer,

giving orders to his henchmen, / they instantly dig pits in the ground, / and cast the three men and women right in / while still bound fast in rough hemp ropes. / Daring to commit a deed of lawless atrocity, / a burial alive too dreadful to look upon, / they heap earth over them to make a grave, / carry heavy stones from afar to pile upon it, / and raising cries of victory, they turn away. / Ah, Kamunagara, Kamunagara! / With the deep protection of Kami, / little sister Oume, safe and sound, / successfully crossed the perilous pass / of nine deaths and one life without suffering, / hiding her form in the shadow of the trees—how wise! / The three of us, buried in the cold earth of rough iron, / in the agony of being unable to move hand or foot,

while grieving and troubled for the path ahead, / praying to heaven and earth, and at the very moment / of praying for the safety of my dear husband, / Kurohime no Tsukasa appeared, / and together with little sister Oume, / joining their forces, they graciously rescued / the three of us without the least trouble. / How deeply grateful is that divine grace! / Weeping tears of gratitude, following Kurohime no Tsukasa, / led by the hand of little sister Oume, / along the tree-shaded path overgrown with weeds, / at the very moment of our safe return, / oh, the joy when my lord and husband, Lord Tora, / led by Lord Tama and accompanied / by the henchmen Shin, Kyū, and Hachi, / came hastening to us! / Ah, Kamunagara, Kamunagara! / Blessed by the grace of Kami, / here our party of nine together
