小杉の森〔680〕

§ 1

In the Ihaya of Takaharuyama, the Magakami Takayorihime, who had built her nest there, was to be brought to submission to the Mioshihe of the true Kami; the Sendenshi of the Ananahi path, Takahime and Kurohime, boarded the Ama-Ihakusubune and, with the spirit of Ikishiyōten, left Takaamahara behind and flew high into the Tenkū.

Three months passed in the winter sky, and with no news whatsoever, the Kamuzukasa Kotoyoriwake secretly instructed the three pillars, Tatsukuniwake, Kuniyoriwake, and Tamaharuwake, and sent them toward Takaharuyama.

Here, the three Sendenshi, in the attire of Warajikiyahan and Minokasa, in a light appearance, spent a night at the Kamudachi of Ume-teruhiko of Kameyama, which celebrates the Yorozuyo, and worshipped at the Itsunoiwaya of Takakuma, listening to the words of the Kami.

Sent off by Kimodome-no-Kami, they crossed the great mountain pass of good and evil, right and wrong, and finally climbed to the side of Togakure-iwa in Hōkidani, only to see, to their surprise, four or five suspicious shadows appearing in their path.

Tamaharuwake-tsukasa identified the group as mountain bandits, and with quick wit, he claimed they were the right-hand men of the Mukade-hime of Mikunidake, which suddenly changed the situation; though the bandits took off their helmets for a moment, they were fundamentally twisted Magamatsukami.

They stopped by the house of Mokusuke, a woodcutter in a hut at the bottom of the valley of Yutani-ga-tōge, and their eyes were dazzled by the light of a bundle of gold and silver.

Once again, blocked by the demons in their hearts, they reversed back to the path of the devil, and the six of them secretly exchanged glances, following Tatsukuniwake, pretending to know nothing until they reached the shore of the lake of Tsuda, finally arriving at the lakeside together with the three Tsukasa.

Sanshū: 'Tamaharuwake-sama, you claim to be a Sendenshi of the Ananahi-oshihe, but there is no mistake that you are actually Tamakō, the foster child of Mukade-hime, is there?'

Tamaharuwake: 'It is troublesome for you to say such foolish things. Why on earth do you see me as such an evil Kami?'

Sanshū: 'Evidence speaks louder than argument; you used the foster child of a thief to sneak into Mokusuke's house, stole a large amount of gold and silver, and you waited in ambush at Akago-iwa to receive it from the foster child, did you not? Even if you say you did not steal it directly, you had someone else steal it for you, so in short, you are the mastermind behind this robbery case.'

Tamaharuwake: 'Are you still saying such things even now? My innocence has already been cleared by Mokusuke and many others, has it not?'

Sanshū: 'Even so, at the foot of Togakure-iwa, you confessed that you were the right-hand man of Mukade-hime, did you not? Hey, Kōshū, Unshū, you are my witnesses.'

Kōshū: 'That is right. There is a saying that a frog confesses its own guilt through its own mouth. ...Hey, Tamashū, it is no use now. Since you said it with your own mouth, no matter what you say, Ryūshū and Kunishū, my observations cannot be wrong. Even if this staff I strike against the earth misses, my words shall not miss.'

The Sannin of Sanshiu, Kafushiu, and Unshiu left the Kohen of Tsuda behind and arrived at the Sanroku of Yuyagadake. Here stood a small, nominal shrine dedicated to Sukunahikonanokami. A great Taiboku of oak, though half-withered and reduced to mere bark, put forth Chiumitsu leaves from its young branches, sealing off the sky. The cries of monkeys could be heard, 'kyatsu-kyatsu,' in the thicket of trees behind the shrine.
