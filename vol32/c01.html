Chapter 1: 万物同言 [892]

万物同言〔892〕

§ 1

Kuniharutachinōokami appeared on Chijiyō once again, and established the Mirokushin of Shijin and Shiai, the world of Kami of Shinzenbi, as Kakihatokiha. While waiting for the time, keeping the Shinpau of His Keirin, the Uzudakara respected as such, the Kongaufue Niyoihotsushiyu, the Murasakiiro Reigiyoku, and the Kogane tama in the Seichi of Ananahi, Kurohime of the Nishiki no Miya lost the Kogane Uzudakara which she had kept in her custody. Kurohime was then banished by Takahime, a Mitama of the Hitsupō who calls herself Hinode no Kami, and crossed over to Riugūjima, an island in Oceania.

It is pitiful to see her advancing to search for the whereabouts of the tama. Takayorihime, who opened the Alps religion, also could not help but receive the suspicion of Takahime, and accompanied by Riukokuwake, she floated far across the waves, searching through many islands. Though she ran about and searched even to the ends of Kokoro-Tsukushi, the whereabouts remained like white waves. Crossing over by boat, at last, to the port of Asahi where the waves rise high like Takasago, she added Terisutan and Kārinsu, making a party of four. Gradually they arrived safely here, and marched south along the Teru-koku highway, passing over Takatori-mura.

Near the Arina waterfall, in a cavern of Kagami no Ike, the party of four set up their dwelling. Squeezing out strange knowledge, starting from Teru no Kuni, without distinction of night or day, even to the ends of Hiru no Kuni, Karu no Kuni, and Brazil-koku, if there was anything named tama in the divine spirit of Kagami no Ike, they offered it without regret or concealment, and advertised that one should quickly receive the Kōdai-muhen divine virtue. That effect was not in vain, and the sacred ground of Kagami no Ike was piled up like a mountain with various kinds of miscellaneous tama. Nevertheless, the Shinpau of the Kogane tama that Takayorihime was seeking did not appear easily.

The tension in the hearts of the two, Teru and Kā, loosened before they knew it, and they became weary. 'Ah, Kannagara-kamigami, does it not meet the heart of Kami?' Takayorihime proposed to her teacher that they should abandon this country as soon as possible, select a pure sacred land in Haru no Kuni, and set about collecting the tama again. While she was worrying her heart with various miscellaneous matters, the chieftain of Tena no Sato brought the gold tama, a secret treasure of his house, on a portable shrine and offered it, dedicating the sincerity of Ananahi before the Kami.

The party of Takayorihime immediately took the tama, and under the cover of night, climbed up Mount Arina and fled to the wilderness of Uzu no Kuni. Being severely punished by the incarnation of Kami for their non-action, they finally repented, and the party of four advanced pointing toward the east. Departing from the port of Aru, while encountering a hundred hardships, they drifted to the port of Zemu, headed toward Mount Tenshō, saved the harm of Mōrubando, and from the port of Chin, built an Iwakusu-bune, went up the Amazon River, and sought to pacify the Yamata no Orochi and the ugly demons lurking in the Shigure no Mori, illuminating the grace of the Ananahi Kami, they advanced with their hearts determined.

The Amazon River, with its wide river width that the eye cannot reach, its deep, murky, and turbulent current, and its spray flying, flows freely with a force that fears no suppression of anything; the party of Takayorihime finally arrived safely at its mouth, letting the wind fill their sails.

Here, a monster called Mōrubando, an amphibious animal, holds sway as the king of all wild beasts. It has a long body as if fourteen or fifteen elephants were gathered together, and like the reptile family, it has webbed feet at the ends of its four legs, and its claws are seven or eight shaku long, shining and sharp like swords. Its head is like a crocodile, its mouth is extremely large, and it has horns like a deer, from the tips of which it is always spewing something like smoke.

Its eyes are always bloodshot, and its tail is like that of a lizard, which can be extended up to forty or fifty ken as needed. Furthermore, at the tip of its tail, it carries a lethal weapon like a sharp double-edged sword. Against this Mōrubando, any great serpent or wild beast fears and trembles, hiding its figure deep in the forest, trying to escape the harm of the Mōrubando.

§ 2

Furthermore, in this Amazon River, many Chiyōdai snakes reside, and at times they hide in the forests or at the bottom of the water, waiting to swallow beasts, and if any Hitobito happen to wander into this location, they compete to emerge and lie in wait to swallow and devour them.

Also, there is another animal called the Erubando, which is neither a crocodile nor a Chiyōdai snake; its scales are as hard as iron, it has whiskers like a dragon, and it possesses four legs. The head of the Erubando is round like a jewel, and its eyes are relatively small. The Erubando is a Kuwaibutsu that, when necessary, extends its round Tōbu thinly and long to suck the blood of animals and survive.

When the Morubando devours a Maujiu, it glares at its enemy in a Danseiteki manner, swings its Shitsupo, strikes the enemy with the sword at the Bitan, cuts it to death, and thereafter puts it into its own stomach. Conversely, the Erubando acts in a manner that is extremely Jiyoseiteki; it considers the moments when animals are sleeping, extends its soft, octopus-like head as thinly and long as it needs to, applies the tip of its tongue or mouth to the animal's Kōmon, and is a terrifying animal that extracts fresh Ikichi. However, it is said that for both the Morubando and the Erubando, their most feared enemy is none other than the schools of Kiyodai crocodiles that inhabit the banks of the Amazon River.

Takayorihime and her Itsukau arrived at the Kahaguchi of the Amazon River where such Kuwaibutsu reside, and because the Kiken was pressing upon them moment by moment, they abandoned their boat, landed on the southern bank of the Amazon River, and while singing songs of propaganda toward the Shigure Forest, they proceeded westward, weaving through the shade of trees, accompanied by Ryūkokubake, Tērisutan, and Kārinsu.

All things in the universe are formed by the water and fire of Yin and Yang, without exception. Therefore, it goes without saying for divine beings, but even down to birds, beasts, insects, fish, mountains, rivers, grasses, and trees, all possess the water and fire of Kototama, and there is nothing that does not emit language. In the Nakatomi no Harae, there is also the phrase 'even the single leaf of grass is stopped by words, and so on,' and the people of old were able to fully understand even the language of birds, beasts, grasses, and trees.

Furthermore, birds and beasts understand human language, and humans were able to understand the language of birds, beasts, grasses, and trees well. However, as the world gradually declined, the hearts of humans became twisted and crooked, eventually becoming lumps of sin and evil, and they fell into the state of being extremely inconvenient humans who could not understand any language at all. Also, even among the same humans, the differences in language between countries and places are primarily due to the relationship of the spirits of the national guardian gods and the relationship of the climate and weather, but human ears have become so dull that they cannot hear them one by one and grasp their meaning. Fortunately, when one masters the study of Kototama, one should be able to grasp meanings through the color of the sounds as well as their intonation and cadence, without having to study narrow-minded linguistics such as English, French, or Russian. Therefore, the languages of birds, beasts, insects, fish, mountains, rivers, grasses, and trees should also be easily understood.

For example, when one plays a shamisen, which is an inanimate object, to produce various musical scales and makes it 'speak' in accompaniment to jōruri or songs, an ear accustomed to it can distinguish what the sound is saying. Just as other instruments such as vertical flutes, horizontal flutes, shō, hichiriki, drums, and hand drums achieve their purpose with merely five, or seven or eight notes, when we purify our ears and listen, we can clearly grasp the voices of birds, beasts, insects, fish, grasses, and trees. For example, a trumpet uses only the five sounds of ta-chi-tsu-te-to to move a large army, a shamisen uses the ten sounds of pa-pi-pu-pe-po and ta-chi-tsu-te-to to say everything, and cows use ma-mi-mu-me-mo, horses use ha-hi-fu-he-ho, cats use na-ni-nu-ne-no, and dogs use wa-wi-u-e-wo; each uses specific Kototama to express their intentions completely, and where that is not enough, they supplement it by working their eyes and using the form of their bodies, and furthermore, they clearly express their intentions through the intonation and cadence of their voices.

§ 3

Nihonjin are a superior race capable of fully utilizing the seventy-five sounds of Enman and Seirau. This is precisely why the Kunitama is excellent, and it is also the reason why they are a superior race. Depending on the race, there are those who cannot use language beyond twenty-four, twenty-five, or around thirty sounds. Furthermore, their Seion are mixed with Euon, Dakuon, Bion, Handakuon, and Jiuchikuon. However, even for a race capable of using the seventy-five sounds like the Kami, their hearts' ears are now completely blocked and their hearts' eyes are closed; thus, they lack the sharp heart-ears and eyes that allow one to understand ten things by hearing one, as stated in the Shinyu. Their efficiency in utilizing their ears and eyes has declined to the point where they can barely understand one or two things after hearing a hundred, reaching a state where they are almost indistinguishable from other animals. Is this not truly a matter of great lamentation for humans, who are the administrators of the governance of heaven and earth?

Takayorihime, Ryūkokuwake, Tē, and Kā, these four, advanced into this forest, heard the voices of tigers, bears, lions, wolves, great serpents, eagles, foxes, rabbits, and all other animals, and were well able to understand their meanings. Here, they came to employ the wild beasts, became kings of the forest, and engaged in the conquest of Mōrubando and Erubando. However, the people of ancient times did not use such overly clever and smooth rhetoric as modern people do, nor was there any need to line up tens of thousands of words and chatter incessantly. Therefore, they were able to exchange their intentions well with birds, beasts, plants, and trees through the breath of 'a' and 'u'.

However, modern people lack the ability to understand intentions through such simple language. Therefore, in order to make this oral account easy for the ears of modern people to understand, I intend to translate and narrate it as if the great serpents, bears, deer, tigers, wolves, foxes, rabbits, and even other birds had spoken in the language of modern humans. Just as there is a divine text in the Kojiki where Ōnamuchi-no-Mikoto meets the white rabbit of Inaba at the cape of Keta and engages in various questions and answers, even the voices of birds, beasts, plants, and trees can be well understood if one returns to the true Kunitama of the Kami.

Up to this day, birds, beasts, plants, and trees vaguely understand human language, but where is the authority of humans as the primates of all creation if they cannot hear the voices of birds, beasts, plants, and trees? With such incomplete five senses, how can they provide rest to all things as living shrines of the Kami and fulfill their duty as administrators of the governance of heaven and earth? When I think of it, when I think of it, it is truly the height of regret.

The narrator has never learned foreign languages, nor can he understand a single character of horizontal script. However, when I am nearby and listen intently to the conversations of people from various countries, I can understand what their intentions are based on the principles of Kototama. This story also lists the languages of ancient natives in some places, and in some places, it uses modern foreign languages. However, because the world's linguistics today disrupts its principles based on the study of Kototama, there are many points in modern human language that are difficult to understand. Insofar as the language of any country does not err from the principles of Kototama, just as the birds, beasts, plants, and trees of today do not, it is the marvelous application of Kototama and the truth of the universe that it should be universally understood throughout the world. Ah, Kannagara Tamachi Haemaese.
