Chapter 39: Discourses by the Shore [239]

第39章　海辺の雑話〔239〕

§ 1

Standing upon a beach bordered by a high mountain to the west and the boundless sea stretching ten thousand ri to the east, four or five men lingered, absorbed in idle talk as they watched the raging billows that rose like mountains.

Kou said, "Ah, has not the world grown strange? Look at those waves. One can hardly tell whether they are sea or mountain. Recently, someone calling himself a missionary arrived and took our breath away by shouting in a loud voice that the sea would turn into mountains and the mountains into sea. Yet we sneered, saying, 'Nonsense, how could this deep sea ever turn into a mountain?' Furthermore, such billows have never been seen since my grandfather's day. The other day, too, the missionary shouted that a tsunami would surge up to the mountainside of Mount Takoma. This vicinity may also be swept away by a tsunami before long. Why do you not also consider it and evacuate to the summit of Mount Takoma or perhaps to Mount Chikyō?"

So he said with a tilted head and a thoughtful look. Otsu, with a sneer, said:

"What! How could such absurd nonsense ever happen? That fellow called a missionary from the other day was mad; he blustered about stars falling, floods rising, and mankind being reduced to a third, boasting utter nonsense without sense, rejoicing only in frightening us. There is no god in this world, nor could such a vast upheaval ever take place; if by any remote chance such a thing occurred, would it not violate all ordinary order? If all the people of this world died and only twenty or thirty percent survived, it would be unbearably lonely. Do not say such things; rather, what the missionary who came before that one said was far more clever."

Hei asked, "You say clever—what did he say?"

Otsu answered, "Whatever he said, it was something truly wonderful. If anything can be called a heavenly gospel, it must be something like that."

Kou said, "What do you mean by a heavenly gospel? Surely it must be things like 'The plum blossoms blooming at once across the three thousand worlds,' or 'Though the earth should sink, the true God saves the world.'"

Hei said, "Do not talk nonsense; this heaven and earth came into being naturally. Rain falling, wind blowing, waves swelling, and tsunamis are all matters of the seasons. This world is called the floating world because it floats upon the water. We do not care at all for such petty, fear-inducing sayings as 'though the earth should sink.' When we hear such a song, our heads throb, and we become more infuriated than by those waves out there. The gospel we heard is no such petty, rotten claptrap meant to scare fools. It is an incomparable, matchless, and utterly extraordinary gospel. Well, perhaps I should keep such an important matter to myself. If I told you, it would be troublesome if you were shocked into a fit of convulsions."

§ 2

Otsu: "What is this? What you say makes no sense at all. Grandly making a show of hearsay as heavenly tidings (fukuin)—it is probably no more than blowing big boasts (fukuin), or at best the sound of foaming at the mouth (fukuin) in an epileptic fit."

Hei: "Be silent and listen. Even if the earth sinks, the power of an adulterer saves the world. If I say such things before cowards and faint-hearts, our divine favor might run out and heaven's punishment might strike. As it would rather cause you trouble, perhaps I had better stop."

Tei: "Do not put on such airs; it is just cheap theatrics and all talk. This fellow, the other day his own little son fell into a well. At that time he panicked, instantly clasped his hands and blabbered, 'Sun god, Sun god,' prostrating and praying with a tearful face. In the meantime, the little son bubbled up foam and sank away. Then he fell into desperate despair, ranting and complaining, 'Is there any such thing as God or shit in this world? Omniscient and omnipotent God indeed—my buttocks are dumbfounded, the privy dances, and the chamber pot beckons come hither!'"

Hei: "Do not say unnecessary things! Exposing even another's faults in such a place—how was it when your own wife died? Unmanly as you were, toward a creature that had turned cold, stretched out stiff, and hardened like stone, you cried, 'Hey, wife! Leaving me behind, why did you die first? Call me husband once more!'—such a blockhead you are to tell a dead corpse to speak!"

Tei: "Talk sense! My wife is a deity. She is of a different breed from your wife, who has a strange face like a yawning lizard. Even after dying, night after night she comes to my bedside to look after me; she is truly kind. And she wears splendid attire as though a heavenly maiden had descended."

Hei: "Go wash your face once! That is an illusion. You became utterly infatuated with an ugly woman; every single day while she breathed, you revered her as the Great Bright Deity Wife, cowering timorously morning and evening, drooling over your wife's serving—such a simpleton you are!"

The entire company rolled with laughter. At this moment, the roaring of the sea grew increasingly violent, and the waves rushed up to their feet. Crying out that this was dire, with pale faces, they ran about one chō up the mountain.

Tei: "You spouted nothing but arrogant, brazen-faced talk, but what a sight you are! Turning pale as a sheet just because a wave came, and running up the mountain almost completely giving way at the waist—how utterly disgraceful! You are an unbearable sight; looking like that and speaking of heavenly good tidings—even the good tidings would be dumbfounded, dumbfounded as Kishimojin of Iriya! Without wasting any more time fooling around, reporting that so-called epileptic froth-blowing sound before all of us in awe and trembling is for the sake of your afterlife!"

Hei: "Speaking of that afterlife, I remembered: just the other day, someone crying out that he was a missionary of Uralhiko had an attendant carry a five-shō cask and came shouting down the main street. That is it—what they call the good tidings of the kingdom of heaven."

Tei: "What are you talking about? Chattering on and on with nothing but prefaces, it is probably just about guzzling sake. Well, as for their good tidings, showing them a cask is quite enough. Gathering a mouth full of spit, blowing froth like a crab, parching their throats impatiently, wearing a pitiful expression like a hungry ghost unable to drink the water it desires, following behind that missionary, walking along merely twitching their noses just like a dog trailing a wild boar—that is how the story goes. Their idea of good tidings is catching the scent of sake, being unable even to drink properly, sucking their fingers enviously, tagging behind the missionary's smelly behind, and while the missionary went into the privy, popping out the bung of the cask, putting a long tongue inside the cask to lick slobberingly, only to be spotted from the privy window by the missionary, apologizing profusely, and as compensation, that foul fellow had the privilege of wiping that splendid, beautiful posterior—or so the rumor went. It must surely be about this fellow. Not the good tidings of the kingdom of heaven, but the butt-wiping sound of an excrement-dumping fellow. How utterly absurd; even dung would be appalled."

Hei clenched his fists and, with a sullen look on his face,

"You have quite the cheekbones, do you not?"

Tei: "Paulownia clogs are better than cheekbones; do not misunderstand me so badly."

Hei: "The misunderstanding is yours! Trying to trample someone underfoot with another person's clogs, and with muddy feet spouting robber-like talk about the three thousand worlds being an ocean of mud—how ridiculous! Rather than that, if you drank muddy water instead of sake, perhaps you could hear a bit of heavenly good tidings.

Drink and make merry, for an inch ahead lies darkness; After the darkness, the moon will rise.

Yo-itosa, yo-itosa!"

Tei: "Who would ever dance, pretending to have drunk sake when there is no sake at all?"

Hei: "Quit your carping! Go home quickly and meet the ghost of your wife or whatever; leave me alone!"

Just as they rolled up their sleeves and began to grapple with each other, from far ahead, the three missionaries chanted:

"Plum blossoms opening at once across the three thousand worlds, Opening, scattering, and bearing fruit."

Thus they came singing. Hei immediately shut his eyes, grimaced, and holding his head with both hands,

"This is unbearable!"

and crouched down onto the ground.

Just at that moment, the violent storm grew ever fiercer, and waves surged beneath their feet. Striving to be first, the whole party once again fled toward the top of the mountain.

丁『何の事だい、べらべらと序文ばつかり並べよつて、おほかた酒を喰ふことだらう。まあこいつらの福音といふのは樽さへ見せたらよいのだ。口に唾一ぱい溜めよつて、蟹のやうな泡をふきよつてな、喉をぢりぢり焦げつかして、餓鬼が飲みたい水を飲まれぬ時のやうな憐れな面付をして、その宣伝使の後から跟きまはつて、犬が猪の後をつけるやうに鼻ばかりぴこつかして歩いていつたということだ。こいつ等の福音といふことは、酒の匂ひを嗅ぎつけて、よう飲みもせず、けなりさうに指をくはへて、宣伝使の臭い尻からついて歩きよつて、宣伝使が厠へでも這入つてゐるまに、樽のつめをポンと抜いて、長い舌を樽の中へ入れべそべそやつて居ると、雪隠の窓から宣伝使に見つけられて平謝りに謝つて、その代償として立派な美しいお尻を拭かしてもらつた臭い奴があるといふ評判だつた。大方こいつ等のことだらうよ。天国の福音でなくつて糞放の尻拭音だよ。馬鹿々々しい、糞が呆れらあ』

丙は拳を握り、むつとした顔付きで、

『貴様アよい頬桁だなあ』

丁『頬桁より桐下駄がよいのだ、あまり穿きちがひするなよ』

丙『穿きちがひは貴様のこつた、人の下駄で人を踏みつけやうとしよつて、泥足で三千世界泥の海なんて、泥棒の言ひ草みたいなことを吐してな、馬鹿らしい、それよりも酒の代りに泥水でも飲んだら、ちつと天来の福音が聞けるだらう。

飲めよ騒げよ一寸先や暗よ 暗のあとには月が出る

ヨイトサ、ヨイトサ』

丁『酒もないのに酒を飲んだ気になりよつて、踊る奴があるものかい』

丙『ごてごていふない、早う帰つて嬶の幽霊になと会つてこい、かまふない』

とたがひに腕を捲りあげ格闘を初めたとたんに、はるか前方より三柱の宣伝使は、

『三千世界一度に開く梅の花、開いて散りて実を結ぶ』

と謡つてくる。丙は矢庭に眼を塞ぎ、顔を顰め、両手に頭を抑へながら、

『こいつはたまらぬ』

と大地にしやがんだ。
