Chapter 44: The Head Shining in the Night [244]

第44章　夜光の頭〔244〕

§ 1

With the mountain wind blowing down from the Rocky Mountains, the wind blowing the world to the northeast, the surface of Lake Superior immediately raised raging waves and tossed the small boat back and forth, left and right. Here, Sukunahikona-no-Kami drifted along with numerous deities. The wind roared incessantly and grew violent. The sky was barred with dark clouds on all sides, and the forms of sea monsters appearing and disappearing endlessly in front of and behind the boat were indeed a dreadful sight. Sukunahikona-no-Kami immediately presented a ritual prayer, and with a clear, refreshing voice,

"Though the morning sun shines or clouds over, Though the moon waxes or wanes, Though the waves of Lake Superior are high And our boat may capsize, That which changes not is the divine heart. Holding the divine heart in our chests, Blowing away the surging, raging waves With the mist of the breath of kotodama, The divine way of clearing and purifying. Waves, subside! Wind, calm down! Yet, yet, the evil spirits of perpetual darkness, Whose hearts are dark, Tremble and shake, their facial colors Like earth and the whirlpools of Naruto. Ah, calm, calm, calm, Shinadohiko! Wind of Shinado, if you blow Eternally, then blow! After we have crossed this lake water safely, Blow forth with a roar! Do not blow now, god of fortune / blowing wind!

Do not blow now, god of fortune / blowing wind!"

When he chanted this toward the storm, wondrously, at the same moment this voice ceased, even such a storm stopped completely, the waves suddenly grew calm, and the surface of the waters returned to a peaceful state as though mats had been laid out completely across it.

The people of Tokoyo-no-Kuni, who wore facial expressions like green gourds cowering from the cold, also suddenly felt as if they had revived, and with one voice, amidst tears of joy, gave thanks to Sukunahikona-no-Kami.

Sukunahikona-no-Kami chanted the customary missionary song with an intriguing melody. Among the deities, an exceptionally robust, dark-complexioned, tall man, pouting his lips, turned toward Sukunahikona-no-Kami and,

"Which missionary are you? To the power of your kotodama, both the wind and the sea have entirely submitted. We beseech you, let us hear your name,"

said. Sukunahikona-no-Kami,

"I am indeed Sukunahikona-no-Kami, having little of the infinite desires like an ordinary mortal toward the treasures of this world,"

introduced himself by lining up many pillow-words, and thereupon gradually began to expound the virtues of the great deity.

"In all this vast and boundless universe, there exists a single great deity, Ōkunitokotachi-no-Ōkami, who possesses infinite, absolute, beginningless, and endless omniscience and omnipotence, and who created all things. He divided His purest and most refined divine spirit into thousands upon thousands, bringing forth the deities of seas, rivers, mountains, and plains. Therefore, in this world, there is not even an inch of space where God does not reside. Unless we praise God and rely upon God, we cannot survive in this world even for a brief moment. Even the breath we now breathe is entirely God's breath and never our own; down to the lowest insect, there is none that does not receive God's mercy. Therefore, between heaven and earth, that which ought most to be revered, felt close to, believed in, and loved is none other than the venerableness of the sole true God, the Creator of the world, Ōkunitokotachi-no-Mikoto. The deities of seas, rivers, mountains, and plains who were branched off and born from the holy spirit of this great deity must also be respected; in all matters, such weak ordinary mortals have no choice but to borrow the power of God."

§ 2

He thus instructed them. The deities in the boat opened their mouths respectively,

"If it is indeed as the missionary says, are there gods in the water of this Lake Superior as well now?"

they asked. Sukunahikona-no-Kami said,

"In the sea there is a god of the sea, in the river a god of the river, and in the boat there is a god of the boat. We must never defile the gods."

The boat advanced gradually and drew near to the western shore. The lights of the fishing fires flickering along the lakeside were like fireflies flitting about in the darkness of a summer night, an indescribable scene. The inside of the boat was wrapped in a curtain of darkness and was pitch-black.

At this time, a deity whose head shone brightly raised a wild voice and said,

"Oh, how cruel! Do not make a fool of me!"

In the shadow of the boat, even the sound of snickering could be heard.

The twentieth-day moon emerged from the edge of the eastern mountains and began to shine brightly. First of all, the bald head began to reflect light like a mirror. Looking closely, it was Zukutanbo. The bald head sharpened his voice,

"Thinking I would not know in the darkness, someone urinated on our head. Even in the dark, the eyes of a god are shining; this I cannot tolerate!"

he raged, his very eyeballs shining. A dark man who stood nearby, with a face like that of a roofer's assistant or a charcoal burner, stood up and

"Since I heard that in the sea there is a god of the sea, and in the boat there resides a god of the boat, what is wrong with splashing urine on a head that has no hair [kami]?"

he retorted back. Muttering to himself, the bald-headed man began to wash his gleaming head with the water of the lake. At last the boat reached the western shore. Sukunahikona-no-Kami, once again singing a missionary song, proceeded aimlessly toward the west, ever westward.
