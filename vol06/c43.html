Chapter 43: The Helping Paw [293]

第43章　猫の手〔293〕

§ 1

The evening bell echoing in the distant sound; the darkness beneath the trees under the fifth-month sky; a single cry of a cuckoo in the sky; a night journey wherein black and white cannot be distinguished. The missionary of Hiromichiwake-no-Uzu-no-Mikoto traveling to Rome, looking back at the sky of his home that weighs on his mind, parting the cloud-paths of falling rain, the missionary advances pointing toward the east. Cuckoo of blood-spitting grief, keep it dark in the shadows for a while; soon the face of the full moon of the fifteenth night shall appear; the capital of Rome, where Mototeruwake-no-Tsukasa dwells, draws near; madder-red tinges the eastern sky; the crows of dawn, crying out "Change, change!" as they fly back and forth high above their heads to the right and left, do they perhaps console the fatigue of the journey?

Those cries too are prone to be dampened by the fifth-month rain in the dawning sky, crying across "Ka-i, ka-i." Today, rarely, opening the curtain of rain clouds, the heavenly sun casts its tranquil light upon the earth, as if illuminating the path ahead. In the dew of the blessings of the Sun Deity who illuminates the path ahead, the sky of the inner heart also clears completely. "In this passing world there are no demons"; though under the sky of Rome demons, giant serpents, and wolves rage fiercely with violent fury, relying upon the divine grace of the Heavenly Deities, Earthly Deities, and Tsuku-no-Mihashira-no-Okami, subduing the crookedness that presses in, weaving the loom of damask and brocade of the warp and weft of the beautiful divine age.

Likened to the color of scarlet maple leaves glowing brightly in the morning and evening sun, the teachings of a hundred and a thousand looms woven by Haniyashiko-no-Mikoto and Haniyashihime-no-Mikoto, centered around the Riverbed of Heaven, are the divine hearts of Takuhatahime, Chijihime, and the pure-hearted Wakasakurashime-no-Kami-no-Mikoto. Spreading the teachings of the full moon of the fifteenth night without leaving any dark corner beneath the heavens to the countries of the four directions—the color of their hearts is truly beautiful, the flower of their hearts is truly fragrant.

Though the sky of the fifth-month rain had been wrapped in clouds with rain falling and stopping, today, unusually, the Deity of the Heavenly Sun revealed His round, warm countenance in the eastern sky, and cast burning-hot brilliance upon the lower world.

The two missionaries, sighing with relief while chanting the propagation song, spurred on their own legs and advanced, pointing toward Rome. This was a small village called Mitoshi Village on the outskirts of Rome, where some twenty or thirty small houses stood in a row.

Five or six young men of the village, spreading straw raincoats by the roadside and sitting down, were absorbed in idle chatter. It appeared to be in the midst of the rice-planting season; in the rural fields on the outskirts of the town, wearing the armor of straw raincoats and straw hats, equipping the gauntlets and greaves of arm-covers and gaiters, forming ranks in groups of three and five, while singing rice-planting songs to an entertaining melody, they were completely engrossed in planting rice.

As far as the eye could see, the silhouettes of oxen and horses plowing the paddies, and the sight of men and women of all ages moving busily about, were truly a scene that might be called the fierce battleground of farmers, where even a cat's paw is counted as a human hand.

降ったり降らなかったりして雲に包まれていた五月雨の空も、今日は珍しくも天つ日の神が東の空に丸く穏やかな顔を現し、下界に焼けるように熱い輝きを投げかけられた。

村の若者五、六人が、道端に蓑を敷いて腰掛けながら、雑談にふけっている。田植えの最中と見えて、町外れの田舎の田園では、蓑笠という甲冑を身に着け、手覆や脚絆という小手や脛当てを着け、三々五々隊列を整えて、調子よく田歌を歌いながら、田植えに余念がない様子である。

見渡す限り、牛や馬が田を鋤く姿や、老若男女があちこちに行き来して活動する様子は、実に「猫の手も人の手」という農家の激戦場の中とも言うべき光景であった。

§ 2

Kō: "Ah, though we work dripping with sweat and plant the rice fields, unable to sleep properly night or day, I wonder if a great flood will not come again like last year and wash it all away. If such a thing continues for two or three years, a farmer cannot bear it. When I think of such things, my arms grow languid, and the hoe in my hand seems ready to drop with weeping. There are those who say farming is an honest trade, but is there anything as unreliable as farming? Though it is hot, we weed and apply fertilizer, and just when we think fine rice plants have grown, leafhoppers breed. When it comes to the crucial harvest time, the target rice cannot be harvested and there is only straw. Isn't this truly what they call false joy? Even that would be tolerable if it were our own land, but all the harvested rice must be delivered to the place of the god Nozuchi, and after delivering it, what remains behind is nothing but straw and chaff. Ah, subtract the fire and ash remains; subtract the ash and fire remains. The calculation does not balance at all. I have grown sick of cutting earthworms and making frogs jump; it is not something that can be reconciled. Rounding off is also vexatious and troublesome nonsense. Those fellows in the capital of Rome say it is hot or cool, fanning themselves in luxury, babbling noisily like frogs in a paddy field, carefree, saying, 'Drink and make merry, for one inch ahead is darkness,' without shedding a single drop of sweat, devouring the rice we produced, complaining endlessly about whether the rice is delicious or tasteless, whether the husks are many or few. Furthermore, they make us clean up even the feces they drop, foul-mouthedly spouting arrogant complaints from their mouths, calling us dirt farmers, dirt farmers. Anyone who thinks of this would truly boil with rage. Any fellow who feels nothing at this is a snot-nosed, farting, weak, foolish, dung-dripping..."

Otsu: "Hey, you are a fellow who also spouts a great deal. One should not spout such complaints. Everyone resigns themselves to it as karma and endures. Even if a dirt farmer tries to become a city person, sit still, do good business, and imitate city folks, it is useless because he lacks wisdom. When a tadpole is small, it resembles a catfish, but as a few days pass and it grows bigger, arms and legs grow out and it turns into a frog. No matter what, the child of a frog is a frog. Rather than spouting such worthless nonsense, it is more profitable to work hard and even drop dung. For it becomes fertilizer. In any case, you fellows are dung-making machines that turn rice into dung. Humans eat rice and turn it into dung, give dung to the rice plants and produce rice, eat that rice again and turn it into dung; dung becomes rice, and rice becomes dung; it is a world of mutual karmic cycles. Even so, once you are buried beneath the turf and return anew, you will come to lead a splendid life like those fellows in the city."

§ 3

Hei said, "Is that so? Well, that is amusing. You have told me a good thing."

Saying this, quicker than gripping his large hoe, he tore up sod from the roadside, covered his head with it, and said,

Hei said, "Hey, I have put on turf and made a fresh start. What should I do after this to become like a city person? Will you not teach me?"

Otsu said, "Fool, to wear turf means to die."

Hei said, "Does dying mean putting on turf? That makes no sense at all."

Otsu said, "Well, never mind such matters. In this busy time for farmers, when even a cat's paw is counted as a human hand, this is no time for idle chatter. Do not worry about the future. Humans need only do today's duty today. Even if one wished to make tomorrow's weather rain or fair, it is not something that human beings can freely control. Everything is just as the gods do. The other day, too, an evangelist from what is called the Anaikyō came forth, gathered the peasants, and gave a difficult sermon. Among his words, there was just a single sentence that impressed me. If we dirt-farmers do not have that heart, we cannot fulfill this present day. Truly, he is an evangelist; he said a noble thing."

Kō asked, "What kind of thing did he say?"

Otsu said, "The secret of heaven must not be leaked. It would be troublesome if it rained again. During the feast-holiday after rice planting, I shall tell you at leisure."

Kō said, "Even if you say just a word now, it will not hinder our work, will it? In the plight of frail humans who cannot know what lies an inch ahead, how could there even be a post-planting holiday? Even while we speak, the wind of impermanence is lurking around us, targeting us. If that wind blows softly from nowhere, that is the end, leading to peaceful extinction and sudden enlightenment. Speak without putting on airs. It is for the sake of the afterlife."

Otsu said, "Well then, I shall tell you. I am an evangelist."

Kō said, "O sudden evangelist, flinger of earthworms and slasher of frogs, shitter and snot-nosed one, skilled only in wagging your jaw, hating farming, and being an evangelist by not even an inch..."

Otsu said, "Do not spew unnecessary things, you snot-nosed fellow. In the first place, the doctrine of the Anaikyō is the Imperial Ancestor Religion."

Kō said, "What is the Imperial Ancestor Religion? If you blabber such things, look out, you will be reported to the police for lèse-majesté."

Otsu said, "Just listen to the rest. Is this brat an earthworm, an anglerfish, a louse, or a flea? / Both today and tomorrow, he merely drops his dung."

Kō said, "Fool, what are you babbling? You misheard it; indeed, the son of a frog-slasher is himself a frog-slasher. What a hopeless fellow. I shall recite it. Ahem. Though I know not if this autumn brings flood or storm, / as my duty for today, I weed the paddy. Tomorrow does not matter. The evangelist blabbers that one should do today's business today. What an unreliable evangelist. I have become sick of it. Ah, shall I work up another sweat?"

And Kō stood up. Following him, four or five young men also donned their straw coats and hats, and rustled into the paddy field.

"The deity appears manifest in the world, / and divides the good from the evil."

Crying out thus in loud voices, two evangelists came marching toward them.
