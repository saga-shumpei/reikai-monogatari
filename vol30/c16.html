Chapter 16: 荒しの Forest [858]

荒しの森〔858〕

§ 1

Crowned with light clouds upon its head, / drawing a sash of mist around its waist, / standing kitsuzen amidst the great mountain range (daisanmiyaku) / at the border between Hiru and Teru, / clothed in garments of green leaves, / the mountain stream at the foot of Mount Mikurayama / plays a natural koto with a sōsō sound, / and cool breezes constantly waft fresh air. / Yet even in this fairyland (senkiyō) of the heavenly Pure Land (tengoku jiyōdo), / for a hundred days and a hundred nights neither rain falls nor wind blows, / the tips of the trees wither / and are just like dead leaves. / Ah, what heavenly chastisement is this? / Has it issued forth from the wrath of Kunitamagami? / Like sinking into truly terrifying cruelty, / the voices of the countrymen's sorrow: / in the morning parting in death from fathers, / in the evening sending mothers to the unseen realm; / infants cling to the milk of parched, compassionate mothers / and cry out in grief. / Ah, between heaven and earth, / do the Kami exist or do they not?

The valley water of Mikurayama is clear, / fish swim briskly through the torrent; / while people, unaware even in dreams of this painful and tragic state, / humans born as the children of Kami, / wrapped in the curtain of famine, / wander down the alleys of the realm of hungry ghosts, / suffering this pitiful plight. / Humans suffer from hunger, / while fish frolic in the ocean-like, clear stream. / What on earth is this heavenly intent? / Ah, this contradiction, ah, this misery! / Look upon this, ye Kami of heaven and earth, / did the countrymen pray in vain, / gazing up at the empty sky and bowing down to the earth, / morning and night protecting and failing to protect us with the Kunitamagami? / Now folding their hands and closing their eyes, / rather than leaving themselves to the hands of death / creeping forth from the land of Yomi, / this misery has no remedy left. / The countrymen, without distinction of old and young, men and women, / pointing toward the shrine of Kunitama, / leaving their hearths behind, Ryūseihime / kneels at the edge of the valley stream, / her voice growing faint like the singing of insects / swarming in the autumn fields. / Just as the unbearable curtain of tragedy / was cut down and lowered, / watching for the time and seizing the gap, / waiting and waiting, the Ural religion's / Kami ministers, Būru, / Anan, and Yūzu, three men, / together with two or three companions outside, / suddenly appeared in this place / and preached the doctrine of making light of life and heavying death.

Yet, yet, the people / now weep before their eyes in hunger, / and even the thread-jewel of life is about to snap. / Now at this very time, at this juncture, / no matter how precious the teachings of the Kami may be, / how can they forsake bread / and affirm the loving heart of the Kami? / The darkness of negation makes the air of the valley stream murky and dreadful. / Ah, the heavenly way does not slay men, / ninety-nine out of a hundred lives / are about to snap their swift thread-jewel of life. / At that very moment, / the Kami minister filled with benevolence and mercy, / the Great Preacher Kotoyoriwake, / accompanied by Kuniyoriwake, / descended like a dove. / Ah, death or life, the heart of the Great Kami, / within the breasts of old and young, men and women, / instead of the sound of the valley stream's waters, / the pounding of hearts, / the splashing of joy, the waves of sorrow, / the surging and falling waterfall's rapids, / the tears of negation are pitiful.

The Kami minister of the Ural religion / wields impassioned eloquence, / spraying spittle from the corners of his mouth, / desperately proclaiming the gospel of the heavenly kingdom. / Kami is spirit, and man is not one who lives by bread alone. / Ah, spiritual indeed, spiritual indeed! / Ye who thirst for the water of life, / live in the true, pure water of the spirit / that never thirsts and never runs dry. / The heavenly kingdom is thine. / In a dark world where the three great calamities and three small calamities / come in turns, / bid farewell to this world and seek to be saved now / in the august realm of the Kami; / the hand of judgment has been lowered. / Ah, ye who live in the loving mercy of Kami, / do not set your hearts / upon the turbid netherworld, he teaches. / Ah, what a tragedy, what cruelty! / Just as man is not one who lives by bread alone, / so too is man's body / not one that lives by spirit alone. / Spirit and flesh are like yin and yang, / like husband and wife. / The Ural religion places undue emphasis on the spirit, / falls into the heavens, and suffers in the Kami. / Preaching the doctrine of the unity of the manifest and the hidden, / and the common roots of spirit and flesh, / first saving the torments of the flesh, / and then saving the spirit. / The Izumo teaching is precisely the saving truth of salvation, / appearing as the Mizu no Mitama.

The loving words of the Kami minister Kotoyoriwake / and Kuniyoriwake, / through tears of compassion, revive the parched people; / their flesh prospers, their spirits smile, / and even upon the soil parched like a withered moor, like hell, / moistened by the waters of this valley stream, / their flesh is satiated, / and suddenly hell is transformed into a heavenly kingdom. / The Kami is not necessarily distant, / nor is He on high; / the joy of the heavenly kingdom is before our eyes, / it is within our bodies, / within our hearts the flowers of spiritual delight blossom, / and the waters of rapture gush forth eternally. / Ah, what divine love is this, / what divine grace! / The tears of compassion of Kotoyoriwake / illuminate the dark gloom of heaven and earth, / and Kuniyoriwake's red heart, thoughtful of the world, / dyes all creation / in the light of the heavenly sun. / Ah, Izanagi-Izanami, how wondrous, how wondrous!

§ 2

Gazing upon Tengoku before one's eyes, enjoying Jiyōdo. Is the Ananai-kyō good, or is the Ural-kyō good? Seeking to live in the spirit, one dies in the body; seeking to live in the body, one dies in the spirit. How could the Kami of Tenchi overlook such Hisan?

Preaching the Shintai of Reinikuitsuchi and the unity of Keniu, where spirit and flesh live and thrive as one. The threads manipulated by Takuhatahime, who weaves the Ayanishiki of warp and woof, are very long; and forever, the Eikuō and grace of Kami are born as the Onko of Kami and appear as the Ikimiya of Kami.

May it descend upon Hitobito. Ah, Kamunagara, Kamunagara. Prostrating before the Mimahe of Kami, I pray for the Yobito of the world yet to come.

Kuniyoriwake preached and taught the teachings of Kami in the Keikan of Mikurayama, saving the lives of the Kunibito suffering from starvation, and preached the truth of the Mikuni of Kami, which is eternal, unchanging, motionless, without worry, and without destruction.

Manifesting the Shintai of Shaba-soku-jakukō-jiyōdo before the eyes of Hitobito, allowing them to bathe in the divine virtue and compassionate light of Kami, he taught the teachings in detail to a man named Parks, who was the most excellent among the Kunibito, and had him change his name to Ashihiko.

As the priest of Mikura, he bade farewell to many Hitobito, and while singing the propaganda song, he entrusted his body to Kamunagara along the mountains, gradually approaching the village of Chiru and the forest of Arashi.

At that very moment, the Ural-kyō priests, harboring resentment for having been driven away by Koto-yoriwake and Kuniyoriwake in the valley of Mikurayama, led by the propagandist Bull, incited many believers of the Anan and Yūzu priesthoods.

Surrounding them in ten or twenty layers as destroyers of Tengoku, the absurdity of their raging madness to take the lives of the thread-like Mikoto was such that Kuniyoriwake, adding the light of his tempered soul to the sphere of the jewel, immersed his body in that spiritual light.

Now being the time for the manifestation of divine virtue, he emitted a most intense five-colored spiritual light from the fingertips of his right hand toward the crowd.

Starting with Bull, Anan, and Yūzu, they fled for their lives.

While the pursuers of the Ural-kyō were scattered in all directions like spiderlings, Kuniyoriwake rested for a while, sat under the shade of a tree in the forest of Arashi, and while lost in meditation, Kuniyoriwake spoke to himself:

Kuniyoriwake: 'Ah, propagandists are truly amusing fellows. In the teachings of Ananai-kyō, it is ordained that a propagandist shall be a lone traveler. When Koto-yoriwake-dono parted ways with me coldly in the valley of Mikura, I felt a sense of loneliness and resented the cold-heartedness of the Mikoto. However, deeply respecting the words of the Great Teacher, I did not offer even a single word of rebuttal. Yet, now that I think of it, there is truly nothing as pleasant as traveling alone. No, no, I am by no means traveling alone; the divine revelation that Kami is with thee shines as brightly as the sun and stars. There is no enemy to oppose justice, and no blade to wound sincerity. Ah, how interesting is the journey of propaganda! Ah, how valiant is the duty of a propagandist! The joy of the heart that makes the vast, boundless universe its dwelling and wanders between Tenchi! I shall now save and assist the twisted priests and believers of the Ural-kyō, teach them the Shintai of Shaba-soku-jakukō-jiyōdo, let them spend the modern age interestingly, happily, and valiantly, and give joy and peace to their souls, thereby constructing the Mikuni of Kami upon the earth. Ah, Kamunagara, may thy divine spirit be blessed, and may the Aohitogusa under heaven smile and flourish like the green grasses of summer! Kuniyoriwake, bowing in heaven and crouching on earth, prays before the Mimahe of the Great Kami.'

He clasped his hands and tasted the joy of religious ecstasy.

At this time, two shadows came running toward this place in a flurry. Kuniyoriwake looked at these shadows through the starlight and said:

Kuniyoriwake: 'Hey, you people there, I am Kuniyoriwake, a propagandist of Ananai-kyō. Where are you heading in such a hurry?'

Suddenly addressed from the shade of the forest, the two immediately crouched on the ground and said:

Kiji: 'Yes, I am a person named Kiji, who was saved by you...'

Machi: 'I am a person named Machi... Our parents starved to death, and our wives and children were also driven to starvation and returned to the spirit world. Now, both of us are unfortunate beings who have lost our parents, wives, and children. We decided to die, thinking there was no joy left in living in this world, and while we were lying in the valley of Mikurayama waiting for death, you two appeared from somewhere, and we are grateful that you saved the lives of us Kunibito. While bathing in such precious divine grace, we cannot bear to repay none of it and live a life of drunken dreams and dead-like sleep. We have come all this way following you, wishing to save the people of the world just as we were saved, and to repay the divine grace. We wish to become your servants and accompany you, and that is why we have come this far. We humbly beg for your permission in every way.'

Kuniyoriwake thought to himself:

Kuniyoriwake: 'Well, this is troublesome. I have just realized the joy of traveling alone, and I was rejoicing and feeling brave about wandering through the country of Kami freely and at ease, without any worries in the vast sky and sea... I cannot simply refuse these two men who have crossed such distant mountains and slopes to follow me. Ah, it cannot be helped. This must also be the great will of Kami...'

Muttering to himself, Kuniyoriwake spoke to the two as if he had made up his mind:

Kuniyoriwake: 'It is the law of Kami that a propagandist of Ananai-kyō must travel alone. However, I shall make an exception this time.'

Kiji: 'Thank you for your immediate permission.'

Machi: 'Although I am an unworthy person, I humbly ask for your favor.'

They were moved to tears of joy. From this point on, Kuniyoriwake led the young men Kiji and Machi and proceeded toward the capital of Hiru.
