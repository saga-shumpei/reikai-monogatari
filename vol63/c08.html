'Teru is a fine place, catching the southern breeze, Where the Sūraya wind blows softly. Sūraya Island, floating in the offing, Illuminates the waves for a thousand ri at night. By day the Nichirin, by night the Riuō, It is clear and bright with the light of jewels. This sea is a lake famous even in the land of the moon, Where the moon floats upon the waves. Itahiko-sama, the minister of the Ananahi-kyō God, How auspicious is this day of departure. This lake surface, where the wind clears the high sky, Is the lake of Teru, whose depths are unknown.'

Itahiko began to chant. The group beat upon the gunwales to keep time.

Itahiko: 'A pillar of the Ananahi-kyō, Revering the divine Mikoto of the great Ōkami Kamususanō, As a teacher and lord of Tamakuniwake, I have crossed mountains and fields and traversed the sea, Crushing my heart into a thousand pieces, To fulfill the message entrusted by the Kami, And having come all the way to the village of Teru, Unexpectedly, the manifestation of Princess Burawāda appeared, And caused me to realize here the red cord bound by the Kami. As the weather is exceptionally fine today, the shadows of fishing boats are particularly numerous, floating here and there with the waves, their wing-like sails flickering like butterflies dancing among the flowers of the spring fields. Although the waves are somewhat high, whipped by the north wind, the mood is indescribably refreshing. The boat, rowed by Asumagaruda, Kākusu, and Bēsu as they stream with sweat, finally reached a corner of Mount Sūraya at the twilight of that day.

The shoreline looked as if white cloth had been bleached, and the crashing waves rose up, struck the rocks, and shattered and scattered; the sight was quite fearsome. Rowing the boat through the midst of snow-like white spray rising about a jō high, they finally reached the land. They then pulled the boat high up onto the shore and moored it. The sun was gradually setting.

Suddenly, a gale began to blow fiercely, the lake surface began to rage with wild waves, and a noisy, roaring sound began to be heard. The five members of the party decided to hide in the shadow of a large rock at a place about one or two chō up from the landing point to rest from the fatigue of the lake. They determined to wait for the dawn of the next day to climb the mountain. Spreading straw raincoats and placing sedge hats over their faces, they all lay down in the shadow of the rock.

The night deepened, the gale ceased, the roaring of the sea quieted, and the surroundings became profound and silent. The fourteenth-day moon pushed aside the clouds and began to shine brightly. Itahiko, Burawāda, and Asumagaruda all fell fast asleep without a care. Kākusu and Bēsu, however, felt restless and could not sleep, and as they lay wide awake, around what seemed to be the hour of the Rat, a strange shadow approached, rustling the branches of the dense forest all around.

Kākusu, trembling, watched the movement of the figure as if stealing a glance. The strange figure stood erect before the five, exposing a face as red as fire, with a blue tongue hanging out about five or six sun, and holding a khakkhara staff. Suddenly, the monster raised a voice like thunder,
