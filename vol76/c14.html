Chapter 14: 磐楠 Boat [1931]

磐楠舟〔1931〕

§ 1

At the sacred ground of Takachihoyama, serving as the Mihishirogami, the kamutsukasa of the Yahashirahime—among whom Asakahime, known as the deity of Omokatsu, was particularly manifest—had no way to suppress her fierce heart, and on a spring evening when cherry blossoms lay scattered, being all alone, she mounted a white horse and, stepping steadily across the unaccustomed Ōnohara, parting the pathless paths, she struck forth the living kototama to drive away the crooked deity of the river rapids of Sai with the merit of the true fire, and crossing the wild fields once more, she arrived at the sacred ground of the mountain of Sakaki, where she rejoiced in the warm hospitality of the myriad kami, and resting her feet for a while, she whipped her steed anew,

Wishing to press forward to the august presence of the deity Ōmotoakitsuo, while the earth was still young, she ventured forth all alone with courageous spirit across the Ōnohara where mists rose densely and billowed.

As twilight drew near, there was a large marsh lying ahead; the horse inclined both its ears forward, suddenly stopped its hoofs, and for all her whipping would not advance a single step. Finding this strange, thinking to rest from the fatigue of the journey and observe the situation anyhow, she dismounted onto the lawn where daylilies grew luxuriantly.

In short, all horses are sensitive animals; when there is an enemy ahead, they incline their ears forward and refuse to advance, while when a horse is in good spirits and triumphant, it pricks its ears straight up toward the sky, and when it harbors dissatisfaction toward its rider or thinks to throw them off, it inclines both ears backward. Therefore, those who ride horses must above all pay attention to the movement of the ears.

Asakahime no Kami dropped heavily onto the growth of reeds, and for a while she sat with her hands clasped, pondering, but the white horse would not stop backing up step by step, and in the end raised its front legs and stood erect, looking as though it were uttering a cry of astonishment and reporting a bad omen:

As I see my horse in terror, upon the path ahead, / Might Magatsumi be laying traps and waiting? / With its left and right ears inclined ahead, / How strange the hesitation of my halting steed! / In the dusk at the end of the endless great moor, / Why does my spirited horse stand paralyzed and fret? / As the evening mists grow ever deeper and denser, / This is a wondrous moorland where inches are indistinguishable. / If it must be thus, until the dawn of night upon the grassy growth, / Resting my steed, I shall surely wait. / Even I, who advance and advance and know not how to retreat, / Have no means when my horse is startled. / The voice of the evening crow sounds sorrowful too, / Though its shadow cannot be seen for the deep mist. / Gloomily evil spirits press close, and even my water and fire / Must have grown painful by now. / Day after day shall the divine words of Akitsuo no Kami / Cause me to meet with such hardships? / I who am called the deity of Omokatsu, / How could I ever hesitate before Magatsu?

Even now, striking the flint, I shall make the true fire shine, / And drive away the eighty crooked deities.

Chanting thus, she took out the flint and struck it sharply; the sparks scattered in all directions and fell upon the wilderness, and the fire instantly leaped to the dead grass of the previous year that lay heavy at the roots of the young spring grass, and fanned by the wind that blew up as one watched, the fire spread forward and widened, reaching the bank of the marsh and burning itself out. The deep mist that had enshrouded all sides suddenly scattered and vanished in the four directions, the sky cleared to reveal the texture of the azure clouds, and the sixth-day moon cast its sharp light upon the ground; thus, as far as the eye could reach, there was nothing to obstruct the view, and the surface of the marsh became a night where moonlight floated glitteringly. The deity Yaso-magatsumi no Kami, having been driven away and wounded in the dead of night at the river rapids of Sai by the merit of the true fire of Asakahime no Kami, had hidden its shadow for a while, but as its burns gradually healed, it conceived a second tactical plan, making them stray into the marsh together with the horse, waiting to exact revenge. It is to be thought that the spirited steed quickly knew such a perilous place to be close at hand ahead, and feared and hesitated before the danger.

Here Asakahime no Kami calmed her heart, mounted her steed once more, and hurried to the wide and long-extended bank of the marsh, and while gazing at the clear moonlight floating among the waves, she intoned a divine poem:

The clumsy craft of Magatsumi too has turned to mist, / And vanishing as smoke, it has fled away. / In the great moorland, burned by the fire I struck forth, / It has been purified without leaving a trace. / Did Magatsumi hide its shadow in this wild field, / Waiting and lying in wait to wound me? / Pierced by the keen ears and eyes of the spirited steed, / The trap of Yaso-magatsumi has been broken. / Grateful is this true fire bestowed by the kami, / Perhaps it is the guard of the path I walk. / Though tens of thousands of Magatsumi come to attack, / I possess the sword of true fire. / The moonlight shining brightly upon the surface of the water, / Is it perhaps the august spirit of my dear brother-spouse? / My brother-spouse who dwells ten thousand ri away, / Alas, I see his shadow close at hand here. / The moonlight shining brightly above and below, / I am filled with joy thinking it is my brother-spouse. / Gazing upon this marsh, with no recourse, I shall go on, / This water which bars the land of the western region

Anyway, tonight we shall face the moonlight of the marsh and spend the night awake.'

When Asakahime no Kami alighted nimbly from the back of her horse, wondrously, upon the shore devoid of even a single small pebble, a rectangular boulder lay horizontally, and since it made a fitting seat, she struck down her waist to rest while chanting a miuta.

'The Lord God must be gracious indeed, for upon the watery shore there was a boulder where I might rest my weary self. Resting the fatigue of my body upon this boulder, would that I might worship the moon and pass the night! Do the voices of the insects chirp, lurking in the roots of the grass that were burned away, with sorrowful sounds? Flying across the surface of the waves to the right and left, earth-birds are crying while clinging to the moon. Before one knows it, even my swift horse has crouched upon the boulder, resting its water and fire. Since this boulder is still young, making it into a boat, I shall cross this broad marsh.'

Even as she caused this to be chanted, Himegami used her slender, soft right and left hands to hollow out the inside of the boulder and fashion it into the shape of a boat. Just as a potter kneads plates, teabowls, and the like with soft clay, in the twinkling of an eye she created the shape of a boat,

'One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten, a hundred, ten thousand, tens of millions of Kami, gather ye together and protect us! Ha-ho-he-hi, become a boat, become a boat, this boulder! This boulder of the rock-boat which I have now created, become so light that it floats even upon the surface of the water, become light as a wooden boat!'

When she caused this to be proclaimed, even the stubborn boulder suddenly changed into a wooden boat, and of its own accord sliding smoothly, popped and floated upon the shore; whereupon Himegami, together with her horse, leaped directly into the boat,

'How bright, how bright! The blessing of the living Kototama causes the boulder to have become a true timber boat. Though there are neither oars nor rudder, by virtue of the Kototama I shall pilot and cross this august boat. The moon in the grand sky grows ever more clear, and the boat upon which I ride slips over the waves. Gazing upon the moon mirrored at the water's bottom, the wind caresses and goes over the surface of the waves. It is a feeling of crossing through the middle sky of heaven and earth, gazing upon the moon above and below. The stars of the countless august heavens become gold sand and silver sand at the bottom of the water, shimmering. Even the thousand-pulling boulder of the crooked deity's ugly wicked scheme has indeed become a boat to help me. O horse, O horse, thou art wise and valiant, making known to me the wicked scheme of the crooked deity! However broad the marsh may be, by the power of the Kototama I shall reach the dawn shore. Easily seeing the moon within the august boat, I shall rest the fatigue of my journey.

Wafted by the northwestern wind, my boat proceeds onward ever more though it has neither oars nor rudder. The mountain of Takahideno rises toweringly among the clouds, illuminated by tonight's moonlight. When I looked up, it was distant indeed, since not much time had passed since Takahideno's mountain emerged. The ridge of Sakaegeyama became faintly visible, clearing up beneath the moonlight. Although the moon shines in the grand sky, the distant Takahayama has its form invisible. There is nothing whatsoever obstructing the eyes in the great moor; the moon of this broad marsh is clear. If there is a crooked deity blocking the path where I am to go, I shall drive it away with the living Kototama. Galloping through the midst of the wideness of heaven and earth, I am a traveler all alone. By the assistance of the swift horse, crossing the endless moor, I am lonely. Whipping my horse of lonely heart, I shall advance with courage through the endless realm of the country.

My boat draws near to the shore yonder, and the depths of the august sky have become like the dawn. As the shore draws near, the clear voices of magpies singing can be heard high and clear. Soon, if the morning sun rises today, the voices of all birds will freshly sing of the world.'

At length, when the august boat lay horizontally on the shore at the end of the broad marsh, Asakahime no Kami together with her horse abandoned the boat,

'My boat, change thy body into a thousand-pulling boulder, and exist eternally upon this shore! One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten, a hundred, ten thousand, O boat, O boat, become as thou wert originally a boulder, become a firm and everlasting thousand-pulling boulder!'

When she caused this to be proclaimed, the august boat suddenly became a great massive boulder as it was originally and stood toweringly upon the shore. She gave this boulder the name of August-Boat Boulder.

The sky of dawn gradually brightened more and more, and the new heavenly sun ascended brilliantly, parting the clouds, illuminating every nook and corner of the marsh's surface.
