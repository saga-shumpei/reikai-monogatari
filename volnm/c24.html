Chapter 24: The Moon over Mukyoku (Mukyoku no Tsuki) [24]

木局の月〔24〕

§ 1

On the fourteenth day of the fifth month, which was the Ōjitsu, at nine o'clock in the morning, General Rosenkuwai came to the encampment of Taijiyōshiyō Hideo, and at ten-thirty in the morning, he saw off the departure from the Shimomokukiyokushiu barracks. With two carriages and one cart loaded with baggage, followed by numerous soldiers in the front and rear, they crossed the rushing torrents of the Tōrugaha, which flowed like a winding serpent through the great plains, many times on horseback, and at three-thirty in the afternoon, they arrived safely at the temporary palace of Shimokukiyokushiu. Mongolian horses are slightly smaller in physique compared to Japanese riding horses, yet they are resistant to extreme cold and heat, and possess strong endurance and a docile nature. When they see the river water, every horse shakes its head and charges forward, and the way they cross the bluish, rushing torrents with composure is almost as if they were traveling on flat ground. Inoue Kanekichi had been acquainted with the bandit leader Mandakan and others for a long time, and thus he was well-versed in the affairs of Manchuria and Mongolia. As he traveled, he sang a brave song said to have been composed by Japanese bandits while on horseback.

Storm blow, blow, O Makatsu gale / In the snowy Mongolia the sun sets / In the moonlight illuminating the war saddle / Looking up, the flock of wild geese is high / I have no home, I have no wife / Ten years and more since I left my country / Though I have a home, it is a rock cave / The followers I lead are over two thousand horsemen / In the play of shouting from horseback / When I draw the spear, swish, swish / It extends one jō, the gleam of the blade / Lightning flashes, forming a jewel / At the hidden house in the Kōanrei / Whipping the small end of the sword / When I peer through the darkness, two thousand men / Ride side by side, creeping closer / The group of bandits, filled with killing intent / Where did they drink, they smell of sake / Suffering from boredom, they drink sake / Piled up like mountains, the meat of tigers.

When I push back my clothes, all over / Are the sword scars I boast of daily / Today's prize is fifty thousand ryō / Tomorrow we shall raid the land of Mongolia / Now, for the moment I shall doze / The pillow I take out is a skull / Ah, can I ever forget my hometown / The face of the dear child dances before my eyes.

To protect Hideo, who was at the temporary palace of Shimokukiyokushiu, three barracks were established within a distance of only fifteen shiri. Their arrangement was such that at the very front, namely the northwest, Commander Tsū led two hundred soldiers to stand guard; in the center, Commander Ka guarded with over one hundred and several dozen men; and at the last, namely the southeast encampment, Lieutenant General Chōhiko served as brigade commander to guard it. Hideo rode back and forth between these points with his subordinate soldiers, practicing horsemanship with ease and without any hesitation. Whenever Hideo visited each barracks, it was customary for each commander to have the soldiers line up outside the gate, perform a salute with presented arms in unison, and then lead the way into the barracks. When Brigade Commander Chōhiko was repairing his Mauser rifle himself, he accidentally shot his own shin, and the bullet struck the bone and remained deep in the flesh, causing him to complain of pain. Upon receiving the urgent report, Hideo, leading Colonel Ino, the chief of the medical department, along with Masumiwake, Moritaka, and others, rushed to the brigade commander's encampment, performed chinkon on the affected area, and stopped the intense pain on the spot, while Colonel Ino immediately took a knife and devoted his efforts to extracting the bullet. However, as the bullet was deeply embedded in the bone, it could not be extracted, so Hideo had no choice but to pray to God for it to heal as it was. Miraculously, the brigade commander suddenly forgot the pain, and was able to mount his horse and command his subordinates with composure, so all the officers and soldiers let out voices of admiration at the miraculous sign. One day, several Japanese, along with Hakuryōkaku, Onchōkō, Daishibun, Kōkokuhō, and others, were riding horses between the barracks when, startled by something, Hagiwara Toshiaki's horse suddenly reared up, and at that moment, Hagiwara was thrown headlong to the ground and fell flat on his back. Hagiwara's horse ran off as if into clouds and mist. Hideo, who came from behind, saw Hagiwara fallen under his feet, and suddenly whipped his horse's belly, leaping over him in a single bound to avoid the danger of being trampled by the horse's hooves; but this time, Hakuryōkaku's horse threw him to the ground and ran off as if into clouds and mist. They sent numerous cavalry soldiers in all directions and fortunately were able to capture both horses. Two or three days later, Nadahiko, who had gone to Hōten as a military envoy, returned safely to the temporary palace of Shimokukiyokushiu with several Chinese soldiers. Upon seeing Hideo, Nadahiko raised his voice and wept with nostalgia. He had encountered danger on the way many times, and the joy of having finally preserved his life and returned welled up all at once. From then on, Moritaka and Nadahiko practiced horse riding daily without distraction. Furthermore, Moritaka taught jujutsu to Commander Ō and Staff Officer Ō whenever he had spare time. There were four or five among the Chinese officers who learned jujutsu from Moritaka. However, the majority of the officers and soldiers despised jujutsu. They said, 'No matter how skilled one is at jujutsu, it cannot match a projectile weapon; in today's war, there is nothing more effective than guns and cannons; things like jujutsu are a kind of amusement.' One day, when Moritaka was strolling in the suburbs on horseback, he was barked at by the usual shīgō, and as his horse was startled and bolted, he fell from the horse, but he laughed and made excuses, saying that he had not fallen but had landed safely. Nadahiko also claimed to be a master of horsemanship, but he too was surrounded by dozens of shīgō, and the moment his horse was startled and bolted, he was abandoned on the ground, and by the time he was startled and got up, his horse had fled far away to a place where not even a shadow could be seen. Upon hearing this report, Hideo ordered several officers and soldiers to capture the runaway horses. Major On, along with six soldiers, ran around the endless wilderness, and by the time the sun set, he finally caught the horses and returned, so Hideo thanked Major On and the others for their hard work and comforted them by giving them various sweets and cigarettes. The Nitsuponjin staying at the Karidono of Kamimuchizu were all carefree, and lived their lives merely by engaging in trivial and silly chatter such as, 'Ochikokoteno, utsutokohate-na, bohoranubo, onkusuarutechi, unnurutte, ohonotorute, pishyamutsutorute, marakaunsuna, kotoraanteina, pasapana, shiesushiena.'

千万里荒野の奥の馬遊かな

寝そべりつ窓の側にてペンを執り

ペン先は早くも坊主となりにけり

山も野も吾も坊主の蒙古かな

ポロハナの力も薄き蒙古喇嘛

どの山も金字形なり上木局収

駒並べて軍の司来りけり

紅の夕日の空に月清し

夕日影山野をボルに染めにけり

紫の雲たなびきて入日近し

十四夜の月は日の内輝けり

窓明けて初夏の満月拝みけり

初夏の月初めて見たり蒙古地に

月清く星稀にして風寒し

吾友は今宵の月を吾と見む

月次の今日の祭りや月丸し

雪解けて河水日々に増りけり

草も木も青み出でけり初夏の雨

大空の月を包みし雲散りぬ

雪解けて三五の月空に照り
