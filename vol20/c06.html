Chapter 6: Plum Flower の痣 [668]

梅花の痣〔668〕

§ 1

Matsutakahiko had abandoned fishing, which he had loved more than food until then, moved his residence to the Nagatoko of the Takeshi Shrine, and together with Munehiko and Okatsu, served the Kami morning and evening, while also conveying the Kyōri of the Ananai-kyō in a modest manner. Tagosaku and Oharu stopped by to visit the Omiya and to listen to the old man's stories.

Tagosaku: 'Grandfather, your reputation in the village has become quite good lately. Thanks to the sound of the Onorito that can be heard from early in the morning, the whole village is free from illness and disaster, and everyone says it is entirely thanks to Matsutakahiko-sama. Furthermore, the reputation of Munehiko-sama and Okatsu-sama is also very good. However, the villagers have been saying that although a great deal of time has passed since you came here and became the Kamunushi, Otake-sama has passed away, and you are living as a widower without a wife, which is truly a pity. They have been discussing among themselves whether it would be better for you to adopt Munehiko-sama and Okatsu-sama as your children and have them succeed you.'

Matsutakahiko: 'Since I entered the Ananai-kyō, I have completely removed the attachments I held until now, and I have realized the splendor of the Yūkai, so I know how much more carefree it is to spend the rest of my life alone than to worry about getting a successor. There is probably no one in the wide world who would become the child of such a senile old man. When I pass away, they will surely not just throw me away. Out of the kindness of the villagers, they will likely bury me somewhere. It is better to go to the Yūkai and live with my wife than to have attachments remain in this world.'

Oharu: 'Grandfather, that may be so, but if you were to fall ill, would you not have no one to take care of you? Even if they are villagers, they cannot just stay by your side and nurse you through your illness. It might be fine during their spare time from the fields, but if you were to fall ill in the middle of rice planting or rice harvesting, there would be nothing you could do. Please, do find some way to arrange for a successor.'

Matsutakahiko: 'No, if it were a child raised from the straw, I might be able to force them to look after me, but I could not speak so bluntly to a son adopted suddenly, and I would have to be reserved instead, so please, do not urge me to do that any further.'

§ 2

Tagosaku: 'Did you have no children, sir?'

Matsutakahiko: 'I was originally born in the land of Ki, but when Lord Susanōnomikoto was banished from Takaamahara by the Kamuyara and departed for a distant land, the world was once plunged into a night of Tokoyami. It was at that time that Akuma began to Ōkau, and my three children—two boys and one girl—were snatched away by someone. My wife and I, in tears, came here fifteen or sixteen years ago, and thanks to the kindness of the villagers, I have been serving as a Miyamori and spending the rest of my days. Ah, I wonder what has become of my children. Until now, I had been able to forget about my wife and children by distracting myself with my beloved fishing, but when a story like this comes up, I remember them again.'

Matsutakahiko sheds tears. Munehiko says,

Munehiko: 'Excuse me, Grandfather, you said just now that you are from the land of Ki. Exactly which part?'

Matsutakahiko: 'I was born in Kumano.'

Munehiko: 'That is a strange thing to hear. In truth, I do not know my own parents, and I heard from others when I was a child that I was born around Kumano. I was snatched away by an evil god into a rocky cave deep in the mountains of Ōdaigahara and was kept confined there for a long time. A magnificent god appeared there and said to me, "You shall now go to the village of Naniwa and endure hardships. Once you have become a full-grown man, go on a pilgrimage around the world." I still remember that as if it were a dream. It is said that I also had three siblings. Being a person with neither parents nor siblings wherever I went, no one would show me any affection. From the time I finally reached adulthood and was no longer in danger of being trampled like cattle or horses, I gradually began to drink sake. I tried being adopted into families here and there several times, and I also tried having a home of my own, but having been raised wandering everywhere like a beggar since I was a child, having a home or being adopted felt stifling, so I would always run away. I would drink sake in despair, cause my wife worry, and I have had many wives pass away before me. If I listen to what some people say, I was born in the year of Hinoeuma, and it seems I am a spirit that brings a curse upon women. Though I am young like this now, as I think of you, Grandfather, I cannot help but feel anxious about my own future.'

Matsutakahiko: 'Sir, do you not have a birthmark that looks like a plum blossom under your arm?'

§ 3

Munehiko: 'How on earth do you know that, Anata?'

Matsutakahiko: 'Well, both of my sons have a plum blossom birthmark. The elder one had it clearly on his left armpit, and the younger one on his right. Relying on that, my wife and I have spent four or five years searching for them, but we could not just grab people and ask them to strip and show us their armpits. When summer came, I went to the kaisuiyokujiyō and checked the armpits of people around the age of my children, but it was a difficult place to see no matter what. Even when I went to the onsen, I could not find them. I have never been so worried in my life.'

He then looks down.

Munehiko: 'Grandfather, I have something that looks like a flower under my right armpit. Would you mind taking a look at it?'

Matsutakahiko starts, his body jerking naturally, and says:

Matsutakahiko: 'Let me see, let me see. Ah, truly, truly, it is an unmistakable plum blossom birthmark. The five-petaled plum was slightly chipped at the top, and it is still chipped. If that is the case, are you not the man called Take?'

Munehiko: 'Yes, when I was a child, I was called Take. I received the name Munehiko from the Brahman Kyō.'

Tagosaku looks at both of their faces and says:

Tagosaku: 'Grandfather, the shape of the head, the way the kobana is slightly raised, and the set of the ears—do they not look very much alike? Perhaps he is your son, is he not?'

Matsutakahiko: 'He is my unmistakable son. Ah, Munehiko, thank you for being safe. This is all thanks to the okage of the Kamisama.'

He then prostrates himself, weeping.

Munehiko: 'Were you my father? I have been rude to you, not knowing who you were. Please, forgive me.'

Munehiko prostrates himself firmly and weeps aloud with tears of joy. Okatsu folds her hands, lost in thought, and helps Matsutakahiko up, saying:

Okatsu: 'Excuse me, Grandfather, Anata, you said you had one daughter, did you not? Does that daughter have any distinguishing features?'

Matsutakahiko, his voice strained, says:

Matsutakahiko: 'As for the daughter's features, I remember that she had moles arranged in a triangle above her navel. Ah, I am happy to have met one of my sons, but where might my elder son Matsu and my daughter Ume be living? One wish is granted, and yet another attachment I had managed to cast aside has resurfaced. Truly, this is a painful floating world.'

§ 4

She sinks into dejection. What strikes deep into the heart of Okatsu is the triangular mole above her navel; because she has lived as a married couple with Munehiko, she cannot even reveal her identity, and she suffers alone in her heart.

Matsutakahiko: 'I ask a strange thing, but is it my own heart, or do I feel as though I cannot help but yearn for you? There is something about you that resembles Take in some way, and you also resemble my wife O-Take somewhere, but could you perhaps be my daughter?'

Okatsu, while stammering,

Okatsu: 'I have heard that I had parents and siblings, but their whereabouts are still unknown to me. However, I do not have the mole that you, sir, speak of.'

Matsutakahiko: 'Ah, is that so? That is fortunate. If by any chance you were my daughter, it would be a blood-relative marriage, and being a violation of the heavenly law, we would be punished by the Kamisama; it would be pitiful, but well, it is good that you are not my sister.'

Okatsu: 'Is a marriage between siblings such a grave sin? However, if by any chance one were to marry without knowing, what would happen then?'

Matsutakahiko: 'Well, I cannot say for sure. As it is a rare occurrence, even at my age, I have never heard of it, nor have I ever been taught about it by Maura-sama of the Ananai-kyō.'

Munehiko: 'Is it not said that there is no curse from a god one does not know? Even if there were such a couple in the Seken, surely the compassionate Kamisama would forgive them.'

Okatsu covers her face with both sleeves, writhing and weeping.

Tagosaku: 'Hey, hey, Okatsu-dono, is it right for you to be crying at such a wonderful reunion of father and child? Ah, I see, you are envious. Munehiko-sama must be happy to meet the father he has been longing for, but you are crying because you are reminded of when you might be able to meet your own. Well, well, leave everything to the Kamisama and wait for the right time. Surely, through the virtue of your faith, if your father and mother are in this world, the Kamisama will let you meet them.'

Okatsu: 'Yes, thank you very much. You have said such kind words. While you were rejoicing in the reunion of parent and child—no, no, while you were rejoicing in your reunion—I did not share in your joy and instead showed you my tears. I am truly sorry. Please, father—no, Munehiko-sama's father—please think of me as your child and treat me with affection. From now on, I will change my ways and treat you as my parent and serve you with filial piety.'

Matsutakahiko: 'Ah, you have spoken well. I cannot help but feel as though we are not strangers. Let us treat each other as parent and child, show mutual kindness, and perform the work of the Kamisama.'

The three were overcome with tears of joy, and for a while, a curtain of silence fell upon them. The spring sun shone brightly, hanging over the treetops of the thousand-year-old cedar in the forest of Takeshi. Crows were hatching their eggs, the male and female flying to and fro, foraging for food to give to their chicks. The chicks opened their yellow beaks as wide as they could stretch.

Tagosaku, being a hasty man by nature,

Tagosaku: 'Hey, Grandfather, Sou-sama, congratulations on your reunion as parent and child. Well then, I shall return home now and bring something to celebrate. Hey, Oharu-sama, you must celebrate something too. Come, let us go home.'

Matsutakahiko: 'Excuse me, Tagosaku-sama, Oharu-sama, please do not tell the villagers for a while. I would feel terrible if it caused another commotion.'

Tagosaku: 'What are you saying, Grandfather? The arrival of Daikuwankitensama, which is a cataclysm of heaven and earth, a miracle where one knows not where to dance with hands or stomp with feet—how could one keep silent about this? Come, Oharu-sama, quickly, quickly!'

Urging her on, and paying no heed to the grandfather calling him back, he tightened his Nejihachimaki, tucked up his skirts, and ran back toward his home.

Matsutakahiko: 'Ah, what a kind man. He is a meddlesome fellow, but there is no one with a purer heart than he. Ah, even though he appears that way, his heart is firm. He is the pride of the whole village. I truly wish to find a good bride for him.'

Meanwhile, Tagosaku, wanting to report to the missionary of Maura who was staying at Tome-kou's detached house faster than anything else,

'It is a disaster, a disaster!'

he shouted along the way, running as if a fire had broken out in the neighborhood. Tome-kou was just about to head out the gate, shouldering his hoe.

Tagosaku:

Tagosaku: 'Hey, Tome-kou, where are you going with that hoe on your shoulder? It is a disaster, a cataclysm of heaven and earth! A great event has occurred, a joy so great that one knows not where to dance with hands or stomp with feet. Come, come, hurry up and get ready! Why are you dawdling? You can afford to take a break from farm work. It is a heavenly change, a heavenly change!'

He stomped his feet and danced around.

Tome-kou: 'Even if you say that, you do not tell me the reason, so I do not understand. What on earth is it?'

Tagosaku: 'What is it? You know what it is, don't you? It is a joyous occasion. Ah, ah, it is a disaster! Quickly, take me to the missionary, Maura-sama!'
