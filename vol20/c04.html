§ 1

Even a demon at eighteen, and coarse tea at its first brewing, such is the scroll-story of the serpent at twenty; though the season is a little early for the Miroku period, having the long and thin, Katonbozen-like Katō Niyorai take up the brush, while lying sideways, Zuigetsu confuses the past and present in a dream-story; the waves of the heart are at Utsuyama, and the propagandists of the Brahman religion who have entrenched themselves in the village, though their Kototama be turbid, are the one line that flees upon striking the Kototama of the heavenly Maura of Hiko; reading the rafters of the ceiling, with a futon under his buttocks, together with the smoke of Shikishima, in a cloud of Unen Mōrō, the elusive and tearfully recounted Domohiko-monogatari; Aa, Kannagara-kamu-kamu, riding upon the glib Kototama-kuchiguruma, reluctantly, one goes along.

Tagosaku, leaning on his hoe as a staff, with a cheek-wrap made of a grime-stained towel, sidled up to Tomekō, and raising a rattling voice that sounded like dragging an empty kerosene can over a stony field, began to sing a socially incoherent song of Tomohiko-conquest.

Tagosaku: 'Whether the morning sun shines or clouds over, / Whether the moon waxes or wanes, / The villagers of Utsuyama, / Carry their hoes morning and evening, / Old women and young girls alike, work in the fields, / In the midst of their diligent toil, / From where did they descend and appear? / The Brahman who disrupts the discipline, / The pillar of hypocrisy, / That Tomohiko-san came along, / And first of all, he brought Tomekō to submission and harmony, / And from the day that the young widow Oharu, / Lost her senses to him, / The villagers of over twenty houses, / Forgot their field work, / From morning till night, reading the Brahman's, / Incomprehensible sutras, / Shedding tears of joy, / This year makes exactly three full years, / The fields and farms are going to ruin year by year.'

Tagosaku: 'Wondering what will become of us in such a state, / To the villagers lost on the path, / Being laughed at as a stubborn fool, / I sowed wheat and planted beans, / Tending to the potato sprouts morning and evening, / Giving them fertilizer and nurturing them, / Looking forward to their maturity, / Sweating from morning till night, / Into the field I cultivated, Tomekō, / Fearing the living Kototama, / Of Mamorihiko of the Sangyōkyō, / Deviated from the path and into my field, / Stepped in and cruelly, / Crushed the sprouts to death, so, / I too felt a bit of anger, / And came to Tomekō's house, / To demand a strong explanation, / But the words of that fellow Tomekō, / By no means sit well with me, / There is even a theory of a female-governed nation.'

Tagosaku: 'In this world, even if it be potatoes, / Can one endure having the sprouts trampled? / Return them to the way they were, / Is the teaching of the Brahman religion, / Something that may cruelly, / Damage and destroy the blessings of Heaven? / As I pressed him for an answer, / This Tomekō raised his face, / And while floating a cold sneer, / He said that Sangar-fujin would come, / And even practice birth control, / When he spouted such noisy theories, / That crushing twenty or thirty potato sprouts, / Is for the sake of the country, / And for the sake of the people, he argued with a paradox, / Even I could not bear it, / And the tip of the hoe I carried, / Aiming at Tomekō's head, / I struck down, wishing his bones would shatter, / Instantly, Tomekō dodged.'

As the Ananahi Kami's Oshihe Sendenshi Morihiko-sama unexpectedly cut off his own toe while fleeing, he crawled upon the ground in Giyōten, and when he apologized for his Burei, the Shinjin of infinite Jinji, Morihiko, with a smile brimming upon his face, forgave his sin; ah, how Arigata, how Arigata it is.

If it had been a finger of Tomohiko of the Brahman religion, what would have happened? He was bullied with much fuss and agitation, instantly stripped of his garments, and forced to endure Aragiyō such as the bridge of swords, walking through fire, and diving to the Minasoko for five or ten days, until his very Inochi was measured; when one thinks of this, the nobility of the Ananahi Kami's Oshihe sinks into the depths of one's heart, and Tagosaku, who finished the Tetsuzu for Nifushin with joy and courage, is Mohaya no longer of the Brahman religion.

Come, Tomohiko, Tomohiko, Mohaya your luck has run out; either show the fruit of Kaishin as quickly as Hitohi, or else return pointing toward the mansion of Onikumohiko of Mount Ōe; the season when an Akugami like you could act with the authority of a Kaumori in a village without birds has become a dream of the past; do not make a fool of me just because I am Tagosaku, for I too am a Bunrei of Heaven and Earth; even though I am an adopted son, once I have inherited the house, I am the master; you, who hold honey in your mouth and a sword in your tail, have deceived everyone well, even down to your wife and children, and because of that, there has been no end to the sectarian strife within the village, causing great trouble.

Nevertheless, now that it has come to this, though it is uncouth to argue, the worm in my belly will not be calmed; if you take off your helmet, roll up your flag with the spear reversed, and surrender, Tagosaku will release his long-held grudge, but if you remain stubborn and arrogant, I, this Tagosaku who has been called a mere peasant, will cut the string of my patience and will not forgive you, performing the frog-leap and earthworm-cutting; let me hear your answer, let me hear it quickly; though I have heard the noble Oshihe of the Sumegami that the Kami Naohi who created this world and the Ōnaohi of broad heart say that in the human world one must look, listen, and speak straight, how could I ever forget this? It is not just the hardship of us alone, but needless to say of Utsuyama Village, and by extension the great hardship of the world; I must cut off the roots and wither the leaves of the Akugami while there is still time, rebuild Utsuyama Village, the secret village of the original past, and make a pilgrimage of gratitude before the shrine of Takeshi; come, Tomohiko, Tomohiko, will you not reform your heart quickly? I, who have breathed the same fresh air morning and evening, have seen your hardship with my own eyes.

Buried in snow, I peer ahead to see a faintly flickering light. As I approach the rope of power, I open the half-broken gate, and there appears a giant of a man, tall enough to pierce the clouds. 'Please, come in,' he says with a kindness that seems ill-suited to his face. Feeling as if I am treading on thin ice, my movements are constrained, but I follow the will of Kami and enter to find yet another giant, sitting cross-legged by the hearth, glaring around with a loathsome look in his eyes. Just as I suspect this to be the den of mountain bandits, there appear from the opposite side the messengers of the teaching of the Three-Five Kami. The joy I felt upon realizing they were Akihiko and Komahiko, the two men, was like the feeling of meeting the spring of Tokoyo.

Waiting for the dawn, the three of us tread upon the snow of Hitono-o Pass, stumbling and falling until we reach the village of Uke. Arriving before the shrine of Takeshi, we offer up Norito, and while we rest for a moment, an old man with white hair and a strange appearance climbs up, leaning on his staff. He is the priest of the shrine of Takeshi, Matsutakahiko, coming to pay his respects. Following behind the old man, while half-buried in snow piled more than five feet deep, we look up at the sheer cliff. Just then, Akihiko and Komahiko, whose hearts are impatient, seize the moment of my unguarded state and push me with all their might, performing a feat of sliding through the air.

Landing at the foot of the snow-covered cliff, I rejoice, taking it as a trial from Kami. As I am pouring my heart into prayers of gratitude, Akihiko and Komahiko, the two men, speak in unison: 'At the foot of Hitono-o Pass, we gave you sixty-five points. Now, we have appeared here again as examiners to test your spirit. If you are truly a splendid Sendenshi, we shall grant you thirty-five points, making you a messenger of Kami known to all under heaven.' Saying they would celebrate me, their figures vanish, and even the sound of their footsteps is hidden in the white snow. Braving the white snow falling like goose down, I reach a solitary house by the river, and what do I see but the old couple, Matsutakahiko.

They are sitting upright before the hearth, in the midst of sipping bitter tea. Staying there for three or four days, the old man takes a net to the river, going out to scoop up small fish to treat me. Suddenly, there is a splash and a spray of water. Surprised, I rush over to save him, but when I arrive, the old man is clinging to a willow tree by the river, climbing up with a broad grin. I immediately run back, take his change of clothes—an act of non-action—and rush back to the riverbank to hand them to the old man. I wring out his wet clothes and return to the hut. The old couple rejoices, and morning and evening we ask and answer questions about the teaching of Kami, talking together as the snow-laden spring gives way to the dawn of spring.

The plum blossoms have scattered, and the Yayoi sky is filled with the brush-like ears of wheat. Tomohiko of the Brahman religion enters, claiming to be a messenger. With Tomeko and four others in his company, he shows his face at the gate, turns toward the old man and woman, and whispers something to them. Finding their behavior suspicious, I peek through a tear in the door. Seeing that the situation with the five of them appears ominous, I begin to turn the wheel of Kototama, and at once, they turn on their heels and flee. Ah, Kannagara Kami-Kami! Witnessing the blessings of the spirit before my very eyes, I praise the great divine majesty of Kami in detail and in full, then quietly slip out of this house and onto the path through the fields where beans and wheat grow thick.

§ 5

Just as they were advancing, Tomekō, who had arrived earlier, was snarling at a man over something in a potato field. With a hoe raised high, they were engaged in a fierce quarrel, wearing twisted headbands, arguing over whether or not one had stepped on a baby in the potato field. As I stepped forward to mediate and waited for the right moment, Tagosaku swung his hoe down with all his might. Tomekō nimbly dodged, and the force of the blow, missing its mark, landed squarely on his own foot, biting into it with such strength that it bit off a little finger. In a panic, he reached out his hand, and as he tried to fix the finger he relied on as a parent, he turned it inside out. From that moment, Tagosaku suddenly shook hands with Tomekō-san, concluded a peace treaty, and they advanced happily.

When they arrived, Tomohiko of the Kami-no-Oshihe was standing there with a composed air, listening intently to the Sendenka that echoed through heaven and earth. Hearing it, he stood there, troubled by the judgment of good, evil, right, and wrong in a Kototamasen where the distinction between good and evil was nowhere to be found. Tomekō-san stepped forward and pressed him, saying, 'There are bones in my arm, give me an answer quickly.' Finding the style of this ridiculous, though I knew it was wrong, I could not help but burst into laughter. Following him, Tagosaku advanced and sang a Sendenka with all his heart; it was like a scene of flowers and autumn leaves, none inferior to the other.

Though they bear no fruit, the propellers flashing above the autumn leaves were divided into left and right, a great void of incoherence. At these words that seemed to fly through the sky, Tomohiko, the missionary of the Brahman religion and of the Kami-no-Oshihe, wore a look of bewilderment, having been taken by surprise. The trap surrounding the mansion, which he had relied upon as an impregnable fortress, was indeed a rough business. A deep pit, a chasm difficult to approach, was suddenly dug between the two men and Tomohiko. Ah, Kannagara-kamu-kamu, may your spirits be blessed, and may the depth and nobility of the grace of the Sumeru-Ōkami shine quickly into the depths of the heart of the priest Tomohiko.

Even if the moon waxes or wanes, even if the earth should sink, should the sincerity of Ama-no-Maura be left without salvation? Originally, the divided spirits of heaven and earth, both the Sangyō and the Brahman, are children of Kami who serve. Oh, priest of Kami, please rectify your heart as soon as possible. I hereby reveal the sincerity of Ama-no-Maura. Ah, Kannagara-kamu-kamu, may your spirits be blessed.

As soon as he finished singing, perhaps startled by this voice, Tomohiko immediately dashed out from the back gate at the speed of Idaten, plunged into the river with a splash, swam across toward the opposite bank, and disappeared into the thicket of old trees. A mountain gale scattering cherry blossoms brushed the surface of the river, drawing ripples like fish scales. Here, Maura was welcomed by many villagers, including Tomekō and Tagosaku, and they gathered at the Takeshi-no-Miya to offer prayers of gratitude, after which it was decided that he would stay for a while at the thatched hut of Matsutakahiko.

The mountain sides in all directions are adorned with the garments of fresh green, in the wind of early summer. In the month of the Hare when Shiyaka was born, on the night after the sky has cleared, beneath the moon that trembles in the cold, I open the window and gaze out.
