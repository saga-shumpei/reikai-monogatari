Chapter 2: 赤児の誤 [664]

赤児の誤〔664〕

§ 1

Buried in snow, the humble cottage by the riverbank in Kahabata welcomes the spring; the cold snow melts away, bringing joy to the old couple. The waters of Uzuyamagaha grow warm, the withered trees begin to sprout green buds, and the plum blossoms at the eaves start to scent the air; the season has arrived when the nightingale visits, opening the doors of the valley. Surrounded by mountains, this place is a secret realm of the world, where the hearts of men are simple and honest, just as in the age of the gods.

At that very time, Tomohiko, known to the world as a remnant of the followers of Onikumohiko, a missionary of the teachings of the Baramon god, advocated the doctrine of Reishiyutaijiu to the villagers of about twenty households. Crossing swords and crossing fire, diving into the depths of the water and rising again, he forced the men and women of the village to strip naked in a thorny hut where even birds would not perch, and with clever words, he persuaded them to perform perverse practices that destroy the body. The villagers, believing it to be the first and purest act of serving the Kami to drive nails into the soles of their wooden clogs and walk upon them, suffered in heart and body, weeping with joy in their forced and unnatural devotion.

To save them from this tragic state, Matsutakahiko, a missionary of the Ama no Maura, stayed for a long time in a humble house, explaining the way of the Kami in detail, morning and evening; yet, the villagers, deeply lost in their delusions, would not listen, and their plight was truly pitiful.

Spring has gradually deepened; the rape blossoms have scattered heartlessly, bearing needles of green pods, and the radish flowers are blooming white, though late. Matsutakahiko's thatched hut, situated on a riverbank sandwiched between a large river on one side and rice fields on the other, is approached by four or five men and women.

Kō: 'Yes, excuse us.'

Matsutakahiko: 'Oh, is that you, Tomekō? Where are you all going with such a crowd and looking so pale?'

Tomekō: 'No, we are not going anywhere. We five have come to your house as representatives of the villagers. Today, we must have you listen carefully. It is a major issue concerning the personal affairs of you and your wife.'

Matsutakahiko: 'What is this major issue? I suppose you have come to exaggerate some trivial matter, like a kappa breaking wind in the water.'

Tomekō, squaring his shoulders, rolling up his sleeves, and wearing a twisted headband, while half-retreating, says:

Tomekō: 'Hey, you lot, clear the path quickly. It will be troublesome if you get in the way when the time comes.'

Matsutakahiko: 'What is it, you? You are squaring your shoulders and rolling up your sleeves, putting in quite an effort. If you exert all your strength like that now, you will run out of the raw material for your energy. Why don't you settle down first?'

Tomekō softens his posture slightly and, feeling awkward, quietly hides his rolled-up sleeves.

Mayst Thou save Tomekō and the other Satobito in peace. Ah, Tomekō, and you Satobito, bring Tomohiko and come quickly. The Kamuzukasa of the Maura of Ten is waiting, adjusting his collar. Ah, Kamunagara, Kamunagara. May the Mitama be blessed.

Tomohiko of the Brahmin sect, who was entrenched in the detached room of Tomekō, spitting out strange flames day and night and wrapping the Satobito in smoke, was peering from a sunny corner window, waiting impatiently for the return of the messenger he had sent to the thatched hut of Matsutakahiko.
