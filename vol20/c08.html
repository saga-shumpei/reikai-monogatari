Chapter 8: 心の Ogre [670]

心の鬼〔670〕

§ 1

Munehiko bade farewell to his siblings, ascended to the sacred site, and was commanded by Kotoyoriwakenomikoto to serve as a glorious Sendenshi. With a heart full of joy, he returned to the village of Uzuyama, performed a Haukokusai before the shrine of Takeshi, and bid farewell to the villagers. He then prepared his travel gear and set out on his first journey of Senden to conquer the Magami who had entrenched themselves deep in the mountains of Mikunigadake.

Tomekō and Tagosaku had gone ahead and were waiting at the edge of the village.

Munehiko: 'Are you not my brother-in-law Tagosaku? Oh, Tomekō-sama, you are there too. Where are you going?'

Tagosaku: 'Please, take me with you as your companion on this Senden journey for just two or three days. I consulted with Tomekō-sama and we have been lying in wait for you here.'

Munehiko: 'I appreciate the offer, but I have received word from Kotoyoriwakenomikoto-sama that the Sendenshi must travel alone. If it were any other matter, I would gladly go with you, but as this is my first Senden mission, I must decline your kind offer, however difficult it may be.'

Tagosaku: 'If I am not a Sendenshi, there should be no harm in accompanying you as a believer, should there?'

Tomekō: 'Please, just two or three days will suffice, so do take us with you.'

Munehiko: 'If you go on your own accord using your own feet, there is no harm in it, as we are traveling the same path... However, as a Sendenshi, Munehiko must travel alone from beginning to end.'

Tomekō: 'Where are you headed?'

Munehiko: 'Let me see. Kotoyoriwakenomikoto-sama told me to cross Akashi Pass, pass through Yamakuni, and conquer the demons of Mikunigadake. It must be a very high mountain, I suppose.'

Tomekō: 'It is a high mountain that spans the three provinces of Omi, Wakasa, and Taniwa. They say it is inhabited by fierce beasts, monkeys, and giant serpents.'

Tagosaku: 'Hearing that, I cannot let you go alone. Please, let us accompany you.'

Munehiko: 'That is absolutely out of the question.'

He shook his head repeatedly and walked on ahead.

It was the beginning of autumn, and due to the thick fog for which Taniwa is famous, one could not see even six feet ahead. Munehiko quickened his pace and advanced toward Akashi Pass. The two men, hidden by the fog, hurried ahead and, at a great waterfall falling at the foot of Akashi Pass, stripped naked and waited for Munehiko to approach while performing Misogi. Munehiko, obstructed by the fog, did not notice the two men being struck by the waterfall.

Munehiko: 'I hear a tremendous sound of water, it seems.'

He whispered to himself and climbed the slope. The two men did not notice at all that Munehiko was passing just two or three ken ahead of them. Munehiko reached the summit of Akashi Pass. The fog filled the valleys, and only the peaks of the high mountains floated here and there like a painting.

§ 2

Munehiko: 'Ah, how beautiful the scenery of this sea of mist is; it is a sight that could never be seen in the land of Kii. The feeling of gazing upon this scenery is exactly like the mood of wandering in the Daiichitengoku.'

He is speaking in a Monorogu.

Just then, a woman of about Shijifu, looking worn out and in a shabby Minari, appeared as if floating out from the mist, walking briskly.

Munehiko: 'Yo, a strange woman has come along. She is walking as if she is in a great hurry; there seems to be some Yōsu to this. I shall try asking her when she approaches Koko.'

He thinks this in his heart. The woman notices Munehiko's figure, stops abruptly, and stares at Konata with suspicious, wide-open eyes.

Munehiko: 'Anata, you are a woman all Hitori, crossing this high mountain pass; Doko are you going?'

The woman, looking frightened, says:

Woman: 'Hai, I am a person from a small village called Kumada below here. I am going to the Akashi waterfall to be struck by the water from now.'

Munehiko: 'Doko is this place called Akashi waterfall?'

Woman: 'It is located about Shichihatsuchiyō down this mountain.'

Munehiko: 'I also just climbed up this slope, but because of the deep Fukagiri, I did not notice it. Dauri, I thought there was a Kashiyo where I heard the Minaoto. And to go to be struck by the waterfall must have some deep Wake; tell me about it.'

Woman: 'Hai, my husband is a person named Harahiko, and for two or three years he has been ailing, and lately it has become a very serious illness. Therefore, I am going to immerse my body in the waterfall, wishing to pray to the God of Akashi waterfall to save my husband's illness.'

Munehiko: 'What kind of illness is it? Depending on the circumstances, I am thinking of praying to the God to help him...'

Woman: 'Hai, thank you very much. When night comes, various things appear and torment him. Each time that happens, he breaks out in a cold sweat and grows thinner day by day; now he has become so shabby that he looks like nothing but skin and bones.'

Munehiko: 'That must be an illness caused by a Mononoke. Come, I will examine him once, so please guide me.'

Woman: 'That is thank you very much. From here, we go down this mountain slope, and in a small village about four or five Chō away, my thatched hut stands at the foot of the mountain. I beg for your trouble.'

She stands in front and guides him. They finally arrived at the woman's house. Under a forest of large trees, there is a fairly large house with a Kabukimon gate; that is this woman's residence.

§ 3

Woman: 'It is but a shabby Abaraya, but please, do come in.'

She bowed and entered the house. The voice of her husband Harahiko, suffering as he was tormented by something, leaked even to the outside. The woman muttered in a low voice, 'He has been possessed again,' and rushed into the house in a panic, running to the bedside of the sick man. Munehiko stepped over the threshold a little later, climbed onto the Shiyōjiyō, and began to recite the Amatsunorito; upon this, the sick man let out cries of even greater agony and began to writhe about. Four or five villagers were waiting in the next room, whispering about something. As soon as they heard the sound of the Norito, two or three men appeared on the scene.

Man: 'I do not know who you are, but you must be the one whom Otsuyu-sama brought back with her; come, please, do come this way and rest.'

Munehiko said, 'Gomen,' and as he was invited, he stepped into the room and sat down. Though he could not hear exactly what was being said, the sick man was shouting very loudly at Otsuyu. Hearing this voice, Munehiko turned to the villagers.

Munehiko: 'Is the illness always like that?'

Villager A: 'Since four or five days ago, it has become even more violent. He started saying, "Tago is coming, Tago is coming," and... he suffers terribly. And then, he sometimes recovers as if nothing had happened, as if he had been telling a lie. It is an illness with no known cause... they say it is the curse of a dead spirit.'

Munehiko: 'The curse of a dead spirit... do you have any idea why that might be?'

Villager A: 'We villagers did not know the cause of the illness at all at first, but lately we have begun to understand that it is a dead spirit. He keeps raving that it will take his life within these two or three days, and his struggling is truly terrible.'

Villager B: 'It seems the master of this house, Harahiko, is from the Kamigata region, but although it has been thirteen years since he became the husband of Otsuyu-san, he has never revealed his background, so we did not know where he was from or what he had been doing. But listening to the delirious ravings of the sick man, though I cannot say it in a loud voice, it seems this man is a thief who killed someone. And he himself is revealing that the spirit of the man he killed is cursing him. The retribution of Heaven is a fearful thing, indeed.'

Munehiko: 'Human beings commit sins quite often without even realizing it; those who kill people, set fires, or commit crimes such as robbery and fraud are truly to be detested for the sake of the world. However, there is a saying that one should hate the sin but not the person; the impartial and selfless Lord God would likely not punish the physical body. He is surely being tormented by his own sins. If only his sins could be removed, Harahiko-sama would surely recover before long. In this world, there are many sinners invisible to the human eye, and among them, the heaviest sins are those of scholars and religious leaders. Those who cloud, rot, and kill the magnificent soul received from the Lord God—those who spread erroneous theories, those who misunderstand the heart of the Lord God and propagate it as if it were true, or those pseudo-religious leaders and pseudo-scholars who imitate the Lord God—are the ones committing the gravest sins against the Kingdom of God.'

Kō: 'Hey, is that so? Where are the sins committed in the heart, or the sins of scholars and religious figures, judged as good or evil?'

Munehiko: 'It is impossible for incomplete humans to judge things like good and evil, or merit and demerit. That is why Kami appear in the world to distinguish between good and evil; humans have no choice but to interpret everything with good intentions, pray to the Nahohi-no-Kami, and have their sins seen anew, heard anew, and rectified anew by Kamunahohi and Ōnahohi. We often unknowingly contain grave sins within the things we do every day, thinking they are for the sake of the nation, for the sake of the Way, and for the sake of society. If one says that, one must carry out what one believes to be good to the very end, or one cannot fulfill one's heavenly duty as an administrator of the governance of heaven and earth; yet if one remains still, saying one must not commit sins, one commits the great sin of a sluggard. Therefore, one has no choice but to carry out what one believes to be the best to the very end, and morning and evening, recite the Norito and pray to Kami to see and hear our sins anew.'

Otsu: 'Current laws only punish sins of action and do not punish sins of the spirit. If, by any chance, a soul commits a sin and the body is used as a tool, is it not a truly extreme contradiction from the perspective of the divine world that the body is still treated as the sinner?'

Munehiko: 'That is where you are human. In any case, laws treat everything that is inconvenient for the life of humans among one another as a sin... Even if one becomes a sinner under the law, there are times when one is praised in the divine world for doing excellent service; and conversely, things recognized as exemplary conduct under the law are sometimes recognized as great sins in the divine world. That is why, unless Kami appear and judge, it is impossible for humans, in their station, to distinguish between good and evil with absolute fairness. Furthermore, human laws and the sanctioning power of the state are finite, not absolute. When Mount Asama erupts and ravages forests and fields, destroying houses; when Sakurajima explodes and destroys many human lives; when the catfish of earthquakes leaps, turning mountains into seas and seas into mountains, burning houses, killing people, and plundering all property—there is no place to sue Mount Asama, earthquakes, or Sakurajima as defendants under laws made by humans, no prison to throw them into, and no way to put them on trial. It is utterly futile. There is no choice but to leave everything to the great will of Kami.'

Just as they were speaking, the Biyōnin in the next room let out a hideous voice.

Munehiko: 'Strange, was there a person called Tagosaku in this area?'

Otsu: 'When you say Tagosaku, it is a Gamei for all of us. Tagosaku is like a Daimeishi for humans who till the Tahata, and Mokubee is for those who enter the Sanrin to work as Kikori or cut Takimono, so there are so many Tagosakus that I cannot tell who was killed.'

Munehiko: 'I understand that, but is there not a man specifically named Tagosaku?'

Kō and Otsu simultaneously,

Kō, Otsu: 'Well, we have not heard of one. It is said that there is a great Awatemono in the village of Uzuyama who is called Tagosaku, but whether that is his Jitsusai name or just an Itsupanteki name for a Hiyakushiyō, that is not Hatsukiri. In this area, we call a man who is a bit flustered and makes mistakes a fellow like the Tagosaku of Uzuyama; we have only heard of it vaguely, and we do not even know if such a person actually exists or not.'

Harahiko: 'Tagosaku-dono, the ghost, I was wrong, I was wrong, please spare me... What? Why should I spare a wicked man like you? You are the one who took my Inochi. I will dwell in your body, eat your intestines, gouge out your lungs, twist your stomach, and torment you, torment you, and kill you by inches. Do you think I will let this grudge go unavenged?'

Harahiko, his wife, and all the villagers realized from the bottom of their hearts the truth that the most fearful sins arise from Shifuchiyakushin, and that such sins instantly turn into evil spirits that torment one's own body. The small village of Kumada, as a whole, came to believe in the teachings of Munehiko and eventually became followers of the Sangyōkyō.
