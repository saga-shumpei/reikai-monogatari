Chapter 5: 親不知 [667]

親不知〔667〕

§ 1

The waves of Kogane flow through the valley between the mountains of Uzuyama, winding along the Uzu River. As the water warms, the Magohi, Higohi, Funa, and Moroko swim upstream. The season for Ayu has finally arrived, and fishermen are here and there. Among them, a white-haired, strange-looking old man, taller than the rest and possessing an indefinable strength, stands with a thin rod to which he has attached bait, intending to spend the long spring day enjoying fishing. Just as he is doing so, two people wearing Minokasa come walking up along the riverbank. They wear Sedge Ogasa inscribed with 'Shiyogiyōmujiyō Zeshiyōmetsupofu Shiyōmetsumetsui Jiyakumetsuiraku,' and are supported by Kongauzue.

They stand before the fishing Okina, and with an Ahauzura, ask, 'Are you catching anything?' The Okina is so absorbed in his fishing that he does not even look their way, which is frustrating. The Giyōjiya walks right up to his side, taps him on the back, and says, 'Hey, hey, old man,' and asks again, 'Are you catching anything?' The Okina replies, 'I am a solitary person in this miserable Ukiyo. My old woman fell into the river and became Jiyakumetsuiraku. Lamenting a life that cannot escape the principle of Zeshiyōmetsupofu in this Shiyōmiyōmujiyō world, I am Matsutakahiko-okina, spending my remaining days by this river. You are a married couple of Shugenja. Originally, this world has no east or west; where is there a north or south? This is the universe. Because you are lost, it is the castle of the three realms; because you are enlightened, it is the emptiness of the ten directions.'

'Eating, excreting, sleeping, and waking; after that, there is only death. This is the path of human life. Why have you become a Giyōjiya, despite your young age? There must be a reason for this. Tell me the details,' he urges. The young man removes his hat, casts off his Minokasa, sits down heavily on the riverbank, wipes his eyes, and says, 'We are Shugenja of the Brahman religion, Munehiko and Okatsu. In the sadness of being predeceased by our only beloved child, we vowed to pray for the baby's happiness in the next world. My wife and I, bound for two lives, set out on a journey, leaving our fate to our feet, wandering like clouds and water, with no fixed destination, using grass as our pillow. Since the day we left, we have been a married couple living in sorrow for three years.'

'The horse of time flows away, abandoning me. The end of us two is like the sound of the Sayanokinu. The sound of the water of the Uzuyama River is also a sad, heartless cry. Hearing that the spirits of the Amatsu-mikami dwell in the fish that float and play in the rivers and seas, as we protect the teachings of universal love, the sight of the Okina fishing makes the feeling of Shiyōgiyōmujiyō deep. Although it is said to be the custom of the world that the living must die and those who meet must part, the lamentation of the caught fish is right before my eyes. It is painful for me to see. I wish to pray for the happiness of my child by mourning the soul of that fish, and Matsutakahiko, with all his heart, fished it up.'

Matsutakahiko, seeing the Nakigara of crucian carp and Moroko, joined his Riyōte in prayer. He cast aside his fishing rod, released the fish he had caught into the shallows of the river, and with tears flowing, walked dejectedly toward the Abaraya. Munehiko and Okatsu, the Riyōnin, while darkened by tears of Hiai, followed the Toiki-sighing Okina as they searched for him.

Munehiko and Okatsu, the Riyōnin, finally found the Abaraya built on the Kahaberi and called out from outside the door, 'Is anyone there? Is anyone there?' From within, the Okina from before replied,

Okina: 'You must be the pilgrim of the Brahman Kyō I met just before. I dislike the Brahman Kyō. However, there is something in what you said earlier that makes me tilt my head and think. This village was composed only of believers of the Brahman Kyō, but just about a year ago, the entire village converted to Ananai-kyō, so I cannot let you enter through the front. But since this is a solitary house on the Kahaberi and no one is watching, please enter quietly. I am the one who serves as the Kamunushi of the Takeshi-no-miya in this village. Having been predeceased by my old woman, I am so lonely that I enjoy fishing Mainichihinichi, offering fresh fish to her Reizen as a small comfort. Regarding that, there is something I wish to ask you. Come, come, please enter.'

Munehiko: 'Whether it be the Brahman Kyō or Ananai-kyō, there should not be two kinds of truth. From the beginning of the creation, it has been firmly established that fire is hot and water is cold. If you reject the Brahman Kyō to that extent, I shall stop entering your house. Come, Okatsu, let us go.'

Matsutakahiko: 'You are young, so you get angry immediately, but why not sit down, have some tea, calm your spirits, and exchange words? I, too, live alone, and like a willow on the riverbank, I am one who spends my life worrying while watching the flow of the water.'

Okatsu: 'Munehiko-sama, as the Ojii-sama says, shall we have a cup of tea?'

Munehiko: 'That is true. In that case, I shall concede and enter.'

Matsutakahiko: 'Come, come, please enter... (in a low voice) ...The fellows of the Brahman Kyō are stubborn to the end.'

So muttering, he poured tea made of shinobugusa from a pitch-black earthenware teapot and offered it to them.

Matsutakahiko: 'Looking at you, you appear to be a young couple, but you have managed to set out on such a journey; there must be a deep reason for this, and I would like you to tell me about it.'

Munehiko: 'In truth, I have also come to fear the afterlife, so to atone for my sins, I became a pilgrim and have been visiting sacred mountains and holy sites in various regions; today marks almost three years since I began traveling around this Onokorojima. Though I have become as docile as a cat now, I was once a notoriously wicked man. I would take a wife, strip her naked, and drive her out; I would become a forced son-in-law, only to ruin that household; I have exhausted every possible evil deed, such as being a home-wrecker who made wives weep, a destroyer of families, and one who swindled widows and defaulted on debts. Eventually, my last wife, distressed by my dissolute conduct, plunged into the Tameike pond behind our house, and with a splash and bubbles rising on the water, she attained Jiyakumetsuiraku. Immediately after that, I took this Okatsu as my wife, and while we were living harmoniously, counting on the assets of my adoptive family and drinking nothing but sake from morning till night, a pale blue fire would begin to burn from the altar where I enshrined the spirit of my wife around midnight. When we, as a couple, would cover ourselves with our bedclothes and tremble, holding our breath, a cold hand would stroke our faces—how repulsive it was. I realized this was the ghost of my first wife, Okuni, and though I thought to offer an apology, for some reason, no voice would come out. Throughout the long night, there was a loathsome sound, and those cold hands would stroke us. Thinking I could not bear this, I chanted the sutras of Brahmanism from morning till night, and thanks to that, the hauntings at the altar ceased that night. While things were like this, there came a sound of rain shutters being beaten with a thud, and that, too, sounded like the voice of my dead wife. When I peered stealthily through the window, thick white smoke was rising from the garden pond where Okuni had fallen, and the pale face of my wife with disheveled hair was staring resentfully into the house. I begged my wife, saying, "Please, separate from me; if you do, perhaps Okuni will also find liberation," but such is the deep-seated attachment of this Okatsu that she would not leave me no matter what. She would spit out such repulsive things as, "If you want to sever our ties, then do it yourself. I will throw myself into the pond, become a ghost, and organize a ghost alliance with Okuni to attack you." I could no longer stay in the house, so I disguised myself in pilgrim's attire and fled my home. About a year later, in the spring, as I was passing in front of a roadside shrine, I saw a woman suffering from a stomach cramp. I approached her, saying, "Hey, young lady, you must be in great distress at this deserted roadside shrine; let me nurse you," but to my surprise, this tenacious Okatsu had disguised herself as a pilgrim and was searching for my whereabouts, and I ran right into her. Ah, what a terrible way to be loved; it was as if I had been targeted by a snake. If I had known it would come to this, I should have just passed by in silence. Being a mere mortal, not a god... ah, there is no help for it, I sighed, looking up at the heavens. She should have just died, but the moment that wretch Okatsu saw my face, she forgot all about her cramps and blurted out, "Ouch, ouch... it hurts, but what hurts most is that I wanted to see you." I am walking along with her like this.'
