Chapter 19: The First Heaven [586]

第一天国〔586〕

§ 1

The rocky cave of Hisakata Takaamahara hath opened, and here the Ametsuchi, the hundred Kamitachi, stand forth in valor. Oh, how wondrous! Oh, how clear and bright! The Kazūta of Ame resounds in joyous clamor, and the flower of Kotoba begins to bloom. Though the darkness of Tokoyo hath cleared, the inner heart remains yet unclouded. Kamususanō no Ōkami hath taken upon His own person the Tsumitoga of the hundred Shinjin of Ametsuchi, even unto the Momochiyorozu. As the pitiless storm blows, He beareth the Chikura no Okido upon His back, and leaving Takaamahara behind, He crosseth the Manai of Ame, passeth over Morokoshiyama, the plains of Kara, and the land of Tsuki, until He reacheth the land known as the land of Himitsu, where Kauzan surround the Yomo.

In the land of Chibetsuto, deep in Yuishiyo, throughout every mountain, field, and village, He causeth His great divine majesty to shine. Advancing further, He passeth the land of Fusa and the capital of Taru, where hundreds of mountains tower, pressing against the clouds, here and there. Upon the mountain range of Ubusuna, and before the mountain of Kajika, buffeted by the rough winds of the world, He ascendeth with hurried steps. Upon the summit of Mount Ubusuna, choosing a pure place where the scenery of Yomo is beautiful, He buildeth a palace of eight fathoms, decreeing it a dwelling for a thousand generations, a humble abode to endure this world. As black clouds gather in Yomo, and in a world where black and white cannot be distinguished, the divine majesty of the Kami groweth ever higher.

Secretly, He illumineth Yomo. Yearning for that divine virtue, the true Shinjin who appear upon the ridges of the mountains and the shallows of the rivers, from far and near, in secret and in silence, contend with one another, saying, 'I shall go, I shall go.' How noble it is to seek Him.

Kototamawake no Mikoto, the divided spirit of Toyokunihime no Kami, the original ancestor of Mizutama, who in ancient times appeared in the holy land of Jerusalem as the Kami of Sakimitama, prior to the retirement of Kuninotokotachi no Ōkami, bore the Chikura no Okido and once departed for the Yūkai. Renaming Himself Sukunahiko no Kami, He guarded the land of Tokoyo eternally. However, hearing that Kamususanō no Ōkami, the original ancestor of Mizutama, had departed Takaamahara and embarked upon a journey of lonely wandering through the lands of Toyoashihara, His heart found no peace. Appearing once more in this world, He borrowed the womb of a lowly person and became Kotoyoriwake no Mikoto. Determining Tamahiko, the descendant of the spirit of Moritakahiko, to be His attendant Kami, and accompanied by the three Kami—Itsuhiko of the land of Tokoyo, and Kusunohiko who saveth the people of the world—He crossed the vast waves of the Taiping Ocean, landed at the port of Furu in the land of Tsuki, traversed the great land of India, and arrived at the capital of Taru in the land of Fusa, where His steps were troubled.

Akatsukatsu no Mikoto appeared in the capital of Taru, the chief city of the land of Fusa, as Hinodewake no Kami, and was conducting the divine government. Kotoyoriwake no Mikoto met with Hinodewake no Kami of the capital of Taru, was taught the place of retirement of Kamususanō no Ōkami, and with joy and courage, together with Tamahiko, Itsuhiko, and Kusunohiko, mounted their steeds and crossed the Kajika Pass. Upon the unexpected steep slopes and difficult roads, even the swift horses could scarce advance, and risking the danger of the steeds falling time and again, they proceeded slowly toward the mountain summit.

§ 2

The mountain range throughout this land is beset by fierce winds and irregular cold and heat, where the growth of a hundred grasses and trees is poor; as far as the eye can see, jagged rocky mountains and barren peaks rise and fall here and there, presenting a prospect that is a peerless spectacle under heaven.

Kamususanō no Ōkami built a palace upon the high plain of the Iso at the summit of the Ubusuna mountain range, and desiring to bring the Shinjin of the four quarters to Kotomukeyaha, He transformed His Misugata in a thousand and ten thousand ways, established this palace as His Honkiyo, and caused Yasotakerunokami to guard it firmly; meanwhile, He Himself bore the name of a Zainin upon the surface, breaking His heart and afflicting His body to eradicate the great serpents, evil demons, and ugly gods that coiled within the Ōyashimaguni, appearing and disappearing with infinite transformation, and thus He continued to protect the Imperial Land. Kotoyoriwake no Mikoto, wishing to have an audience with the Son and to comfort His Great Heart, ventured from the sincerity of his devotion to the Son, braving a hundred thousand hardships to come and seek Him here. The proper path is to cross over the Kajika Pass and traverse the plains once more to reach the Ubusuna mountain range. As the party of four, led by Kotoyoriwake, were descending a slope as steep as a standing board, astride their steeds, pulling tight the reins with cries of 'Hai, hai-hai,' they were suddenly buffeted by a mountain gale of record-breaking intensity, and together with their horses, they fell helplessly into the thousand-fathom abyss of the Kajika Pass, sustaining many wounds; thus, the party of four lay at the bottom of the valley of the Kajika Pass, groaning in agony from their grievous injuries, a truly piteous sight.

This valley is a famous site for the Kajika. The voices of the Kajika are elegant both near and far, as if golden bells were being shaken or a koto were being played, filled with such refined charm that one might suspect celestial beings and heavenly maidens had come to perform beautiful music. As the party of Kotoyoriwake scooped up the valley water and listened to the voices of the Kajika, drinking deeply to quench their parched throats to their hearts' content, before they knew it, their lives—the threads of jewels—vanished, sent off by the voices of the Kajika together with the sound of the white-foaming valley water, a truly sorrowful end.

In the empty sky of their journey, unable to distinguish whether it were a dream or reality, the party of Kotoyoriwake no Mikoto, feeling as if they were treading a path of dreams sent off by the cool voices of the Kajika, were lifted from the earth like leaves blown by the wind, enveloped in five-colored clouds in the mid-air, and drifted toward the east at the mercy of the wind.

Before they knew it, the figures of the party were set down by the side of a great river, where the scenery at the foot of a certain high mountain was most excellent.

Kotoyoriwake: 'O Tamahiko, in which direction might the palace of Kamususanō no Ōkami lie? This appears to be the foot of the Kajika Pass and the bank of the Kajika River. Blown about by the violent wind, we were helplessly scattered to this mountain foot, and I have come to feel a certain mysterious sensation; what do you think?'

Tamahiko said, 'Just as you have said, no sooner did I think that we were cast down into the abyss of a thousand fathoms, blown by the violent winds of Kajikatauge, than I felt as if I were a leaf scattered by the gale; and while my soul drifted, light as air, soaring high through the Ōzora toward the east, behold, a wonder! We, the company, have arrived at the riverbank of a landscape of Fūkuōmeibi at the foot of a mountain whose name we know not. As we ponder this deeply, it is surely not the valley of Kajikatauge; it seems rather to be the Chiushinten of Onokorojima.'

Izuhiko replied, 'Indeed, as Tamahiko says, the scenery of these surroundings is beyond my Gaten, and the Yōsu differs greatly from that of the Genkai; it is likely that this place is none other than Tengoku.'

Kusuhiko said, 'It is surely without Machigahi the Tengoku. Behold how the Kariyōbinga, in Kazukagi, dance in such a frenzy; the wind that blows plays music of exquisite beauty, and the air is fragrant, mingled with the scent of plum blossoms; there is nothing that we see or hear that does not bestow upon us a sense of delight. ...Pray, Kotoyoriwake-no-Mikoto, do not be troubled; the Ōkami has perceived your sincerity and has surely guided you to such a Tengoku as this.'

At the very moment he spoke, a divine being descended to this place, riding upon the Heavenly Rock Boat, accompanied by a flash of light that shook the heavens. The Heavenly Rock Boat descended silently before the company. It was a magnificent vessel, adorned with gold, silver, jewels, lapis lazuli, crystal, agate, pearls, and coral. When one looked at its wings, they were made of a fabric neither of silk nor of hair, but of a strange, soft, yet strong material. The instant one stretched out a hand to stroke these wings, an indescribable and exquisite sound was emitted. As Tamahiko stroked the fabric stretched across the wings to the right and left, various beautiful sounds were heard, as if from the disc of an elaborate phonograph. At this time, eight children appeared from within the Rock Boat; their hair was red and long, and they had small wings about their shoulders; their teeth were dyed black like wet crows, their lips were crimson, their eyes dripped with green, and while their peach-colored cheeks were filled with infinite smiles, these children, who appeared to be five or six years of age, appeared before Kotoyoriwake-no-Mikoto and, in thin, cool voices, said:

'Are you not Kotoyoriwake-no-Mikoto, the divided spirit of Mizutama, born in the land of Tokoyo? I have come from Takamagahara, having received the command of the Ōkami, to welcome you; come, come, pray board this vessel quickly.'

As he spoke with lowered voice and profound reverence, Mikoto, his heart captivated by this beautiful vessel, approached it with an indifferent air; and as he drew near, the exquisite wings equipped beside the Ihafune enveloped the Shintai of Mikoto and bore him into the Mifune. In the moment that a Bimeu Onkiyō resounded, the Ihafune departed from the Chijiyō and, tracing a gentle circle, ascended into the Kūchiu. The Sannin, stunned by this Totsuzen Dekigoto, could only gaze upward at the sky in Bauzen. The Ihafune soared high into the Kūchiu, turned its Senshiyu, and leaving a ribbon-like Kuwasen in the Chiukū, proceeded Iuiu with the light of the moon as its Meate, until at last its form was no longer visible to the eye.

Tamahiko said: 'Having traveled Harubaru from the land of Tokoyo, crossing the Yahoji of salt, battling every manner of Kannan, exposed to Amekaze, and arriving at the capital of Fusa in sweat and tears, the joy I felt upon reaching the summit of Kajika Pass and gazing upon the scenery in all directions, after the weariness of my journey was utterly forgotten through the compassionate Kotoba of the Hinodewake no Kami, was beyond all comparison. The feeling when I descended the precipitous slope, steep as a wooden wall, while astride my steed, was filled with such agony and wonder as if taking a single leap into the path of hell; yet, as I recited the divine Kotoba within my heart, forgetting my pain with every step and advancing with joy in the thought that I might soon be granted an audience with the revered Kami Susanoo no Mikoto, I was suddenly buffeted by a mountain gale, and thinking my body had fallen into a thousand-fathom ravine and been shattered, how could I have foreseen this flight through the Kūchiu with miraculous power, surrendering myself to the wind and clouds? Little did I expect to be set down upon such a beautiful riverbank. No matter how I ponder it, this cannot be the manifest world; and even as I rejoiced, the joy and sorrow mingled in our hearts—how could we feel otherwise?—at the sight of Kotoyoriwake no Mikoto, whom we hold so dear, ascending high into the Chiukū to the divine land of the moon in the ship sent to welcome him; ah, what are we to do?'

Itsuhiko replied: 'There is naught to do but leave all things to the will of the Kami. It appears that Kotoyoriwake no Mikoto has ascended to the most solemn heaven to sit at the right hand of the Ōkami and fulfill the office of inquiring into the governance of the Chijiyō. Let us now abandon our concern for Kotoyoriwake no Mikoto and advance as far as our feet will carry us, shall we not, Kusunohiko-san?'

Kusunohiko replied: 'That is so; and yet, what a pleasant place this is. My spirit feels so buoyant that I have no desire to sit and rest; come, let us hasten our advance.'

He walked ahead and set forth. The shallow and wide Ōkaha flowed with the waters of Suishiyō, moving with gentle pace.

The Sannin spoke: 'Ah, how beautiful this water is; surely this must be the Mashimizu of Inochi. Shall we not scoop up a cupful and partake? As we each bear many wounds upon our Shintai, perhaps if we were to immerse ourselves in this river, this Itami might be healed.'

So saying, they descended the bank at a leisurely pace, and becoming Matsupadaka, they Tobiko into the river with a splash.

The clear Kahamizu, with the Kinginshiyoku Jiyari upon the Kahazoko, sparkled in the reflection of the Nitsukuō; such was its beauty that the Sannin sat down firmly in the Mannaka of the river.
