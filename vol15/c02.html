Chapter 2: Peril on the Road [569]

第2章　途上の変〔569〕

§ 1

Futodama-no-Mikoto, Yasuhiko, Kunihiko, and Michihiko inclined their heads at the clamorous sounds beyond the river, and for a while were lost in thought.

Futodama-no-Mikoto: "Takahiko, Mozuhiko, ye surely know well the state of Ken'onkyō. What is that clamorous noise? Explain it point by point."

Mozuhiko: "That noise, so far as I can guess, must be the troops under Onikumohiko, the chief of Ken'onkyō, coming to attack this side with the plan of seizing you, sirs. Save us as quickly as may be and withdraw from this place. It would be a sorry thing for you, who are no less than divine priests of the Anai Teaching, to be destroyed by nameless evil gods. Quickly, from this place—"

So he urged them again and again.

Michihiko: "What! To sheathe the spear at the sight of the foe, roll up the banners, and shamelessly run away is not the part of a man. For us there are no two letters of 'retreat'; there is only the one letter of 'advance.' Whatever mighty foe may appear, we, by the loving protection of God, will neither flinch nor fear, but press our steps forward and storm the enemy's stronghold. Life or death, victory or defeat, is no concern of ours."

Fearsome was the look of courage upon his face.

Yasuhiko: "Ho! The enemy's vanguard has appeared on the opposite shore, swarming like ants into a black mountain. Come, now has arrived the season for testing our divine power. Takahiko, Mozuhiko, make ready the boat!"

Mozuhiko: "The boat can be made ready at any time; but it is a great enemy, as you see. Even though you had divine valor to crush demon gods, it is few against many; and above all, our side bears not an inch of steel, while the foe comes pressing on with every kind of choice weapon. The issue of victory and defeat is plain without fighting. If time is let slip, it is clearer than seeing fire that they will cross this muddy stream and take us alive. Withdraw, I pray, devise a plan at leisure, and await the time to return in force."

Futodama-no-Mikoto opened his mouth wide and laughed aloud:

Futodama-no-Mikoto: "Ahahahaha! Fortune lies with Heaven. By the power of the Kototama of good words and fair speech, I will pacify through the Word the oncoming foe one after another and restore the Ken'onkyō of old. 'He who strikes first masters the other,' they say; to hesitate and hang back at this juncture is contrary to the divine will."

No sooner had he spoken than he leapt nimbly into the boat. The five of them, perforce, followed Futodama-no-Mikoto and took their places in the boat. When they had advanced almost to midstream of the River Eden, broad as it was, Mozuhiko was shot through the breast by the swift arrows falling like rain from the opposite shore, and at once tumbled into the water. Takahiko, seeing this, was greatly startled and flung himself into the water with a great splash. The four missionaries who remained knew nothing of the ways of this river's waters, and the boat, driven at once by the muddy current, went flowing downstream at the mercy of the flood, swift as a shot arrow. The enemy's arrows poured down like rain. Suddenly the boat struck a rock in the river and was dashed into splinters.

§ 2

Futodama-no-Mikoto barely reached the opposite shore. Yasuhiko, Kunihiko, and Michihiko were swallowed by the muddy stream and their whereabouts became unknown. Alas, what fate befell the three?

Futodama-no-Mikoto wrung out his wet garments and dried them in the sun, and then, singing a missionary song at his ease, went in alone toward the enemy's den in Ken'onkyō. The sun sank behind the western hills, the twilight sky grew dark, and a night so black that not a single star could be seen enfolded him moment by moment. The voice of the missionary song threaded through the darkness and echoed far and near. At that moment there came a sound as though heaven and earth were splitting, and even as he looked, a great flaming light fell before his eyes. Suddenly he saw a god-man, clear of brow and eye and fair of countenance, who, sending forth from his body a fiery vapor like lightning, faced Futodama-no-Mikoto and said:

"I am Ikutsuhikone-no-Kami, the fourth child of Amaterasu-Ōmikami. That thou shouldst boldly come alone into the stronghold of the wicked and lawless Baramon is the height of recklessness. It is more perilous than to hug a rock and cast oneself into the sea. Turn back without a moment's delay by the way thou camest."

Futodama-no-Mikoto: "That thou art Ikutsuhikone-no-Kami is surely an utter lie. Thou must be a transformation of the Eight-Forked Serpent that possesses Onikumohiko, or a guise of the Golden-Haired Nine-Tailed Fox, or a shape of some evil demon. I am, unworthy though I be, a divine messenger of the Great God; and to restore this Ken'onkyō to the heavenly paradise of old is a great mission entrusted to me by the Great God. Even should the divine host unhappily fare ill, that is the decree of Heaven. I have no ears to hear thy needless meddling."

And he pressed straight forward along the dark road. Ikutsuhikone-no-Kami said:

"Then do as thou wilt."

Even as he seemed to speak, his form vanished in an instant, and there was heard only the rustling sound of the storm passing over the mountain ridges. Futodama-no-Mikoto at length grew used to the darkness, and was able, though dimly, to grope his way forward.

At this moment the moon of the thirteenth night opened the doors of the clouds and began to shine. Futodama-no-Mikoto, again singing a missionary song, went on toward the enemy's citadel. From the far side some tens of black shapes appeared, surrounded the lone Futodama-no-Mikoto from before and behind, left and right, and—

Tobihiko: "Ho! I am a follower of Ōkuniwake-no-Mikoto, Tobihiko, the foremost hero and champion of Ken'onkyō. Thou art a great fool careless of thy life, recklessly to come alone into Ken'onkyō. Come, yield thy hands quietly to the bonds!"

No sooner had he spoken than they came pressing in from all four sides, flashing their spear-points in the moonlight. Futodama-no-Mikoto, caught with no way forward or back, drew from his bosom a short-handled Futodama-gushi, the sacred tamagushi wand, and waved it left, right, and left—when lo, beyond all expectation, Tobihiko and the black shapes with him vanished as though wiped away, leaving not so much as a speck of dust.

Futodama-no-Mikoto: "Ahahahaha! All the schemes of evil gods are frail like this. Have they so vanished by the divine power of the Futodama-gushi that I bear? Ah, thanks be, thanks be, O Great God of the Anai Teaching!"

And he prostrated himself upon the ground and gave thanks for the divine grace. When Futodama-no-Mikoto suddenly raised his head—what could this be?—his wife Matsuyo-hime, whom he had left upon Mount Caucasus, and his only and dearest daughter Terutae-hime, who guarded the Garden of Eden, bound hand and arm tightly behind their backs and gagged, were being dragged along by many demon-like guards, and were about to pass dejectedly before him, their eyes somewhat downcast. Futodama-no-Mikoto started in alarm and, holding his breath and opening wide his eyes, gazed upon the faces of the two. Matsuyo-hime and Terutae-hime, perhaps because they were gagged, blinked their eyes toward him as though appealing about something. At this moment a huge man wearing a black hood, goggling eyes like a pond-snail's, said:

"Ho! Art thou not Futodama-no-Mikoto, divine priest of the Anai Teaching? If thou wilt straightway cross this river and never again spy upon Ken'onkyō, I will pardon thy wife and child. But if, unyielding even to this, thou press ever further toward Ken'onkyō, I will torment thy dearest wife and child to death here on this very spot. What is thine answer?"

Futodama-no-Mikoto: "Why, as to that……"

The man: "Come, come, how is it? Let me hear thine answer!"

Futodama-no-Mikoto: "Why, as to that……"

The man: "Come, come, come!"

So they bandied words. At this moment, somehow or other, Matsuyo-hime's gag slipped loose.

And again she fell weeping upon the spot; but Futodama-no-Mikoto, finding something amiss, folded his arms and for a while was lost in thought. Matsuyo-hime and Terutae-hime pressed their hands together again and again, saying:

"The pheasant of the burning field, the crane of the night—none, they say, but has pity for its young; how much less could I, though it be by divine command, bear to abandon my dearest wife and child to an untimely end before my very eyes and press on to the enemy's castle! And yet Matsuyo-hime is no coward to be meekly bound by such devils as these; nor is my daughter Terutae-hime a girl to utter such womanish words.
