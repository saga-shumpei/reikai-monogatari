Chapter 6: The Kitayama River [573]

北山川〔573〕

§ 1

To the land of the four directions teaching sincerity, the Sendenshi of Hiromichiwake, following Futotamanomikoto, headed toward the Castle of Kenonjiyō, where the family of Ham, the Baramon, erected their Oshihe and shut themselves within. Just as they arrived at the ferry of the River of Eden, they were harassed by the Magakami’s bamboo-like swarming Soya; some fell, others drowned, and the boat was shattered into Kotsupamijin. Ichidō together at the Kahazoko of Eden, now sinking, now floating, down the Kahashimo, were caught between the crags, and the thread of their Inochi, like a bead of dew, was well-nigh severed. In that very selfsame hour, from whence none knew, there was heard a sound of Bimeu Ongaku; and lowering down a net of salvation, under the Mimamo of Meuon Bosatsu, the Goninzu preserved their Inochi.

Suddenly there cometh Takatsukami, the messenger of the Kami of Mount Caucasus, who, clasping them beneath his arm, flieth through the Chiukū, pointing toward the east. Yasuhiko, Kunihiko, Michihiko, Momogamihiko, and Takayamahiko, the Goninzu, rescued by the Kami, were cast down upon the sandbanks in the valley of Kitayama, a thousand fathom deep, where, awakening from sleep, the fearful memory of their dream, as they faced the River of the One Way, filled them with terror; and trembling as they arose, they looked upon the scenery of Yomo. Ah, how can this be? Yea, how can it be? Beside the stream of a narrow valley river, wrapped around by mountain upon mountain, to sleep with pillows laid side by side— truly this is the very bound of wonder, yea, the ultimate of mystery.

Kunihiko said, 'Ah, how terrible it was! My very spirit was almost startled into making a journey beyond the seas. Well, well, by grace it seemeth we have somehow landed safely. Never was there so strange a thing: when crossing the River of Eden, the vital screw was washed away, and the boat we rode was left to the waves. Without the feeling of being alive, and scarce saving our Inochi, before we had even time to report the divine words, alas, our boat made a great collision against a huge rock standing in the roaring torrent, and met the miserable fate of being shattered into Kotsupamijin. Thinking that we, together with the Sendenshi of Futotamanomikoto and all Ichidō, had become as dust upon the waters, the Goninzu wandered on foot across an endless grassy plain, until we at last arrived beside the swelling turbid stream; there we were manipulated by the strange machine of an uncanny crone, swept high into the heavens like a tree leaf, and at the very moment when our Inochi was about to cease, struck by the light of lightning and the voice of thunder roaring in the broad sky, we fell headlong to the earth. Though we thought our bones and flesh were smashed to Kotsupamijin, that our Inochi was wondrously saved is entirely the Mimamo of Ōkami. Whatever may happen, it is a wondrous thing; so long as our Inochi endureth, we must press forward for the sake of the Way.'

Yasuhiko said, 'Our situation is beyond all knowing; I feel as though dreaming a dream within a dream, and cannot yet hold a clear conviction whether I am in the present world or in the subtle world. Hark ye, Michihiko-san, what thinkest thou? Can it be that we are indeed making a journey through the netherworld?'

§ 2

Michihiko said, 'Now that things have come to this pass, I have no understanding of the matter whatsoever. Manifest and hidden are mingled together, and no clear division hath been made; wherefore thy thought can by no means be declared foolish.'

Mozuhiko said, 'Pray, Sendenshi-sama, let us pluck one of the fruits of the Kagu tree growing yonder and taste thereof. Inasmuch as it is said that all fruits of the Iukai possess bitterness, if it be bitter, this must surely be the Iukai; and if it be sour, it must surely be the Genkai. Shall I climb the tree and pluck one for thee?'

Kunihiko said, 'That is a goodly thought; climb quickly and pluck one for us. Yet it is a Hikakuteki Taiboku. Its branches are high, so unless thou climbest with Yōjin, thou mayest perform Kūchiukuwatsusō even as in a dream, smash thy Zugaikotsu against the Ganseki beneath the tree, and make an Angiya to the Iukai once more, which were a sorrowful thing; wherefore take thou sufficient care.'

Mozuhiko said, 'Even in Ken'on-kyō I was called the monkey Mozu-kō, being a master of climbing trees; wherefore pray do not be anxious concerning me in any wise.'

So saying, faster than the word, he climbed high into the branches of the Taiboku like unto a monkey. Takahiko drew near unto the Jiyuka and called out, 'Ho, Mozuhiko! How is it? Is it sour, or bitter, or sweet? Which of these is it?'

Mozuhiko plucked a fruit, peeled off its skin, stuffed his cheeks therewith, and then plucked another, peeled it, and ate it with narrow eyes and shrugged shoulders while his throat resounded. Then, gripping the peels, he threw them down, aiming at the face of Takahiko, who stood beneath the tree with open mouth staring up vacantly.

Takahiko said, 'Ho, Mozu-kō! How is it? The taste... Wilt thou not answer me quickly?'

Mozuhiko said, 'Thou art noisy! How can I know the taste when I have eaten but twenty or thirty of them? It must be one of the three: sour, sweet, or bitter. Thou art not a child at the foot of the slope; stand thou there calmly and watch me partake thereof. Ah, it is not sour, nor sweet, nor bitter! It hath a taste beyond words. Ah, because I am alive, I can receive such delicious things in abundance. Hark, Taka-kō, why dost thou not climb up also and eat?'

Takahiko said, 'As thou knowest, my body is heavy and I cannot climb trees. They say that he who helpeth with thatched roofs and he who climbeth trees are the grand champions among fools. Ho, grand champion of fools, wilt thou not drop a delicious-looking one for me?'

Mozuhiko said, 'I shall not drop even one so easily! Once I drop it, thou wilt pick it up and eat it all, so it would be my loss to drop it. If thou desirest it so greatly, though this be not the Battle of the Monkey and the Crab, shall I drop down for thee one as hard as a scab-like stone?'

§ 3

No sooner had he spoken than he struck Takahiko, aiming squarely at his head with a sharp crack. Takahiko, enraged as if ablaze with fierce fire, picked up stones of suitable size and hurled them at the treetops in rapid-fire succession. Mozuhiko plucked green fruit and threw them aiming at Takahiko, and upon this spot a scene of battle was commenced. Mozuhiko dodged the flying stones to the right and evaded them to the left, running about the treetops like a monkey until his foot slipped and he tumbled down—falling headlong over Takahiko's head with a whirring roar. Takahiko uttered a single cry of 'Ya!' and the instant he evaded the body, Mozuhiko hit the earth like a squashed frog, spread-eagled with a single deep groan, stretching his arms and legs as they quivered violently; his eyeballs rolled round and round, and without uttering a single word, he stretched out unconscious.

Yasuhiko said, 'Ah, this is terrible! If he perishes before we can discern whether the matter be sour or bitter, we too shall suffer all the more for want of direction. I fain would revive him by some means and hear a certain answer. Hark, Takahiko, it is as though thou hast slain Mozuhiko; this responsibility resteth upon thee. Wilt thou not devise some plan?'

Takahiko said, 'The God hath declared that neither hawk nor shrike shall be brought together into one place. I am Takahiko; by no means can I take care of a shrike.'

Yasuhiko said, 'Wherefore dost thou utter such foolish babble? Give him water to drink at once; what shall we do if he die?'

Takahiko said, 'Inasmuch as we all died once, there can scarce be any such thing as dying again. It is likely but the afternoon nap of one who hath died; how would it be if we leave him alone in peace until the moment he awaketh? For Mozuhiko is greatly fatigued from his flight through the air, so I pray thee, look upon him with indulgence.'

Michihiko said, 'Ah, Kunihiko-san, Yasuhiko-san! Verily we still seem to be human beings of the present world; if we leave Mozuhiko thus, he will truly perish. I beseech you, let us all together chant the divine words for him.'

The three began to chant the Amatsu-Norito with one voice. Straightway Mozuhiko's body trembled; opening wide his mouth, he raised his voice, crying:

'Ah, ah!'

And drooling saliva copiously like one afflicted with palsy, he began to move his body little by little.

Yasuhiko said, 'Ah, ah! He hath returned to our realm; his life at least is safe.'

Mozuhiko straightway got upon all fours, and uttering repeatedly a whinny like a horse—'Hin, hin, hin'—he pawed the river sand with his feet, and began to crawl up little by little.

Takahiko cried out, 'Lo! Having once died, this knave hath been born into the world as a horse. It is said that there is no Chiū for great good nor for great evil, and verily this fellow was a Daiakunin from of old. Without passing through Chiū, he was instantly transformed—nay, reborn—into the Chikushiyōdau. By virtue of our long acquaintance hitherto, if I bestride his horse, his Ingō shall be fulfilled, and he shall perchance be born again as a Ningen. Come, thou four-legged Mozuhiko, thou art an exceedingly Shiawasemono! While there be many beasts that are subject to Raueki, what bounden happiness is thine to bear Takahiko-san, an Ontomo unto the Sendenshi of Ananai-kyō! Come, gallop forth, gallop forth!'

Mozuhiko uttered, 'Hin-hin-hin!'

Takahiko spake, 'Ha! This fellow is a true beast; yet for a horse his back is somewhat low, wherefore the riding is ill. Nevertheless, since he is a Kōma requiring no Motode, I shall exercise Shinbau.'

So saying, while still bestriding the back of Mozuhiko, he took a piece of wood that lay at hand to serve as a whip, and lashed him without restraint. Straightway the body of Mozuhiko expanded, until he became a wondrous beast with the face of a man and the body of an animal, huge as an elephant.

Takahiko cried, 'O ye three noble Sendenshi, behold! A horse hath been made, whose face is human and whose body is like unto an elephant with thick legs; how say ye, shall ye not all ride together? In ancient days, in the Garden of Eden, the Creator Ōkami of this world commanded the two, man and woman, Adam and Eve, saying, "Eat not of this fruit." Yet Eve paid no heed, but gathered and devoured it furiously like a famished tiger, and urged her husband Adam to eat thereof also, until at last they incurred the divine punishment. And that evil vapor congealed and became a great serpent of eight heads and eight tails, and a sinister fox of golden fur and nine tails, which came to rove rampant throughout the world. The retribution was swift; this knave Mozuhiko, as recompense for devouring this glorious fruit all alone with an air of absolute ownership without giving to others, tumbled down from the tree and lost his life, falling instantly into the Chikushiyōdau and becoming such a monstrous beast-man. Ye all, even the brushing of sleeves is a link from a former life; I pray ye, ride upon him for the expiation of his sins!'

The body of Mozuhiko straightway began to groan, 'U, u, u!'

Takahiko spake, 'Nay, wilt thou not be quiet? Because thou groanest so exceedingly, thy whole body trembleth violently, and even my buttocks have become ticklish. If thou art noisy, I shall smite thy rump!' So saying, he struck him sharply with the piece of wood. The man-elephant began to move up and down. At the first, it moved up and down within the space of four or five feet; but at last it ascended and descended in the mid-air of seven or eight ken, leaping up and down, to the left and to the right. Being thrown off by that vibration, Takahiko bit into the branch of the Kagu tree as before, and escaped a fall. The form of the man-elephant instantly diminished in volume, and returned to the former shape of Mozuhiko.

Mozuhiko: "Ahahaha! Hey, Takahiko! How is it? Is it sour, sweet, or bitter? Wilt thou not give answer?"

Takahiko: "Na-na-na, what nonsense art thou uttering? How could it be sour, sweet, or bitter? Having brought me to such a painful and bitter plight—mark my words, if I die, I shall haunt thee as a ghost! Hyū, doro, doro, doro it shall be!"

Mozuhiko turned up his eyes, stuck out his tongue, and said:

"If it be our Goen, I ask for thy favor again! Now, now, now, ye three Sendenshi-sama, if we leave him thus committed to the fruit of the Kagu tree, it shall be Daijiyōbu until he dieth. Let us all depart from this place even an Ichiji sooner, and set forth unto our Senden!"

Yasuhiko: "Ahahaha!"

Kunihiko: "Ohogoho!"

Michihiko: "Yā, Mozuhiko! Thou art a Bakemono, possessing a strange Biyōki. Yet we cannot leave Takahiko thus Utsuchiyatsu. As this is thy Sekinin, it were well that thou gently lower him beneath the Jiyuka and bring him along."

Takahiko: "Moshi-moshi, Michihiko-san, I thank thee! Thou hast spoken well. Ye Sendenshi Yasuhiko and Kunihiko who know not Ninjiyō, and thou Mozuhiko the Bakayarau—I have caused ye Goshinpai for a long time; many thanks indeed!"

So saying, he slid down swiftly like a monkey from the Jiyujiyō. The Yonin at Ichido,

Ichidō: "Ahahaha!"

rolled over in laughter. Takahiko pulled up Mozuhiko's tail-end and said:

"Kora, Heko-horse, well hast thou made a Baka of me!"

Mozuhiko said, "What!" and at once clenched his fist, striking Takahiko's head repeatedly, poka-poka. Takahiko, burning like fierce fire, again clenched his fist and struck down as if to shatter his bones. Neither late nor early at this moment, Mozuhiko made himself slender and ran away at full speed along the field path overgrown with weeds. Takahiko said:

"Curse thee, Mozuhiko! Cowardly and wretched, shall I let thee escape?"

and tucking up his garment, he pursued after him, hiding his form toward the distant sky shrouded in mist.

Yasuhiko: "Yā, we have witnessed an amusing spectacle! Yet, if we leave them thus, we know not what those two fellows might do. Kunihiko-dono and Michihiko-dono, let us pursue after them even an Ichiji sooner and search for their whereabouts."

As Yasuhiko hurriedly took the lead and ran forth, the two likewise followed after his footsteps.
