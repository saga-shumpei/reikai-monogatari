§ 1

Futodama-no-Mikoto sat down upon a rock by the roadside and was reciting the Amatsu Norito in a low voice. The voices of a hundred birds began to be heard in the woods far and near. The eastern sky grew faintly bright, and the color of dawn became clearer moment by moment. The voices of many demon gods came rustling from beyond the forest. Even as he braced himself, thinking he must not be off his guard, there rang out loudly the sound of horses' hooves, and Iwahiko, Umehiko, Otohiko, Kamehiko, Komahiko, and Takahiko came galloping to the spot like shot arrows and, turning to Futodama-no-Mikoto, said:

Iwahiko: "Ho! Are you not the missionary Futodama-no-Mikoto? We, by command of Hinodewake-no-Kami in the capital of Fusa, have crossed the muddy flood of the River Eden to pacify Ken'onkyō through the Word together with you, and have at last hastened hither. What has become of the people of your company?"

Futodama-no-Mikoto: "Ho! This is an unlooked-for coming of yours. I am just now on the point of storming the enemy's stronghold alone. To pacify through the Word a fortress of crooked gods such as this, I alone am enough. Though you have taken the trouble to ride out, return swiftly, I pray, to the capital of Fusa, and take up each your own divine work."

Iwahiko: "That would be called the very height of recklessness. That we, having taken the trouble to cross mountains and rivers and at last come face to face with this place, should vainly turn our horses' heads about with the enemy before our eyes, is not the part of a man. We beg you, let us take part in this divine battle."

Umehiko and the other four missionaries, with one voice, pressed to be allowed to join the campaign.

Futodama-no-Mikoto: "Then it cannot be helped. I thank you for your pains, and beg your aid."

"Your swift consent—we are grateful and beholden."

And the company of six, following after Futodama-no-Mikoto, went deep into the mountains. The scene at that place was like looking upon a picture scroll.

When they had gone about a league and a half, a deep mountain stream lay across their way. Its width was nearly ten ken, and they came to a dead stop. The seven missionaries tied their horses here for a while, rested a little, and took counsel together how they might cross this ravine to the opposite bank. On the far side of the valley were seven or eight giants—beanpoles fit to steal a fire-bell—wearing hats like obelisks, who, opening their huge eyes and their great mouths, laughed loudly:

"Wahahahahaa! How now? However great the liver of this Futodama-no-Mikoto, he'll not be able to cross this mountain stream! Look well at the bottom of this river!"

So they pointed. Looking, they saw at the bottom of the river, with no space left bare, keen spearheads some two feet long, standing straight up by hundreds and thousands like horsetail shoots springing from the earth. It presented a scene in which, once one fell into this river, any body whatsoever must perish skewered like a potato. Futodama-no-Mikoto laughed aloud:

"Could one carry on the mission of the Anai Teaching, if one feared such a mere mountain stream as this? I'll cross it handsomely, you'll see!"

No sooner had he spoken than he gave a glance to all. All understood and mounted their horses; Futodama-no-Mikoto leapt up behind Iwahiko, and at once they turned back four or five chō along the road they had come, then again wheeled their horses' heads and, plying the whip, leapt in a single bound, by sheer momentum, across the gorge some three ken wide. The huge men, startled and flustered, fled away like vanishing mist. Then following on, Umehiko, Takahiko, Kamehiko, and all the rest likewise whipped up their horses at once and crossed this mountain stream without difficulty; and when they looked back—beyond all expectation—there was nothing at all like a mountain stream, but only a plain thickly overgrown with grass.

Futodama-no-Mikoto: "Ahahahaha! They've cheated us again. Every one of you must take good care. From here on, whatever ravines there may be, let us make it our rule to cross them unconcerned. This is the den of devils who use wondrous, unfathomable sorceries; even just now it was a scheme of the demon gods to take the shapes of my wife Matsuyo-hime and my daughter Terutae-hime and so blunt my spirit. Let us go on with care, that we be never deceived to the last."

So saying, he went on ahead. All tied their horses to the trees nearby and went deep into the mountains along the valley road between peak and peak, singing missionary songs. When they had gone some leagues, they came full upon a majestic castle wall. A vast great gate of vermilion balustrades and blue-green tiles was raised there, and four or five giants wearing crowns like obelisks guarded the gate closely. Futodama-no-Mikoto and his company at once appeared before the gate:

"I am a missionary of the Anai Teaching! In this land Onikumohiko and his wife, possessed by the evil spirits of the Eight-Forked Serpent, the golden fox, and the wicked demon, are shut up within, spreading a divine rule unjust in the extreme, and have made this Ken'onkyō well-nigh a realm of hell. Since this is a great crime that sets at naught the grace of Heaven, I have come forth now in obedience to the command of the Great God to proclaim the Way, that Onikumohiko may be brought back in submission to the good path. Open this gate swiftly!"

So he pressed upon them with stern words. The gatekeeper, taken aback, said:

"Pray wait a while. Since you crossed the River Eden, the castle has been in the greatest confusion, topsy-turvy, and even our chief Onikumohiko is in a state of a thousand pains and ten thousand toils over how he may give you satisfaction. Presently the bell giving the signal for opening the gate will ring out; until then, pray rest yourselves at ease here. Surely, surely there is not a single one who will oppose you. Pray set your minds at rest."

Otohiko: "Ho! Thou art making us waste time with this and that, and meanwhile readying for battle—surely it's a scheme to slaughter us to a man. I have no ears to hear such a 'Yotarisk' of nonsense. Open this gate swiftly!"

Futodama-no-Mikoto: "What! Hesitation and hanging back are the great taboo of the Anai Teaching. Life and death, victory and defeat, are all held in the hand of God; let us leave our fortune to Heaven and go as far as we may go."

So saying, Futodama-no-Mikoto went on ahead. Just as they drew near the palace of Onikumohiko, an elegant gate of plain white wood slid lightly open, and there came forth at their ease some ten and more graceful, lovely beauties who, rubbing their snow-white slender hands, said:
