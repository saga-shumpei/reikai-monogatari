Chapter 13: Appearance of the Divine Maiden [580]

神女出現〔580〕

§ 1

Kamususanō no Ōkami, by reason of the incident at the Ama no Ihato, took upon His own person the myriad sins and transgressions, and from the time He departed upon a journey of wandering through the land of Mizuho in Ashihara, enduring the hardships and sufferings of the Chikura no Okido, those beings that had hitherto hidden their shadows—the Yamataorochi, the Kinmaukiubi, and the hideous Magaoni—began once more to raise their heads in every quarter. The teachings of Ural, the Brahmanic doctrines, and the ways of divination took the fleshly temples of the Yobito as their dwelling, and with wickedness and lawlessness exceeding that of old, the hearts of the Yobito were all twisted and warped, such that not a single Hitohashira remained to uphold the truth.

The world grew clouded day by day and month by month. In the Ihori of the mountains and the shallows of the rivers, far and near, the voices of the Magakami, raging in their fury, were as the tempest or the thunder; they were as the earthquake, beyond all comparison, in the clamor of their simultaneous roar. The mountains and rivers trembled, the grasses and trees withered, the unseasonable rain fell incessantly, and the winds raged, overturning houses; the tips of the trees were rent and broken, and the leaves were torn as if by the teeth of a saw.

Kamususanō no Ōkami, a hero of peerless valor in the age of the Kami and one who may be called the incarnation of benevolence, possessed the majestic form of the Mizutama. His hair, black as lacquer, hung long down His back, and His mustache, orderly and well-defined, and the beard upon His chin, beautiful as the light of lapis lazuli, hung long upon His breast; bathed by the rain and combed by the wind, He appeared in the land of Tibet, which is surrounded by mountains upon mountains.

The Onitsukami, who had appeared upon Mount Chikyō and once sought to block the ascent of the Mikoto, was the reincarnation of Takakuniwake, who in ancient times had been born as the younger brother of Takayamahiko in the capital of Peter, and who had appeared and disappeared many times in the manifest and hidden worlds. He was a man of strength who had once appeared in the Takamagahara of the Kamukai and had taken part in the great incident of the Ama no Ihato. He, who had fathomed the pure heart of Kamususanō no Ōkami and was a man of chivalrous spirit, could not let things remain as they were. By the command of the Sumeru Ōkami, who dwelt upon Mount Tenkyō, he stood against the mountain of Chikyō; and though he once, finding it difficult to refuse the divine command, turned his blade against the Ōkami of the Mizutama and sought to trouble the ascent of the Mikoto, his heart was double-sided. Thinking that he would assist Kamususanō no Ōkami with all his heart and all his might, he waited upon Mount Chikyō, wondering if the Mikoto would ascend, and his heart was indeed noble.

Kamususanō no Ōkami, accompanied by Takakuniwake, left Mount Chikyō behind and approached the capital of Rasafu, a secret land of Toyoashihara surrounded by the Aogaki mountains, where the winter winds blew cold and the snow lay deep. Such an example had not been seen since ancient times; with the Kami as His strength and truth as His staff, He advanced with courage, spurred on by the neighing of the horse of His heart, through the fields where the wild winds blew. Suddenly the sky clouded over, and as the gray heavens hung heavy and dull, a fierce snow began to fall; the two, taking shelter, ran into a certain straw-thatched hut and requested lodging for the night.

§ 2

Susanōnomikoto stood at the threshold, and in a quiet voice, spoke thus:

"We are two travelers wandering upon a journey; the snow hath closed our path, the day hath reached its end, and we are beset by difficulties. We beseech thee, in thy mercy, to grant us lodging for a single night."

Upon this inquiry, a maiden of youthful appearance answered, "Aye," and appeared at the threshold.

"Oh, travelers, ye must surely be distressed by the snow. Though this be but a humble hut, there is a chamber of sufficient size within; I pray you, take your ease and rest."

The Mikoto replied, "Ah, there are no demons in this world... We are most grateful for thy words, and shall gladly accept thy hospitality this night."

"Pray, do so; I shall guide you to the inner quarters," the maiden said with grace, and with gentle steps, she led the two into an inner room.

Guided by the maiden, the two sat in repose before the hearth in the inner room, and while warming their hands, they engaged in whispered conversation.

At this moment, a man who appeared to be the master of the house appeared, rubbing his hands together, and bowed courteously toward the two.

"Oh, travelers, it is well that you have deigned to stay in this humble hut. As we are in a place where fuel is scarce, you must surely be troubled," he said, while placing dried dung of the yak into a basket, casting it into the hearth to warm the room.

This region is a secret land of the world, surrounded on all sides by high mountains, and being a place of inconvenient passage, it is a land that does not permit the entry of foreigners.

However, since the great upheaval in Takamagahara, the hearts of men have greatly softened, and as it was a time when they were leaning somewhat toward the principle of universal brotherhood, they now, conversely, welcomed the entry of foreigners.

Finding them a novelty, the old and young of the village vied with one another to visit, desiring to hear their interesting tales.

Their ordinary fuel consisted of dried wheat straw or the dung of the yak, and they used parched wheat ground into powder as their sustenance.

It is a land where the weather is never constant; at one moment it clears, and at the next, snow and hail descend.

As it is a great highland in the world, grain does not ripen in great abundance; even in a year of good harvest, for instance, if one sows five shō of wheat seed and reaps one to of harvest, such is considered a bountiful year.

The name of the master of this house is Kanan.

Kanan placed the parched wheat powder into wooden bowls, brought forth boiled tea, and bowing with both hands upon the floor, said, "Honored guests, as you know, this is a land of inconvenience, and we have naught worthy to offer those from other lands; yet, as this is the finest fare in our country, I pray you, partake of it at your leisure."

Having said this, he hid his form within a Hitoma. While the Futari were partaking of a Shiyokuji, pouring tea over roasted wheat flour and kneading it with a spoon, five beautiful maidens appeared in this place, bowed with both hands placed upon the ground in a Teinei manner, and rising all at once, sang and danced, appearing to strive to console the weariness of the two travelers.

"Ah, Onōnogata, having come late and caused an Ojiyama, and furthermore having partaken of such a Ketsukō Chisō, I am truly in a state of Manzoku. Are you Nanjira the daughters of this house?"

Before the Kotoba had even finished, the eldest maiden replied, "Yes, I am the wife of Kanan, the Aruji of this house. The women who are here are all my Kiyōdai, and all are wives of Kanan, living happy Tsukihi. However, ever since Kamususanō no Ōkamisama from Takaamahara was made to bear the Chikura no Okido and departed for parts unknown, the evil spirits of the Uranai religion have invaded this secret land, which had hitherto been peaceful and secure, disturbing the customs of old and causing the hearts of the people to be in such trepidation that there is no peace of mind; and now, the evil spirits of the Brahman religion have surged in like the tide, and the country is now in a state of misery, as if it were a scene of carnage. In the age when Kamususanō no Ōkamisama appeared as the ruler of this great earth, this secret land was truly like a heavenly paradise, but since the time the Ōkamisama hid himself, various evil spirits have suddenly entered from abroad, causing all manner of strange occurrences; if left as it is, it is hard to say whether it might not suddenly manifest the path of hell. Those among the people of the country who have a heart are once again hoping for the appearance of the Ōkami, and are practicing abstinence from tea, salt, and fire, offering prayers to heaven. Therefore, for this past month, we have been abstaining from all food and drink, concentrating our prayers day and night, and striving to perform virtuous deeds; as such, we have not been able to attend to you, and for this rudeness, given the circumstances, I pray you will not think ill of us, as I ask on behalf of all the sisters."

Kamususanō no Mikoto folded his hands, let tears fall drop by drop from both eyes, and remained silent, heaving a sigh.

Takakuniwake said, "Ah, truly admirable, grateful, grateful. The Kamususanō no Ōkamisama whom Nanjira and the people of the country yearn for is, in fact, here... nay... he shall soon descend to this country and will surely grant the wishes of Nanjira; do not worry."

"I am one called Kaen, the wife of Kanan, and I pray for your kind favor. I pray that you will stay for a long time, whether it be ten days, twenty days, or a hundred days. As this country is a land where food cannot be harvested, we cannot increase our houses, and thus, as you see, there are many in the family within a single house. My husband is my elder brother."

§ 4

Takakuniwake said, 'In that case, this land follows the principle of polygamy, does it not?'

'Indeed, indeed; of necessity, my household is polygamous, and depending on the house, there are also places where polyandry prevails,' they replied.

Takakuniwake remarked, 'Strange, it is like the Mormon sect.'

'Ho, ho, ho... the night has already deepened; pray, be at ease and retire to sleep,' they said.

The five of them spoke these words of greeting all at once, and then concealed their forms in the next chamber.

The Mikoto said, 'Takakuniwake-dono, shall we rest peacefully this night?'

Takakuniwake replied, 'I am most grateful.'

With these words, they took the skins of beasts from a nearby storage, spread them out, and layering them one upon another, the two lay down to sleep in peace.

The customs of this land forbid the increase of households among one another; for instance, if there are six brothers and one of them is a man, this man is taken as a husband, and he is never permitted to marry into another family.

Furthermore, when there are five men in a single house and a daughter is born, the unwritten law of one wife to five husbands is practiced.

Therefore, there is the inconvenience that when only boys are born, or when only girls are born, the lineage of that house is brought to an end.

Hereupon, Susanoo-no-Mikoto, unable to endure the sight of this tragic state, permitted marriage with other families.

From this time forth, the people praised Susanoo-no-Mikoto as the God of Marriage, and in this land, he came to be respected by the people as the God of Idumu.

Susanoo-no-Mikoto was awakened by the whispering of men and women, and as he listened intently, he heard the sound of prayers being offered for some purpose.

The Mikoto shook the shoulder of Takakuniwake and said, 'Ho, Takakuniwake, awaken; there is the sound of some mysterious people praying.'

Before the words had finished, Takakuniwake sprang up and said, 'Indeed, it is the voice of a great multitude; earlier, I heard the woman of this house, named Kaen, speak of abstaining from tea and salt, and praying for the reappearance of Susanoo-no-Mikoto; perhaps it is a matter of that nature.'

The Mikoto said, 'Since I shall rest in this chamber, do you go forth and investigate the truth or falsehood of the matter.'

Takakuniwake replied, 'I shall obey.'

He departed from that place and proceeded with stealthy steps toward the direction of the voices.

As he looked, he saw several dozen men and women, completely naked, crouching in the field before the garden with their hands pressed together, straining their weak voices, and offering prayers with single-minded devotion.

Presently, one man, while waving a hempen wand, fell into a state of divine possession and rushed headlong toward the northwest.

The many men and women, fearing to be left behind, pursued him with all their might.

However, their bodies were weakened by long fasting, and they stumbled and fell as they followed in his wake.

Takakuniwake watched the situation without blinking, but before he knew it, he began to walk himself, feeling as if he were being dragged along, and followed the multitude stealthily.

Far ahead, a small, bun-shaped hill, appearing as if it were a mound of withered grass, could be seen.

The man waving the hempen wand stood atop the small hill, and while shouting something, he waved the hemp back and forth, left and right, dancing about like one possessed, leaping and jumping, and making strange cries of 'kyatsu, kyatsu'.

The several dozen old and young men and women likewise ran up onto the small hill, and like the man before them, danced and jumped about.

Takakuniwake hid his body in the grass of the wilderness and watched the strange prayer with bated breath.

After a while, the man holding the hemp suddenly concealed his form on the other side of the small hill.

Following him, the many men and women vanished one by one, then two by two, then three and five, until at last, leaving only one beautiful woman behind, they all disappeared entirely.

§ 5

Takakuniwake exclaimed, 'How strange, that such things should come to pass. That so great a multitude of the old and young, men and women, should have vanished like smoke upon this Kuōgen, where the eye meets no obstruction to the horizon, is a matter beyond my Gaten. Surely they cannot have been absorbed into the Daichi and turned to Kona.'

While thus murmuring to himself, he directed his attention to the Zengosaiu and proceeded Hitoashihitoashi. A maiden stood upon the Kōka, her Morote clasped, her head bowed in silence with a Fushimegachi air. Takakuniwake approached with firm steps and said,

'I know not what Jiyochiu you may be, but as I watched from the Monokage Sakihodo, I saw several Sūjifunin, both Danjiyo, following a man who bore a Heisoku, run toward this Kōka. As I watched them dancing and raving in the Zengosaiu, their forms vanished in an instant. You appear to be one of them; I pray you, tell me the Shidai and the Shiyōsai of this matter, if you will. I am a messenger of the Kami who shall save the Tenka.'

When he thus inquired, the woman was startled by his voice and gazed upon the face of Takakuniwake, yet she shook her head from side to side and made no response. Takakuniwake, having no other recourse, ran up the Kōka himself and inspected the vicinity in every detail, yet there was neither any sign of a hole nor any form of a person fallen. Not even the sound of an insect could be heard.

Takakuniwake sat down heavily upon the hill and, with his Morote clasped, remained lost in contemplation, saying, 'How Fushigi this is.'

At this moment, the aforementioned woman suddenly cast a thin cord around the neck of Takakuniwake and, bearing him upon her back, began to run with a rhythmic gait back along the path whence they had come.

Takakuniwake, his throat constricted and his breath failing, was carried away while struggling with his hands and feet. It appeared that the grown man, having exhausted his strength in a desperate struggle for his life, had become limp; when the woman had traveled perhaps one or two chō, she stumbled upon a stone and fell easily to the ground.

At that instant, the woman released the thin cord; Takakuniwake leaped up, turning his body with agility, and seizing the woman by her nape, he cried, 'Thou art a bold and fearless rogue, and I shall grant thee no mercy!'

He clenched his fist, hard as a conch shell, and assumed the posture of striking her with such force as to shatter her very bones. Yet, in the heart of Takakuniwake, there was not the slightest intention to strike this frail woman. It was merely a stratagem to display his power and compel her to confess the truth.

The woman replied, 'Ho, ho, ho, what a terrifying face you make. If you wear such a fearful countenance, no one in this land of Tibet will become your wife. Oh, what a difficult face you have... ho, ho, ho.'

"Oh dear, Taka-san! How admirable, how admirable! Ho, ho, ho, ho, ho!"

What, indeed, shall become of the fate of Takakuniwake?
