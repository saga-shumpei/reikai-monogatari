Chapter 5: The Five Tengu [572]

第五章　五天狗〔五七二〕

§ 1

The celestial expanse of the heavens presented a dismal scene, as though diluted ink had been spilled across the sky. In contrast to this gloomy expanse, across the great plain where green leaves flourished, the missionary envoys of the Ananai teachings—Yasuhiko of the peaceful realm, Kunihiko of the age of Miroku whose prosperity shall endure like the ever-green pine, and Michihiko who proclaims the sacred doctrine—hastened along. Accompanied by Mozuhiko and Takahiko, men born of ready tongues and glib lips, they arrived together at the edge of the river.

Yasuhiko spoke: "Lo, here again lies the River of Single Mind! When last we came unto this place, waters exceedingly pure and clear flowed with a murmuring sound. Yet today, though not a single sudden summer shower hath fallen, how hath the water so swollen? A frightful, yellowish mire rushes along like thick mud! Here two hags are wont to abide, yet today not a trace of them is to be seen. What thinkest thou, Michihiko? Doth not some dread strike upon thy soul?"

Michihiko replied: "Whether it were a dream or an apparition, I know not clearly; yet when I recall the sight of those two hags brandishing their razor-sharp cleavers and slashing at us from front and rear, from left and right, the hair of my flesh still standeth on end! In this place, O Yasuhiko, we must tread with utmost circumspection. Finding that the two hags are absent, had we not best pass on with swift strides? Waste not thy words in idle chatter, nor stir up vain apprehensions."

Yasuhiko answered: "Who is it that entertaineth apprehension? If those hags were present this day, I would gladly dispute the matter with them! Hearken, Kunihiko—thou hast no experience of this matter, and perchance dost not know; they are exceedingly terrifying crones, and when they swing about their kitchen cleavers, they flash like lightning, and one feeleth that the very bones of the neck might be severed to dust in a single stroke!"

Kunihiko exclaimed: "Make not a fool of me! Hast thou already forgotten? Did I not venture forth upon the quest together with thee? I too am yet in the prime of my strength, a man of thirty years! Never shall I harbour so cowardly a spirit as to shrink this precious neck in fear of the cleavers of withered, fading hags! Dost thy legs tremble, then, O Yasuhiko? Very well, let me take the lead; with a single stroke of my blade shall I hew off the heads of those vile hags and make clear our path!"

Yasuhiko said: "At the crossing of the River of Eden, thy boat was swept away in the turbid torrent and dashed against the crags standing in midstream, shattered into leaves and dust, so that thou didst languish for a season beneath the surging waters! From the lips of such a man as thee, I scarce expected such grandiloquent boasting! Conduct thyself with greater sobriety. Upon this venture, no such reckless blunder as being swept down the shoals of Eden may be tolerated. Upon the bed of this river, myriads of cleavers are planted point-upward; if perchance thou shouldst slip and fall into the waters, thou wouldst instantly be minced like sliced raw fish!"

Kunihiko retorted: "A troublesome matter indeed! Yet it were a fair time to die. I have neither beloved wife nor child, nor do I cling with attachment to this earthly world. What say ye, shall we not summon our courage, storm the dwelling of those hags, and smash their pots and kettles to pieces?"

Yasuhiko laughed: "Fierce indeed is thy bluster! Yet if thou soundest thy trumpet so lustily now, thy breath will fail thee ere we reach our journey's end!"

Kunihiko answered: "Nay, I was once known as Yotahiko, and none other! What I have spoken is by no means idle nonsense; it hath a profound and subtle significance. As the proverb hath it: 'The workmanship runneth in many ways; wait and behold the finished craft!' Soon shall I astound you all with the prowess of my valor; follow behind me, keeping that in mind!"

Yasuhiko said: "There he bloweth his steam whistle once again! Traveling with such a fellow is so clamorous that crows, swallows, sparrows, and shrikes alike all take flight, leaving no zest in our journey through the unseen realm. Prithee, Kunihiko, rest thy throat awhile and walk softly! This is a place where even a slight noise resoundeth unto the very heavens."

Kunihiko cried: "Though under the furious assault of General Nogi the fortress of Nanzan at last fell silent, the Yotahiko battery of 203 Meter Hill shall not so easily be silenced! Once born as a brave man, whether one dieth once or dieth twice, 'tis still but a single death! Ho, ho! Old hags of the River of Single Mind! If ye be within, answer me! Kunihiko, envoy of the Divine, hath come to visit you!"

So speaking, counting off his companions to himself, he spurred his strides as if applying oil to his compass and setting his pulleys in rapid motion. Yasuhiko, Michihiko, Takahiko, and Mozuhiko all raised their hands together, shouting with all their might:

All Four: "Ho! Hold, ho!"

Thus they cried to halt him. Yet Kunihiko gave no ear to them; tucking up the skirt of his garment, he dashed headlong toward the cottage of the hags. Carried along by his furious momentum, he overshot the mark by half a block.

"Ha! Perchance terrified by the majesty of Lord Kunihiko, the two hags of the River of Single Mind have vanished into thin air like smoke and mist—a disastrous rout for them! Yet how wretched are Yasuhiko and Michihiko! To gather four or five men together and tremble in dread at the slaying of a mere woman is truly a pitiful tale! Ha, ha, ha!"

Thus, filled with pride, he poured forth his soliloquy. Hearing his voice, from within the hut nestled beneath the roots of the pine tree, a hand resembling tanned persimmon-paper reached out, and in a hoarse, cracked voice,

"Ho! Ho! Come hither!"

Thus called the crone, beckoning unto him.

Kunihiko mused: "Aha! Driving forward with too much horsepower, I must have overlooked the crone's cottage! What is this? 'Tis an exceedingly filthy hag, far more aged than Yasuhiko reported! Not forty-seven or eight, but looking well nigh a hundred and twenty years! Still, one so decrepit with age must have lost all her bodily strength; she will be an easy match to deal with!"

So speaking, he trotted back toward the little hut by the riverside beneath the pine, and pounding upon the portal three or four times, cried out:

Kunihiko: "Lo, I am none other than the renowned Yotahiko—nay, hold, the missionary envoy Kunihiko! Clear of eye, with distinguished brow, straight bridge of nose, and stern, handsome lips seasoned with gallant grit—an incomparable, peerless man filled with heroic spirit! Seeking to propagate the teachings of the Ananai doctrine, I have ventured from afar through the unseen realm and arrived at this very spot. If thou shouldst show any insolence, with this cherished cudgel shall I shatter thy hip-bones into splinters! Answer me! Answer me!"

Thus shouting, with comical gestures of hands and feet, he danced outside the door. From within, the gentle voice of a maiden of seventeen or eighteen years was heard:

"I know not who ye may be, yet how kind of you to visit this humble thatch! Surely thou hast companions with thee; pray, enter all together at once."

Kunihiko murmured: "What is this? 'How kind of you to visit this humble thatch'—why, far from a forty-year-old woman, is this not the tender voice of a maiden of seventeen or eighteen, sweet as the song of a cricket, pure as a precious jewel? If such a fair maiden abode here, why did that knave Yasuhiko not tell me sooner? Indeed, between a crone of forty-seven or eight and a damsel of seventeen or eighteen there lies a difference of thirty years! Could my eyes have played me false? Let me see, let me peep within for a moment!"

Muttering thus, he stole a stealthy look through the narrow latticed window. The maiden had her glossy black hair bound up like the lustrous plumage of a raven; her back was turned toward the window, so that her face could not clearly be discerned, yet her posture was exceedingly graceful, and she sat quietly stitching with her needle. At once Kunihiko was seized with amorous longing, and he said:

"'Ah, what comes to pass is unpleasing, and what one longeth for cometh not to pass!' Yet if such a creature abideth even in the realm of the dead, 'tis far more delightful than the earthly world! While I dwelt in the earthly realm, I suffered grievously at the hands of a mature woman named Omatsu; yet how lovely, how embraceable is this maiden compared to her! Ho, fair one, what is thy name? Are thy father and mother from home? Hast thou a mind to become my bride? If thou wilt come with me, I will buy thee sugar candies and sweet bean jelly well-nigh every day! How sayest thou? Give me an answer! Lo, she remaineth aloof and uttereth not a word. Indeed, 'tis said that beauties are often proud, yet she appeareth an exceedingly clever damsel. Though she bid me enter swiftly, now that I gaze upon her through the window, she acteth as though nothing were amiss and answereth not. If I retreat thus, the honour of Kunihiko would be disgraced; very well, I shall step within, seize her in my arms, and carry her forth!"

Meanwhile, Yasuhiko, Michihiko, and the other two had hastened after Kunihiko. Yet all about that place it suddenly grew dark, and there lay some manner of shadow stretched out across the middle of the road.

Yasuhiko said: "Hark, ye three companions! Lo, some strange thing lieth stretched out upon the ground here. How sayest thou, lend me thy pilgrim's staff; I shall prod it to see what it be!"

So saying, he snatched a makeshift, knot-studded staff from the hand of Mozuhiko and beat down upon it with all his might.

Kunihiko shrieked: "Ouch! Ouch! Ouch! It hurteth! It hurteth sore! Unbefitting such a lovely maiden, thou art an exceedingly cruel creature! How canst thou beat this handsome swain with such a knot-studded staff? It hurteth, it hurteth! Never again will I peep through thy window nor lay hand upon thy body, so put down thy staff! Ouch! Ouch!"

Yasuhiko called out: "Ho, ho, Master Kuni! What art thou doing, lying stretched out in this darkness, babbling in thy sleep? Pull thyself together, man!"

Kunihiko groaned: "Ah! Is that not Yasuhiko? That wench, maiden though she be, hath beaten me mercilessly! Wilt thou not avenge me upon her?"

Yasuhiko snapped: "Fool! Play not the innocent!"

And with that he struck him two or three heavy blows upon the back. Kunihiko at last scrambled to his feet:

Kunihiko: "Ah, how strange! Doth a man dream dreams even after arriving in the realm of the dead?"

Yasuhiko laughed: "Assuredly so! As the worldly proverb hath it, 'Like unto one dreaming in the nether darkness!' Bwahahaha!"

From within the cottage, the voice of the hag cried out:

Hag: "Hey there! Ye two dotards who fled before! Our battle of the kitchen cleavers is yet unfinished! Turn back this instant and contend with me fairly in single combat!"

Yasuhiko retorted: "Ho! Thou art that wretched Hoshi-hoshi hag! With a face like a dried toad, clinging forever to this miserable hut like rotten sardines stuck to a fishnet—what an unsightly wretch! Be thou illumined by the Divine Radiance, and have the uncleanness of thy body cleansed!"

Hag: "Assuredly so! I am an exceedingly sticky old crone! Ye too have now plunged your feet into a bucket of birdlime! Ye are like flies caught in sticky lime; stir but an inch if ye dare, and I shall send you flying into mid-air!"

So saying, she pressed down vigorously upon a lever fitted unto a pillar. The five men, spaced at even intervals in a circle, began to whirl round and round, leaving the solid ground and ascending higher and higher into the mid-heavens!

Kunihiko shouted: "Aha! That mischievous sprite called 'the convenience of modern civilization' hath run rampant even in such a place as this! To hoist the bodies of the departed into the high heavens is merry sport indeed! Hearken, thou vile hag! Pull the lever harder, pull it further, that we may soar higher and higher! 'Tis fine sport, 'tis rare sport! Flying thus through the open sky, we shall plunge straight into Heaven itself! Up, hoist us higher, higher still!"

Thus he clamored as they were swept gradually into the high skies. Yet the five men, whirled aloft into the heavens, were buffeted by tempestuous winds, and the air growing thin, their breath failed them, their voices died away, and floating like five tengu in the expanse of heaven, they gasped:

"Ho! Ho! Old hag of the River of Single Mind! Hearken! Bring us down once more to the place where we were! Ho! Ho!"

Thus they cried aloud. The voice of the hag could be heard faintly, like the weeping of an earthworm:

Hag: "Ye missionary envoys of the Ananai teachings, together with thy two fool companions! Ye are like tailless squid-kites, and thy weight and gravity as missionaries is utterly zero! If ye would descend to thy former place, lighten thy bodies a little more! Because ye are overloaded with greed and heavy of soul, ye float in mid-air and cannot descend to earth! Repent of thy folly, and cast thy carnal flesh into the shoals of Eden!"

国彦『ヤー文明の利器と云ふ悪戯者がコンナ所まで跋扈しよつて、亡者の身体を中天に捲き揚げるとは面白い。ヤイ婆の奴、モツトモツト高く舞ひ揚げるやうに、機関を引張れ引張れ。面白いワイ、面白いワイ。此儘で大空を飛んで、天国まで飛込んでやるから、ソレモツトモツト高く揚げろ揚げろ』 と呶鳴り乍ら、次第々々に中空に捲き揚げられた。天上に捲き揚げられたる五人の男は上空の烈風に煽られ空気稀薄のため、殆ど息も絶え絶えとなり、声も出なくなつて、五天狗は空中に浮み乍ら、

『オーイオーイ、一途の川の婆アサン、ヤーイ、マア一度元の場所に降して呉れ。オーイオーイ』 と叫んで居る。婆の声は蚯蚓の泣くやうに幽かに聞えて来た。

婆『三五教の宣伝使及び二人の馬鹿者共、胴体無しの烏賊上り、宣伝使たるの貫目は全然ゼロだ。元の所にをり度くばモー少し汝が身を軽くしろ。余り欲張り過ぎて身が重たいから、空中に浮んで地上へ降りることが出来ぬのだ。マア後悔して、自分の肉体をエデンの河原へ捨てて了へ』

§ 6

Her words reached their ears brokenly, like a trunk line telephone half destroyed. Floating upon waves of wind, the five men held fast to one another's hands, drifting right and left, up and down, tilting vertical and horizontal, whirling wildly in the open sky.

Suddenly, lightning flashed across the sky and thunder pealed with a deafening roar. In the twinkling of an eye, as though struck by lightning, the five men, still clasping each other's hands, plummeted straight down like an arrow shot into the depths of a trackless mountain where no bird flieth.

Yet all this was the miraculous, fathomless work of the Bodhisattva Myōon, who, manifesting as a fisherman upon the lower reaches of the River of Eden, scooped up the five men with a net, breathed upon them, and commanded the Great Tengu of Mount Caucasus to lift them into the air and save them.

Ah, how wondrous is the working of the Divine!

Ah, how grateful is the salvation of the Great God!

忽ち空中に電光閃き雷鳴轟き渡ると見るまに、電気に打たれた如く五人は手を繋いだ儘、鳥も通はぬ山中に矢を射る如く一直線に落下した。

これは妙音菩薩がエデンの河の河下にて漁夫と変じ、五人の男を網を以て救ひ上げ、息を吹きかけコーカス山の大天狗をして空中に引揚げしめたる神変不可思議の所業なりけり。
