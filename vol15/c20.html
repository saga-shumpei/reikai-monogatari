Chapter 20: Fifty Centuries [587]

五十世紀〔587〕

§ 1

The party of three, accompanied by the Tenshi of Matsuhiko, came to a sudden halt before the Mirror Rock, and pondering how they might break through this Sekishiyo, they tilted their heads, questioning their breasts and pondering in their hearts, and while shaking their heads slowly up and down and from side to side, they spent some Ikubaku of Jikan in a state of some Tauwaku.

Tamahiko said, 'Even in the Genkai, because the mirrors of our hearts are clouded, we are prone to encounter obstacles in all Banji; it seems to be the Shiyōrai of our Mitama that we encounter them even upon coming to the Shinkai. Ah, what should be done? We cannot simply Hikikahe before our very eyes, and it seems that our Honshiyugojin might well provide some good Chie.'

Matsuhiko replied, 'It is because you are like that, that it is Ikan. Is there such a thing as smearing one's own filth upon the Honshiyugojin?'

Tamahiko said, 'We have always heard it said. We firmly believed that if the Honshiyugojin is good, the Nikutai will be Kankuwa-ed accordingly, and both spirit and flesh will become pure and clean, and we shall be saved into the heavenly kingdom. If we cannot reach the highest heaven when we are at ninety-nine percent, then our Honshiyugojin must be somewhat suspicious. Hey, hey, Honshiyugojin, come forth from the Seika-tanden and why do you not protect this palace of flesh? If you do not, you are not fulfilling the duties of a Honshiyugojin. If the Nikutai goes to heaven, it stands to reason that the Honshiyugojin can go as well. It is not merely the virtue of Tamahiko; it also becomes the virtue of the Honshiyugojin. Why are you dawdling?'

So saying, he clenched his fists and struck his abdomen repeatedly.

Matsuhiko laughed, 'Ahahaha, amusing, amusing.'

Tamahiko said, 'This is outrageous; seeing us at a loss, exhausting all thoughts and considerations on how to pass through this iron wall, you laugh at us as if it were funny; you too must have a very stingy guardian deity lurking within.'

Matsuhiko replied, 'In the heavenly kingdom, there is neither resentment nor sorrow. Neither is there mockery. The reason I laughed was that your guardian deity borrowed my body to speak.'

Tamahiko said, 'If I hear it that way, perhaps that is so. Now, Itsukuhiko-san, Kusunohiko-san, what do your Honshiyugojin say?'

Kusunohiko and Itsukuhiko replied, 'Ah, as yet there has been no declaration. It appears that for a while now, they are in a state of silence. When it comes to this, it is truly shameful. Behind us, we see the splendid goddess guardian deity reflected in the mirror; how grateful we are. The moment we thought that, no matter what, our Mitama must be splendid because our guardian deity is as splendid as that, it vanished in a flash, and afterwards, not even the spiritual garment could be seen. Ah, the negligence of the heart is a fearful thing.'

§ 2

Matsuhiko asked, 'Is there not something that you have dropped along the way?'

'Once we have cast aside the Shifuchiyakushin of the Shiyaba, there is nothing left to drop or not to drop. If we must name something we have dropped, it would be that very Shifuchiyakushin.'

Matsuhiko replied, 'Nay, it is not such a thing as that. For you, if you pass through the Kuwanmon of Takaamahara, you shall be able to pass through with ease.'

Tamahiko said, 'Come now, Kusunoki-san, Izuhiko-san, can you not recall something that you have dropped?'

Izuhiko answered, 'Oh, I have recalled it. As we descended the Kajikatauge, the memory arises that I dropped one Taisetsu horse and one of my own Nikutai. If you speak of what was dropped, well, it would be such things. Tell me, Matsuhiko-san, is it impossible to pass through here without retrieving the Shigai of a horse and the Shigai of a human?'

Matsuhiko said, 'That is so. Go and retrieve the Shigai of the horse and the Shigai of the human. If you do so, you shall be able to pass through with ease.'

Tamahiko said, 'Wait a moment, if you please. Is what you are saying not a little bit of a Datsusen? How could we possibly pass through the Kuwanmon by bringing such Musakuru Shigai into a Tengoku that is Shimenreirō? In a Tengoku that is pure above all that is pure, there is no reason to drag a dead horse along, for it cannot be ridden, nor can it take even a Hitoashi; truly, you speak of things that I cannot comprehend.'

Matsuhiko said, 'Come now, if you but bring that dropped horse and the human Shigai back to life, you shall be able to pass through magnificently. Pray, consult earnestly with your Hon-shugoshin for a moment. I shall wait here until then.'

Tamahiko said, 'Ah, I am sorry to trouble you when you are so busy.'

Izuhiko, clapping his hands, exclaimed, 'Ah, I understand, I understand; through the whisperings of the Hon-shugoshin, all things have been resolved. To say that we dropped the horse meant that the reins of the horse of the heart had loosened and it had escaped somewhere. To say that we dropped the Shigai was a riddle meaning that our Mitama had yearned for the beautiful scenery of Tengoku and had let our souls fly into the void. And one thing most Taisetsu of all was the recitation of the Amatsu-norito and the ensemble of the Kamigoto necessary for this journey through the Shinkai. This is what is meant by the winnowing basket leaking. When we were in the Genkai, we sang the Sendenka with all our might and recited the Kototama of the Amatsu-norito morning and evening. The recitation of that Kototama was for the sake of our own salvation in Tengoku and to save many others. Yet, while soaring into Tengoku, which was our goal, we had somehow lost the very Mitama of the Senden-shi, and the horse of the heart had become ecstatic and scattered into the air. Ah, Tengoku is a place where one cannot be off one's guard; it is a place of splendid care and a place of fear. Come, come, everyone, let us recite the Amatsu-norito facing this Kagami-iwa.'

§ 3

Thereupon, the Ichidō felt as if the night had broken, and rising with courage, they recited the Amatsunorito with Itsushinfuran for about Momotabi. The rock of the mirror opened of its own accord to the Saiu, and a pure Daidau, adorned with Tantan flowers, appeared before them.

The Sannin spoke in unison: 'O Matsuhiko-sama, we thank you. Through your Okagesama, we have passed the Nankuwan in Buji. As this is a Riyokau in the Shinkai to which we are unaccustomed, and we know not the Katsute, we pray you to grant us your Osewa.'

Matsuhiko replied: 'Iyaiya, you must do your own affairs yourselves. In the Genkai, did you not go about saying that one should never lean upon others as a staff, nor rely upon a Shishiyō?'

The Sannin said: 'Ahaha, the Keshiki is so fair and our Kibun so uplifted that we had forgotten all things. If that be so, then Yatsupa, attachment is necessary after all.'

Matsuhiko said: 'That is by no means attachment. It is the lifeline that protects your Mitama. Come, let us hasten.'

From the distance, a group of five men and women, each about two shaku in height, approached them while holding hands, moving in a swaying manner.

Tamahiko said: 'Lo, small beings have appeared. This place seems to be the Island of Dwarfs. Do such small humans dwell in the heavenly realm? Tell me, Matsuhiko-san.'

Matsuhiko said: 'Nay, not only in the Shinkai, but in the Genkai it is likewise. Those called the greatest of giants are but three shaku or so, and one is considered a full-grown human at one shaku and eight sun. This is the unity of the manifest and the hidden; the Reitai of humans dwelling in the Genkai have come to play here in this Takamagahara. Unless they walk holding hands in that manner, cranes will descend and carry them to high places; thus, to prevent such a Nankuwan, they walk holding hands.'

Tamahiko said: 'Truly, I understand less and less. We Sannin, starting from the Land of Tokoyo, have traversed the nations of the world and have, in our estimation, traveled throughout the Genkai; yet, no matter how small the humans, there were no men or women shorter than six shaku. Even infants of the Genkai possess that much stature. Are you not, perhaps, mistaken?'

Matsuhiko said: 'It was nearly three hundred and fifty thousand years ago that humans of six shaku or more dwelt there. Since you departed from the Genkai at the Kajika Pass, thirty-five thousand years have already elapsed. The Genkai has surpassed the era known as the twentieth century, when humans of small souls dwelt, and three thousand years have already passed since then. In terms of the Genkai, it is today, the fiftieth century since the appearance of Christ. As the world gradually opened, humans upon the earth came to loathe labor, traveling between heaven and earth by riding electric trains, automobiles, steam trains, windmills, and winged carriages, and because they use neither hands nor feet, their bodies have become increasingly frail, and thus, in this day of the fiftieth century, they have become such feeble humans. However, limited to the lineage of humans who, from the end of the nineteenth century through the twentieth, sprouted the teachings of the San-Go-Kyo, striving for silent execution and finding joy in labor, there exists the Miroku race, who, contrary to that, maintain a physique of six shaku or more and are active in the Gen, Yū, and Shinkai as living shrines of God.'

§ 4

The Sannin said, 'We believed that we had met our Rakumei at Kajikatauge last Sakuya, yet has it already been Sanjifugomannen? Even if there be no Jikan in the Shinkai, is this not too swift?' Matsuhiko replied, 'Come, let us speak at leisure upon our arrival at the Seichi. The Kamisama awaits us; let us proceed by and by.'

So saying, he walked ahead, and the Sannin followed Matsuhiko with eager hearts. Suddenly, they arrived at the shore of a Kosui that unfolded before their eyes. Kinpa and Ginpa stretched forth, vast as Giyorin, reflecting the Nitsukuō in a Saukuwan beyond compare. Mifune of Menashikatama, adorned with Shitsupōshiyugiyoku, floated in numbers beyond count. Matsuhiko leapt lightly into the most beautiful and new of these vessels, invited the Sannin to Dōjiyō, and, plying the oar himself, drifted gracefully upon the waves toward the Seinan. The surface of the lake, tracing ripples as if painted with the seven-colored waves of the Nitsukuō, bore them onward as they sang a brave boatman's song, rowing with vigor toward the Takamagahara of the Seichi.

Beyond the waves, floating above the mist, stood a golden palace and jade tower inlaid with the seven treasures—gold tiles, silver pillars, pearls, agates, corals, lapis lazuli, amber, and crystals—shimmering in the light of the sun and illuminating the universe with a majesty that defies description. At length, the vessel reached an island. The ground was paved entirely with a pure courtyard of gold, silver, and pearls. The eastern gate was fortified with a colossal pearl; the west was enclosed by a divine gate of agate, the south by a divine gate of lapis lazuli, and the north by a divine gate of crystal. To the northeast was a gate of platinum, to the Seinan a gate of white silver, to the northwest a gate of gold, and to the southeast a gate of agate. Furthermore, eight smaller gates were each inlaid with rare jewels, their grandeur and beauty beyond the power of pen or tongue to exhaust. Matsuhiko first led the Sannin through the eastern gate, and they advanced with solemn steps. At the entrance, beautiful angels of both genders, their faces brimming with smiles, were welcoming the party. Matsuhiko acknowledged these beautiful angels with a bow, and together with the Sannin, he proceeded deeper and ever deeper into the interior.
