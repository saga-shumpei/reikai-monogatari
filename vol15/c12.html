Chapter 12: A Solitary Journey [579]

一人旅〔579〕

§ 1

The eight million Amatsukamitachi, The eight million Kunitsukamitachi, Bearing a hundred Tsumitoga upon His single body, Drenched and sodden like a water-soaked rat, Feeling as though pursued by a cat, Through the winter fields where the cold blast rages, To meet the Mother Mikoto, His departing figure is truly ijira. The Ihato of Ama was flung open, And for a moment it became the age of the pure Kami, Yet without even time for the radiance to spread, The foul Mitama of Adaru-no-hiko and Ebahime Came forth in wild turbulence. Upon the mountain ridges and the river shallows, The wind is foul-smelling and the earth is rotted, The rivers are filled to overflowing with Dakusui, The rain continues to fall by day and by night, His body advancing, drifting and wandering, Having no straw cape and no bamboo hat, He stopped by a certain Iheji.

When He asked for a lodging for Ichiya, A rough man came out answering abruptly; Looking upon the face of that man, Oh, what could this be, oh, what could it be, It was the couple Onikumohiko. Beneath the mountain of Chikyō, Where Kisekikuwaigan stand in rows, By the side of the valley, slenderly, The rising smoke was also faint; In the depths, a groaning voice was heard. Kamususanō-no-Ōkami, Without uttering a word, opened the door, And as He stepped closer to look, A Yamataorochi, sinuously, Coiled throughout the entire room, Red blood over its whole body Was smeared and spread most dreadfully; As soon as it saw the Mikoto, it was startled, And instantly blew forth its poisonous breath. Those whom He thought to be Onikumohiko,

Were entirely the incarnations of the great serpent; She whom He thought to be Onikumohime Was a golden-furred, white-faced, nine-tailed old fox In service to the great serpent. Opening the back door, thud-thud, Looking back and looking back again, They fled toward the deep mountains. Kamususanō-no-Ōkami, The Great Ritual Prayer of the Amatsu-Norito Chanted forth with a clear voice; To pacify and save these Magatsubi By the breath of the Kototama, With His heart devoted, the counting song One, two, three, four, five, six, Seven, eight, nine, ten, By the Kototama of hundreds, thousands, and ten thousands, Even the so very thick Yamataorochi Vanished away like smoke. 'Ah, how strange,' the Ōkami said, And as He fixed His gaze to look,

That which appeared to be a house was a grassy plain, With only the voices of insects, leaving no trace. Enveloped in a cloud of doubt, Taking the mountain of Chikyō as His mark, He climbed, panting for breath. Just then, the mountain wind came blowing, And His eight-grasp beard, shaggy and wild, Was blown by the wind and scattered. The autumn leaves on the treetops Illuminated the red sincerity of the Mikoto; It was truly admirable.

Susano-no-Mikoto sat upon a rock by the roadside halfway up Mount Chikyō, and while He was recollecting the details of the concealment in the Ihato in Takamanohara and was shedding tears of resentment, suddenly from the top of the mountain, a succession of sounds, loud enough to split even the rocks, was heard approaching.

The mysterious sounds drew nearer moment by moment. Susano-no-Mikoto, thinking that the evil deity of the great serpent might have attacked again, stood up abruptly, and while taking a stance with His hand upon the hilt of His sword, He waited; then, a giant of a man, nearly piercing the clouds, along with forty or fifty followers, stood blocking the way before the Mikoto with his arms spread wide.

素盞嗚尊は、地教山の中腹なる道の辺の巌に腰打ち掛け、高天原に於ける磐戸隠れの顛末を追懐し、無念の涙にくれ居たまふ時こそあれ、忽ち山上より岩石も割るるばかりの音響陸続として聞え来る。

怪しの物音は刻々に近づき来たる。素盞嗚尊は又もや大蛇の悪神襲来せるかと、ツト立ち上り、剣の握に手をかけて身構へしつつ待ち居たまへば、雲突く許りの大男四五十人の手下と共に、尊の前に大手を拡げて立ち塞がり、

"Ho! Thou must be Takehayasusanōnomikoto, the very instigator of the Akuma who didst confine the Sumeōkami within the Ihato of Ama in the Takaamahara of Tenkyōzan. Thou shalt not ascend this mountain even by a single Itsusun."

Thus did he shout, yet Mikoto replied with gentle Kotoba:

"I am indeed Susanōnomikoto, who, as thou sayest, was expelled from Takaamahara by Kamuyara. Nevertheless, upon this mountain of Chikyō, Wagahaha dwelleth in Tokoshihe; therefore, thinking to obtain Haigan but for Ichido and thereby determine the Shintai of my person, I have come Harubaru unto Koko. If thou knowest the pathos of things, open this path for once and let me meet my mother."

As He spoke with humility, the great man grew insolent; with labored Hanaiki, he thrust forth arms like unto those of a Niō and said:

"In the Kotoba of a Danshi there are no Nigon. When I say it shall not be, it is Zetsutai that it shall not be. Tatohe the Tenchi be overturned, or the world of Miroku should come, never, never, so long as Wareware keep watch, shall we permit thee to ascend this mountain by even one minute or one Itsusun. If thou art determined to ascend, then twist these arms and go up. I have received the command of the Ōkami who dwelleth upon Tenkyōzan: should Susanōnomikoto by any chance come ascending this mountain, cut him down with the Tsumugari sword. Such is the stern decree. Should we permit thee to ascend even one step beyond this rock, Wareware and our kindred could no longer exist between Tenchi. Wast thou not formerly the ruler of the Reed Plains? Thou shouldst understand the reason of things. Begone, begone! Depart from this place this very instant!"

"Ah, it cannot be helped. If it must be so, then spread thy hindrances as thou wilt; I shall resolutely ascend for the sake of meeting my mother."

As He attempted to ascend composedly through the midst of the thronging people, the great man extended his long arms with a sudden grip and cried:

"Ho, ho, ho! Whom dost thou think I am? To confess the truth, I am known as Onitsukama, the attendant of Onikumohiko, the great chieftain of Brahmanism!"

So saying, he firmly seized Mikoto by the breast. Mikoto, saying it was a vexation, raised one foot and gave a sudden kick; at that moment, the body of Onitsukama glided through the air for some four or five Ken and landed with a thud in the midst of a nearby forest, where he lay groaning, having struck his skull. Mikoto, unconcerned with the details, strode with great steps up the steep slope, and the many retainers, daunted by this might, hid themselves in the surrounding forests like the young of spiders scattering in all directions.

§ 3

Even as the Mikoto quickened His pace and ascended the Kifuhan, from the thicket of trees by the side, a Metsupofukai and Kiyodai Orochi suddenly thrust forth its head and lay across the Rojiyō, obstructing the Tsūro of the Mikoto and moving not.

The Mikoto, being hindered by the Orochi, was in a state of slight Tauwaku and for a time was lost in Shian, when from the Sanjiyō a Riuriyō Ongaku resounded, and Amata beautiful Shinjin appeared in due order at this place; among them, Hitohashira of Kaushiyō and Iubi Megami, facing Susanōnomikoto, spoke:

‘O, beloved Susanōnomikoto, I am thy mother Izanaminomikoto. The purity of thy heart shineth and sparkleth like the Jitsugetsu in Takaamahara. Nevertheless, as it is thy Tenpu no Shiyokuseki to save the sins and pollutions of the Amata Shinjin who come forth in Ōyashimakuni, thou shalt bear the Chikura no Okido and travel widely through the Sekai in Henreki, tasting Arayuru Kannan and hardships. Until thou hast purified the hearts of the Oni, Orochi, evil foxes, hags, and Magatsumi that coil in heaven and earth, and hast aided the good and pacified the evil, and hast cut down the Yamata-no-Orochi with the ten-span sword, and obtained the Murakumo-no-Tsurugi which he possesseth to offer it unto Amaterasu-Ōmikami who dwelleth upon Mount Tenkyō, from this moment forth I am not thy mother, nor art thou my child. Depart from this mountain with all haste. We shall meet again. In the Ashihara-no-Kuni where the Magatsu rage and run wild, do thou exercise great care.’

As He beheld this, the form vanished like smoke, and thereafter remained only the sound of the wind through the pines blowing across the peak of Mount Chikyō, and the shadow of the Orochi that had obstructed the road was also gone before He knew it.

Susanōnomikoto, having no choice, turned back from this place and descended the Kifuhan, whereupon the former man, Onitsukami, prostrated himself upon the earth and expressed his intent of submission toward the Mikoto, saying:

‘In truth, my claim to be a retainer of Onigumohiko was a falsehood; I received a secret command from a certain noble deity of Takaamahara and was given strict orders to intercept the ascent of Your August Divinity to this mountain. Ah, nevertheless, this present upheaval of the Heavenly Rock Dwelling is not the sin of Your August Divinity; the sin lieth rather with the Amatsukami, and all the deities are but those who in their hearts sympathize with Your August Divinity. From this time forth, I shall reform my heart, express my heartfelt sympathy for the circumstances of Your August Divinity, and desire to share in Thy toils. I pray Thee, for the sake of the myriad people of the world, grant my petition.’

Thus he pleaded with sincerity appearing in his countenance and shedding tears. The Mikoto inquired:

‘How is the wound upon thy head?’

Onitsukami, while showing great awe, replied:

‘Yes, by Thy grace, unknowingly and unconsciously, from the very instant I invoked the August Name of the Great Deity Kamu-Susanō, the pain which was so intense ceased as if forgotten, and the split head hath completely recovered as it was before. I am filled with awe at the divine virtue of the Mizu-tama.’

Thus he joined his hands together, his tears falling drop by drop. Susanōnomikoto greatly rejoiced and said:

‘Since I was expelled from Takaamahara, it hath been a solitary journey in the drizzling rain, and I have felt truly lonely; yet the world is a wondrous thing, for I have gained one sympathizer. Come, from this time forth, thou and I shall become brothers in life, and we shall destroy the demons that coil in Ōyashima, save the myriad people, and manifest our utmost sincerity to the world. Onitsukami, come!’

So saying, He went before him, and while blowing a grass flute and quickening His pace, He sang the Heavenly Number Song to an unknown destination, proceeding toward the southwest.
