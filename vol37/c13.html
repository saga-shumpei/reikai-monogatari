Chapter 13: Smoke の Capital [1025]

煙の都〔1025〕

§ 1

By the command of Matsuokatenshi, Kiraku, for the first time, set out from his home early in the morning to attempt the propagation of the faith in Ōsaka all by himself; with a Tennenbue and a Chinkon-no-tama tucked into a leather bag slung over his shoulder, he crossed Namakubitauge, emerged onto the Ibaragikaidau, and from there boarded a Kishiya to arrive in the land of Ōsaka.

He had heard beforehand that Ōsaka was a capital of smoke. To the eyes of a countryman seeing a great metropolis for the first time, he was as bewildered as a frog from a well cast into the vast ocean, and he became unable to tell where or how he should go. The thick black smoke rising from countless chimneys looked as if dragons were dancing in the mid-air, and the sound of Bashiya and Jinshiya coming and going was so thunderous that he doubted if he were hearing peals of thunder; utterly dumbfounded, he wandered aimlessly from Umeda Station, proceeding south and further south. He finally reached the park in Nakanoshima, and while paying his respects at the Hōkoku Shrine, which enshrines Toyotōkō, and at the large granite stone of the monument to the loyalty of Kimura Shigenari, which was cracked diagonally from the center, he wandered about the vicinity, intending to seek out the home of a woman named Okuni, who had been the former wife of Tada Kame. This was because Tada Koto, being the daughter of Tada Kame, had already gone there to propagate the faith, and he thought to join forces with both of them to attempt a great proclamation in this land.

Ōsaka, the capital of water, has its mid-air wrapped in smoke and its land surrounded by rivers. Bridges that look alike are built in such great numbers. It is said that there are nearly a thousand bridges built within the city of Ōsaka, but only two or three are actually named 'Bridge' (Hashi), while all the others are simply 'hashi'. In this great metropolis of nothing but bridges, such as Tenmabashi, Tenjinbashi, and Naniwabashi, he wandered from house to house, wondering, 'Is Okuni-san's house here?'—such was his foolishness; he had forgotten to ask for Okuni-san's address in detail from Tada Kame when he left his home province. Being raised in the country, Kiraku had thought that if he only mentioned the name, it would be understood immediately as it would be in a small village, so he had not asked for the address in detail. He had made the hasty assumption that no matter how vast Ōsaka might be, if he asked at a police box, he would surely find out where Okuni-san was. However, having come to Ōsaka for the first time, he had not the slightest clue. Even when he went to a police box to ask, he was only laughed at by the patrolman; it was utterly impossible with only the name 'Okuni-san' without an address or surname. Suddenly, he remembered that he had heard beforehand that a man named Saitō Saichi from Anō, who lived next door to his own home, ran a rice cake shop in a place called Kūshin-chō, near the end of Tenmabashi. He recalled this and began to think of asking for him. He reasoned that it would be best to be guided by a rickshaw man who knew the geography well, and so he asked a rickshaw man who happened to be pulling an empty rickshaw toward the end of the bridge:

Kiraku: 'Please take me to the rice cake shop of a man named Saitō Saichi in Kūshin-chō.'

The rickshaw man said:

Rickshaw man: 'Kūshin-chō is quite a distance, so please give me twenty-five sen.'

He immediately handed twenty-five sen to the rickshaw man. Then, he was taken across the long Tenmabashi to the south, and after crossing another large bridge, he was brought back to the vicinity of the original bridge. He was dropped off in front of a house with a narrow frontage, slightly recessed at the end of the bridge. Looking closely, it was written 'Jissei-mochi'. Nodding as he thought, 'Ah, this is the place,'

Kiraku: 'Excuse me.'

He said and stepped inside. It was a very narrow house. In the end, he had been dropped off at the very place where he had first boarded the rickshaw. Thinking about it later, he realized he had been taken across Tenjinbashi to the south, then back across Tenmabashi to the north, and driven around the town only to be dropped off at the original spot. Being a countryman, this twenty-eight-year-old youth felt ashamed that this was his first time riding in a rickshaw. The master of Jissei-mochi looked at his face and said:

Master: 'Ah, is that Kiraku-san? You are most welcome. I hear rumors lately that you have become very devout; that is excellent. Well then, please take a rest.'

he said, slightly softening his words. Thereupon, Kiraku left forty yen in the care of the clerk here, put the remaining small change in his bosom, wandered around here and there, visited various churches, looked into Inari-lowering and the like, and spent about two weeks without any effect whatsoever. And as for Tada Koto's mother's house, no matter what, he could not find it. When night came, he would return to the inn, perform Chinkon, ask questions and answers to himself, and consult divine oracles, but not even a bit did Matsuoka-san or Oshimo-san teach him; they only taught him empty misses and never spoke the truth.

Two weeks passed, and the inn clerk,

Clerk: 'Guest-san, I request the settlement.'

said and brought the bill. When he looked closely, it was written that a single day's lodging was two yen and eighty sen, making thirty-nine yen and twenty sen for two weeks. When he had previously stayed at a country inn, it was twenty-five sen. No matter how expensive Osaka is, if you pay fifty yen a day it should be all right... thinking this, the countryman Kiraku was thunderstruck that the vast majority of his travel expenses, which he had relied upon as the lifeline of his existence, had all been taken by unexpected lodging fees, and immediately had eighty sen returned from the deposited money, handing it over as a tip. Ultimately, the forty yen had only been seen when he handed it to the clerk two weeks ago, and that became a long farewell. To tell the truth, this fifty yen was money borrowed by mortgaging his own house and estate, since in the cattle-raising business the three-member cooperative could not do anything with the money. Wandering around there, seeing plays, listening to rakugo, seeing shows, and paying rickshaw fares, seven or eight yen of the money had vanished. Already, there was nothing more than a little over one yen left in his bosom.

This inn was written as Tamaya.

It was a brand-new white-wood building by the riverbank.

Leaving this Tamaya, I passed under the torii of Tenmatenjin-sama in Kitaku and made a worship to bid farewell to Ōsaka.

Inside the sacred grounds, four or five red-crested cranes were kept enclosed by a wire net.

At the same time, an old man of about sixty was selling loaches.

The old man caught hold of worshippers and shouted:

Old Man: 'Everyone, please offer loaches to the crane-san! One serving is one itsusen.'

When I looked, small loaches resembling udon were served together with water in pairs inside shallow bamboo tubes.

When I paid one itsusen to buy a loach and threw it into the shallow puddle where the crane was standing, it thrust in its long beak and immediately took and ate it.

I have heard that the crane is an auspicious bird.

I had seen many cranes painted in pictures, but since it was the first time I saw a living one, driven by curiosity I exchanged a one-sen bronze coin and gave all the bamboo bowls of arranged loaches to the crane.

Then, on the other side, a white-haired steed, stamping its feet roughly, was tapping the wooden floor with the sound of tontonton.

If you look for a moment, there too, boiled soybeans served in seven or eight earthenware plates are placed, and it is written:

'Please give one plate for one itsusen to the horse-san.'

Since I had thoroughly given five portions of loaches to the crane and spent five sen on it, feeling I could not leave this horse without giving anything either, I treated the horse to thirty-five grains of boiled beans by spending a white copper coin, and was in high spirits.

At that time, from behind, a voice was heard calling:

Voice: 'Sensei, Sensei!'

Wondering whose voice it was, when I turned back, an elderly man with white hair, hanging a sign for Zhouyi from his desk, wearing a red woolen blanket, sitting on a chair, and facing this way, was beckoning with his hand.

When I looked, five or six fortune-tellers had set up fortune-telling shops here and there within the precincts.

Since that old man's shop was styled as the Kobayashi Fortune-Telling Office, he was likely a man named Kobayashi.

That old grandfather said:

Fortune-teller: 'Sensei, you must be a person from Tanba. Coming out to Ōsaka now to propagate the teachings of the gods is still too early by a chance. Return to the country of Tanba even a moment sooner. And after accumulating training for about ten years, if you come to Ōsaka for missionary work, you will surely succeed. Now is a critical time, and you must not act rashly and blindly.'

He earnestly told me even though I had not asked.

Kiraku thought:

Kiraku: 'What a strange fortune-teller to say such things; I wonder how he found out that I am a person from Tanba.'

Thinking thus, he turned from this side toward the fortune-teller and asked:

Kiraku: 'Sensei, you are like a person of the gods; in what country were you born in the first place?'

When he addressed him thus, the old fortune-teller replied:

Fortune-teller: 'Ever since I was young, for a long time, I entered the Asama-sama church called Fujikō and engaged in training on Mount Fuji.

That being the case, after the Maruyama Church of Fujikō saw its founder Rokurōbē-san incur divine punishment and die in agony, that church was utterly destroyed and now, having become such a pitiful fortune-teller, I am making a living in Ōsaka.

Actually, I am a person named Isamu Kobayashi.

However, this is just a provisional name; the true name will come the time when I meet you again and disclose it.

First of all, I wish you well and hope you accomplish your training.

The hardships and suffering you will experience after returning to your Tanba from now on, even when thinking of it from now, truly fills me with a pitiful feeling.

However, since this too is for the sake of the gods and for the sake of the world, please endure it.'

Even his voice saying this was clouded with tears.

Kiraku involuntarily prostrated himself, unable to utter a single word, and was choked with falling tears.

After a while, when he raised his head and looked, the figure of the old fortune-teller who had just called himself Isamu Kobayashi had vanished without a trace, no one knew where he had gone.

Encountering such a wondrous event, Kiraku folded his arms, tilted his head, and stood mutely in a daze for a while.

易者『俺は若い時から長らくの間、富士講と云つて浅間様の教会に這入り、富士の山で修業をして居つたものだ。さうした処、富士講の丸山教会は、教祖の六郎兵衛サンが神罰を蒙つて悶死されてから、其教会は滅茶々々に壊れて了ひ今は情ないこんな易者になつて大阪で渡世をしてるのだ。実は私は小林勇と云ふ者である。然し乍ら、これは一寸仮の名だ、本当の名は再びお前に会ふて打明かす時が来るであらう。先づ達者にして修業を成さるが宜しい。これからお前サンの丹波に帰つてから十年間の艱難辛苦といふものは、今から思ふても真に可哀相な気がする。然し乍らこれも神様のため、世の中の為めだから辛抱しなさい』

と云ふ声さへも涙に曇つて居る。喜楽は思はず俯伏し、一言も発し得ず落涙に咽んで居た。暫くして頭をあげて見れば、今小林勇と云つた老易者の影は何処へ行つたか跡方もなく消えて居つた。かかる不思議に出会ふた喜楽は手を組み首を傾けて暫し茫然と佇んで居た。

§ 4

"Ah, was not the person just now a Keshin of Kamisama?"

The Kyōkun I received on the night of Niguwatsu Kokonoka—a night impossible to forget—when invited to Takakumayama by Fuyōsennin Matsuoka Tenshi, welled up in my chest as if Imasara.

The Tairiyaku of Matsuoka Tenshi's Kyōkun at that time was as follows.

'At this present time when violent Jiyashin lean toward Geukimatsupō, you must conduct Kenkiu on the Shisui and Shijiyun Daidau of Kamunagara, purify your Mitama, become a Ritsupa Sendenshi, face the Sekai, blow loud the Ratsupa of Shindau, and bring Kakusei to the Sekai. If at this present time there is no one who will perform Senden of the Daidau of Kamunagara and awaken the eyes of the Sekai, it will not be Deki to effect the Iji of Konnichi's Shiyakuwai. Consequently, that it will bring about the destruction of the Sekai is as clear as looking into a mirror. You must from now on become a servant of God, becoming a light to the dark Sekai, warmth to a cold-hearted Shiyakuwai, salt that saves and purifies rotten Mitama, and medicine that heals the diseases of Mitama; polishing the four spirits and forging the five emotions, becoming a true Yamato spirit, praised as the blossom of heaven and earth and rejoiced in as their fruit, devoting yourself for the sake of the world and the sake of the Way. To shine forth true courage, true parenthood, true love, and true wisdom in order to accomplish this great duty is by no means an easy undertaking. The coming ten years shall be a period of Kenkiu for you. The hardships and suffering that will arise during that time shall be extraordinary. Without enduring these, thou canst not fulfill thy mission. Thou shalt often meet with trials from God, and be surrounded and tormented by hordes of Jiyashin. Ahead of thee, there shall be deep valleys, mountains of swords, hells of blood ponds, chambers of serpents, chambers of bees, and suffering amidst raging winds and violent waves, and thy very life will often be in danger. Thou must be prepared even to have the nails of thy hands and feet pulled out and to be banished by divine divine-expulsion. Nevertheless, there is no need to fear in the least. Take Kamisama as thy strength and truth as thy staff, and charge forward fiercely. No matter what disaster thou mayest encounter, thou must never retreat. Consider everything as the divine plan of God. Thou must not turn away from the Way of God or change thy heart merely because of temporary failure or hardship. Revere the divine will of the God of 5-6-7 as long as thy life lasts, and expand it unto the Sekai. The deities shall illuminate thy body, attend by thy side, and protect thee so that thou mayest fulfill this mission. Especially these ten years are the most necessary period of ascetic practice.'

Such a strict divine oracle had been deeply engraved in my mind. That I now received a similar Kyōkun from a mysterious old diviner named Isamu Kobayashi was truly wondrous.

From here, I bowed before the shrine, clapped my hands, bowed twice, chanted the Amatsu-norito, and left the shrine precincts; with a lonely pocket, I decided to set out in search of Sanemori-esa in Kūshin-chō at the foot of Tenma Bridge.

原文（近代文語）

『アヽ今のは神様の化身ではなかつたかなア』

忘れもせない二月の九日の夜、芙蓉仙人松岡天使に高熊山に誘はれて受けた教訓、今更の如く胸に浮かんで来た。

其時の松岡天使の教訓は大略左の通りであつた。

『澆季末法に傾いた邪神の荒ぶ今の時に当つて、お前は至粋至純なる惟神の大道を研究し、身魂を清め、立派な宣伝使となつて世界に向ひ、神道の喇叭を吹き立て、世界を覚醒せなくてはならぬぞよ。今に於て惟神の大道を宣伝し、世界の目を醒ますものが無ければ、今日の社会を維持する事は出来ない。惹いては世界の破滅を招来する事は鏡にかけて見る様だ。お前はこれから神の僕となつて、暗黒世界の光となり、冷酷な社会の温味となり、腐りきつた身魂を救ひ清める塩となり、身魂の病を癒す薬ともなり、四魂を研き五情を鍛へ、誠の大和魂となつて、天地の花と謳はれ果実と喜ばれ、世の為め道の為めに尽して呉れねばならぬ。真の勇、真の親、真の愛、真の智慧を輝かし此大任を完成せむとするは、仲々容易な事業ではない。今後十年の間は其方は研究の時期である。其間に起る所の艱難辛苦は非常なものだ。之を忍耐せなくては汝の使命を果す事は出来ないぞ。屡神の試にも遭ひ、邪神の群に包囲され苦しむ事もあるであらう。前途に当つて深い谷もあり、剣の山や、血の池地獄や、蛇の室、蜂の室、暴風怒濤に苦しみ、一命の危い事も屡あるであらう。手足の爪迄抜かれて、神退ひに退はれる事も覚悟して居らねばならぬ。さり乍ら少しも恐るるには及ばぬ。神様を力に誠を杖に猛進せよ。如何なる災害に遭ふとも決して退却してはならぬ。何事も皆神の御経綸だと思へ。一時の失敗や艱難に出会ふた為めに、神の道に遠ざかり心を変じてはならぬ。五六七の神の御心を、生命の続く限り遵奉し、且世界へ拡充せよ。神々は汝の身を照らし、汝の身辺に附き添ふて、此使命を果すべく守り玉ふであらう。特に十年間は最も必要な修業時代だ』

との厳しき神示は深く脳裡に刻まれてあつた。其処へ今又小林勇と云ふ不思議な老易者より同じ様な教訓を受けたのは実に不思議であつた。

これより社前に額づき、拍手再拝し天津祝詞を奏上し、社内を退き懐淋しきまま、天満橋詰の空心町の実盛餌を目当に尋ねて行く事とした。
