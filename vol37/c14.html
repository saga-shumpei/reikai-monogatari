Chapter 14: Night の Mountain 路 [1026]

夜の山路〔1026〕

§ 1

Kiraku felt lonely in his heart, felt somewhat disheartened, and was about to finally set out on his journey home. Thinking he would stop by once more to say goodbye at Saitō's house in Kūshinmachi, he visited again, and there, starting with Saichi and his wife, a daughter who had parted ways four years ago after a slight misunderstanding happened to be visiting at a good time. Shige the old woman, showing her refined hospitality, said that though the space was small, he should stay the night and then go home. Since there was a younger sister named Tomino, aged sixteen or seventeen, the five of them ended up sleeping together in a mere four-and-a-half-mat room. An older daughter named Oaki arrived around ten o'clock at night in a rattling cart, and while muttering complaints about something or other, took a female named Oino along with her to share the ride back. Feeling as though his fried tofu had been snatched away by a kite, Kiraku clicked his tongue as he drifted off to sleep. However, since Kiraku at this time was detached from all carnal desires and was completely intoxicated with nothing but faith, even though he met his former woman and slept in the same room, he had no such thoughts whatsoever of rekindling old intimations or anything of the sort. Nevertheless, he felt a certain inexplicable nostalgia and was happy to spend a night in the same house as that woman.

The night wore on relentlessly. Before they knew it, the four of them had fallen soundly asleep.

The long spring day gradually leaned to the west, casting the golden-scaled light upon the waters of the Yodo River; standing upon Tenjin Bridge in the water capital of Naniwa, where the rapids of the river mouths run deep, he tilted his head and was lost in thought. When he looked toward the southeast, the magnificent and grand Osaka Castle, built up sheer like a mountain range, was reflected in the water, and its tiles glittered brightly in the setting sun. Kiraku was struck with emotion at the sight of this and was speaking to himself.

Kiraku: 'Ah, human destiny is a mysterious thing. Toyotomi Hideyoshi, who as a mischievous boy once wrapped in straw mats slept on the two-hundred-and-eight-ken Yahagi Long Bridge, did not let his efforts of endurance and perseverance be in vain; achieving his grand ambition of leaping over the dragon gate, with glorious and awe-inspiring virtue, kicking away the waves of the rising sun in the Eastern Sea, he finally seized mastery over the realm of more than sixty provinces, subjugated the Three Koreas, startled the Great Ming monarch, and transmitted an immortal achievement through the ages to posterity. He is truly a hero delightful to hear about. Even the Taikō Toyotomi is a child born of human beings. He too was a person born from ordinary peasantry. The circumstances of the Taikō's early youth strikingly resemble those of Kiraku's own time. Not the Yahagi Bridge, but the foot of Tenjin Bridge—I too must ponder something here myself. The travel funds which I went through much trouble to scrape together have somehow vanished like smoke in the smoke capital, and I have not a single souvenir to bring back home. When the mind is thoroughly directed, is there anything that cannot be accomplished? I cannot content myself merely with the level of the Taikō's success. Using God as my strength and sincerity as my staff, I must solidify the foundation of the 567 divine rule.'

Just at the instant when, amidst the bustling bridge, forgetting himself and roaring with heroic vigor, he was seized by fantasy, there was a child of twelve or thirteen years old whom he bumped into with a thud. Kiraku was startled and kept his gaze fixed upon the child's face. From behind, panting heavily and rushing up, a big man of around thirty with the air of a head clerk caught hold of the child without asking yes or no, and began to subject him to unbridled violence, beating and kicking him. As the child raised a piercing shriek and wept aloud, with the usual curiosity of the Osaka onlookers, the bridge instantly changed into a scene of traffic stoppage, with three, five, then ten people stopping in their tracks. The man with the clerk-like air still continued to stubbornly seize the child's wrist by force, pulling him as though his arm might be dislocated, and

Man: 'Come out to the police station for a moment!'

attempted to drag him away. Kiraku, finding it unbearable to watch, said,

Kiraku: 'Moshimoshi, please wait a moment. I do not know what kind of bad thing he did, but...'

Before he could even finish speaking, the man replied in a harsh voice,

Man: 'You are a traveler down from the countryside, stay out of this. This fellow is a brat from Achibo. He gulped down a dose of Jiboshi powder that was right at the storefront, and is a brazen little scoundrel who slipped away and ran off. To serve as a lesson for the future, I am handing him over to the patrolman at the bridgehead.'

§ 2

Breathing roughly with a fierce expression, he glared down at the child. The child threw down the medicine package there, placed both hands on the ground, and wept tearfully with such a touching air. Thinking that this child surely could not be a congenital thief, Kiraku spoke politely to the big man, apologizing on the child's behalf, and upon listening to what the child had to say,

Child: 'My mother has been suffering from a uterine illness or the like for a long time and now her life is even in danger. Because we are poor, we cannot buy medicine nor have her examined by a doctor, so unable to bear watching mother-san die right before me, I have been deeply worried day after day; but hearing from a neighbor that women's illnesses are surely fully cured by taking Jitsubosan, I suddenly came to desire that medicine, and out of the single-minded thought of holding my mother dear, without knowing what came before or after, I took one dose of Jitsubosan that was on the medicine shop's storefront and ran away.'

Having finished speaking, the child burst into tears and collapsed right on the spot. Moved by the heart of the filial son, Kiraku, unawares, wept in sympathy while searching his bosom to pull out fifty sen,

Kiraku: 'Please sell this medicine to me, and forgive the child's crime.'

When he said this, the big man puffed up his face and said,

Man: 'This fellow is an unforgivable wretch, but today, sparing you, I will let it go, so henceforth be sure to behave properly!'

Scolding him foully, he snatched away the fifty sen for a medicine costing ten sen, and with such ruthlessness went away without even paying change, shoulders hunched in anger. Thinking him a man devoid of blood or tears, showing the color of anger, he watched the departing man's figure go with gnashing teeth.

Setting out on a grass pillow journey, I realized: Ah, the terrifying heart of man!

Calling it Osaka, he had thought it one of Japan's three major metropolises, a land of commercial development and an earthly paradise of only the Seven Gods of Fortune, but now seeing and hearing the plight of poor children right before his eyes, he realized that wherever one goes, autumn surely brings autumn, winter is surely winter, and the dark realm is the same in both countryside and capital. Sighing repeatedly, the voice of lamentation 'Ah, ah,' entered the ears of Oshige-bā-san sleeping nearby,

Oshige-bā-san: 'Hey, hey, Kiraku-san, what sort of sleep-talking are you uttering?'

Being shaken awake and coming to his senses, he found himself sleeping in the narrow four-and-a-half tatami room of the rice-cake shop. He came to feel in later days that the events inside the dream upon the bridge just now were brought about by the divine will to spur Kiraku's heart, remind him that he had a mother and an old grandmother in his hometown, and arrange for him to return home quickly.

Encouraged by the fortune-teller's words, To return to the land of Tanba, Spurring the horse of his heart, Without even calling a rickshaw, plodding along, He arrived at Umeda Station. When he searched his pocket To make preparations, how pitiful, The remaining money was two and a half sen. Though there were trains, he had no means to ride them. How so? Right down the middle of the railway track, In a straight line on his own two legs. Even his stomach rumbling at the posting station of Suita, The steamed potatoes sold at the tea-shouse Are sweeter than chestnuts, the fifty-three ri distance, Step by step, Taking Ibaraki Town to the north, Heading back toward Tanba, Just as the fifteenth-night moon of the fourth month Revealed its round face at the edge of the eastern mountains,

Though telling it to smile cheerfully, How somehow lonely is the evening sky on a solitary journey; East, west, north, and south, Without a single acquaintance, weeping along the mountain path, Taking the moonlight of the sky as his strength, With a vague recollection of the mountain-to-mountain valley path he had once traversed, Feeling somewhere uneasy, Stopping at a place where roads diverged, Just as he tilted his head in thought, Suddenly appearing before him, A strange traveler in white garments Walked on, standing four or five paces ahead. If Kiraku advanced, he advanced; If he stopped, the other stopped again. Calling out, 'Moshiboshi,' Though he called, the white shadow answered not; Now appearing, now vanishing, Its supernatural manifestation and disappearance was mysterious.

Appearing at the fork in the road, It seemed to act as a guide once more. Though feeling suspicious, yet gaining strength, He moved his feet, but due to hunger and fatigue He could hardly advance, Assailed by the demon of sleepiness, Even while collapsing onto the street, Enduring the drowsiness, he journeyed back. Nearing the outskirts of Nishi-Betsuin village, He approached a downward slope. In the Hour of the Ox, when even the water makes no sound, Across the narrow Hosodani River on one side of the road, there was a small graveyard, And the small roof enshrining the Six Jizō-sama could be seen. Thinking to shelter himself from rain and dew here, Even while knowing it to be an eerie graveyard, Rejoicing at it as a gift from heaven, He laid his body down behind the six stone Jizō statues lined up in a row.

あたり寂然として静まり返る時しもあれ、夢か現か幻か、吾枕頭に近く聞ゆる女の忍び泣く声、幽かに耳に入ると共にフと目をさませば、喜楽は六地蔵の後に横たはつてゐることに気が付いた。

喜楽の頬に、冷たい水のしぶきがかかる。キツと目をあけて見れば六地蔵の前に余り背の高くない、横太い怪しい一人の女が、赤ん坊を背に負ひ乍ら、土瓶のやうな物を片手に提げ、石地蔵の頭から、『南無阿弥陀仏』といひ乍ら、冷たい水をかけて、何事か切りに、石地蔵に訴へてゐるやうである、喜楽は轟く心臓の鼓動を強て鎮圧し、息を殺して伺ひ居れば、怪しき女の影は一歩々々とゆるぐが如く、しづしづとして新しい墓の前に到り、マツチをすり蝋燭を点じ、合掌し乍ら泣き声になつて、伏し屈んでゐる。石地蔵の立つてゐる隙間から、此様子を覗きみた喜楽は俄に恐怖心にかられ、頭の毛はちぢみ、体はふるひ出し、寸時もここに居たたまらず、厭らしさに此場を逃げ出さうかと思つたが、腹の底から小さい声で、『待て』と云ふやうに聞えて来た。此声に自分は再び胴をすえ、直日に省りみることを得た。……喜楽は顕幽両界の救済者たらむとする霊学の修業者である、今幸ひにして斯の如き怪霊に出会し、研究の好材料を得たのは全く神さまの御心であらう。よく考へて見れば天が下に素より妖怪変化のあるべき筈がない、何れも皆心の迷ひから怖くない者が怖くなつたりするのである。何でもない者を妖怪変化だと思つて、昏迷誑惑其度を失はむとしたのは、何たる卑怯であらう。長途の旅にて心身疲労の結果、こんな妄想に陥つたのではあるまいか……と、キツと心胆を据え、目を見はれば妖怪でも幽霊でもなく、田舎婦人が何事か急の出来事の為に、此真夜中に亡き夫の墓に参つたのであるらしく、稍久しく祈つた後、『南無阿弥陀仏』と力なげに口ずさみ乍ら、ヨボヨボと元来し細谷川を渡つて、其姿は木立に紛れて見えなくなつて了つた。

女の姿の消えしより、喜楽も亦俄に恐ろしくなつて来た。永居はならじとソロソロ立上り、頬かぶりをなし、尻をひつからげ、コワゴワ渓流を渡り、山路に出でたる一刹那、

『怖いツ！』

といふ子供の叫び声が、つい足許に聞えて来た。喜楽は此声に二度ビツクリし乍ら、

『何にも怖いことはない、俺は人間だ！』

と呼ばはりつつ後ふり向きもせず、一目散に足の痛みも忘れて、法貴谷の方へと走せ帰るのであつた。
