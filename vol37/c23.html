Chapter 23: Sea 老坂 [1035]

海老坂〔1035〕

§ 1

While staying at the home of Kobayashi Teizau for some four or five days, men and women of all ages from throughout the village gathered, received Chinkon, and engaged in the spiritual training of Kamugakari, giving thanks for the boundless Shintoku, and all became believers. Kobayashi-san begged him to stay at his home for at least a month, but ignoring this, Kiraku left the house, received a reward of some two or three yen from the believers, and intending to cross over to Kitakuhada with it, set out towards Ebisakatauge in accordance with the divine command before dusk.

The sun sank completely, and on top of that, being on a mountain path, he could no longer advance even a single step. An old temple stood in the middle of this mountain path. And an image of Jizau was enshrined in the old hall. Having no other choice, thinking he would enter this hall and spend the night, Kiraku lay down on the floor in front of the Buddhist altar and fell fast asleep just like that.

As a strange sound was heard around midnight, when he suddenly opened his eyes and looked, a large priest carrying a black lantern was standing at the entrance of the hall, chanting the Heart Sutra in a loud voice. When Kiraku woke up in surprise, the priest shouted in a loud voice,

Priest: 'Who is the rascal sleeping in this hall without permission? Outrageous, come on, get out quickly!'

He barked at him. Kiraku stood up, lowered his head slightly, and said,

Kiraku: 'O-Oshō-san, I have truly done an inexcusable thing, but I am a practitioner of spirit science, one who walks about to spread Shinto, but because the road was unexpectedly far, dusk fell on the way, and I took the liberty of imposing upon you. Please let me lodge here just for tonight.'

Priest: 'Is there such a thing as a Shinto practitioner lodging in a Buddhist hall? You-san are a sham Shintoist. You are a mercenary priest, aren't you?'

forgetting that he himself was a priest, he raised an edge in his voice and shouted.

Kiraku: 'Both the Kami and Buddha are originally from the same stock, so please do not make such distinctions and let me lodge just for tonight.'

Priest: 'Hmm, is that so? Do you say that Kami and Buddha are of the same stock? You certainly say things that are well understood. If so, please lodge tonight. However, since nothing can be done here, please come toward the quarters. Somehow the Jizau hall has been on my mind and I could not sleep, so I came to take a quick look. I will treat you to some tea.'

At this completely transformed and relaxed attitude, Kiraku finally breathed a sigh of relief, and guided by the large priest, followed him toward the quarters.

In this temple, apart from this priest himself, no one who seemed to be a person lived there. When they slowly began to talk about their personal histories with each other, mysteriously, this priest was the child of the elder brother of Hitomi Yayoshi, a person from Imazu, Chiyokawa Village, Minamikuwada District, into whose family Kiraku's aunt had married. He was a man named Hitomi Yosaburō with whom he had played four or five times when they were children, and it became known that as a result of dissipation he had squandered his parents' entire property, then became a guard at the Sonobe prison, served as a patrolman, was dismissed also because of liquor, then learned fortune-telling, memorized the secret dharmas of Shintai, and taking advantage of an unassigned temple, had been employed as a caretaker priest. Touched by this unexpected reunion, the two opened their hearts to one another, talked about various things, stayed here for four or five days, taught Hitomi the spiritual art of Chinkon-Kishin, and promising to meet again at a later date, crossed Ebisakatauge to the north, and finally arrived at a rural village called Anpake.

When they did so, an alarm bell suddenly resounded, and drums began to sound. The villagers cried, 'There is a fire in Nozoe!' and desperately rushed out carrying tobisokushi, dragon water pumps, water buckets, and the like. Kiraku also followed behind the villagers and ran as far as the small village called Nozoe. However, smoke was not rising anywhere, and there was nothing that looked like a fire. Nevertheless, the bell and drum of the Nozoe temple were ringing incessantly. According to the account of a person who came running from the opposite direction, it was understood that while digging a deep well, a cave-in had suddenly occurred, burying the well-digging laborers, and the alarm bell and drums were for gathering people from the neighborhood in order to dig them out.

小林貞蔵氏の宅で四五日計り滞在してゐる間に、村中の老若男女が集まり、鎮魂を受けたり、神懸の修行をしたりして、神徳の広大なるに感謝し、全部信者となつて了つた。小林氏はせめて一月計り自分の宅に居つて貰ひ度いと頼むのも聞かずに此家を立出で、信者から二三円計りの礼を貰ひ、それを以て北桑田へ渡らむと、日の暮前から神の命のまにまに海老坂峠まで差かかつた。日はズツポリと暮れ、其上に坂路のこととて、最早一歩も進む事が出来なくなつた。此坂路の中途に古寺が建つてゐる。そして古い堂に地蔵が祀つてあつた。止むを得ず喜楽は此堂へ這入つて一夜を明かさうと、仏壇の前でゴロリと横たはり其儘眠に就いた。

夜中時分に妙な物音がしたので、フト目を醒まして見れば、黒い提灯をさげた大坊主が堂の入口に立ち、大きな声で心経を唱へてゐる。喜楽は驚いて起上ると、坊主は大きな声で、

坊主『断りもなく此堂に寝てゐる奴は何者だ。怪しからぬ、サア早く出て行け！』

と呶鳴りつける。喜楽は起あがり、一寸頭を下げて、

喜楽『お和尚サン、誠に済まぬ事を致しましたが、私は霊学の修行者で、神道を開きに歩いて居る者で御座りますが、思ひの外道が遠かつたので、途中に日が暮れ、一寸失礼さして貰ひました。どうぞ今晩丈此処で泊めて下さいな』

坊主『神道の行者が仏の堂で泊まるといふ事があるものか。お前サンはイカサマ神道家だ。売僧坊主だなア』

と自分の坊主たる事を忘れて、声に角を立てて呶鳴つてゐる。

喜楽『神さまも仏さまも元は一株だから、そんな区別を立てずに今晩丈泊めて下さい』

坊主『ウンさうか、神も仏も一株だといふのか。中々お前は能う分つたことを言ふ。そんなら今晩は泊まつて下さい。併しここでは仕方がないから庫裏の方へ来て下さい。何だか地蔵堂が気に掛つて寝られぬので、一寸見に来たのだ。お茶なつと進ぜよう』

と打つて変つた砕け方に喜楽はヤツと胸をなで卸し、大坊主に導かれて、庫裏の方へついて行つた。

此寺は此坊主一人より外に誰も人らしい者は住んで居なかつた。ソロソロと身の上話を互に始めて見ると、不思議にも此坊主は喜楽の伯母の嫁入つて居た、南桑田郡千代川村字今津の人見弥吉といふ伯父の兄の子であつた。子供の時には四五回も遊んだことのある人見与三郎といふ男で、放蕩の結果親の財産を残らずなくして了ひ、それから園部監獄の看守となり、巡査も勤め、これも又酒の為に免職さされ、それから易者を習ひ、真言秘密の法を覚え、無住の寺を幸ひ、留守坊主に雇はれてゐた事が分つた。此奇遇に両人は打とけて、いろいろの事を語り合ひ、ここに四五日逗留して、人見に鎮魂帰神の霊術を教へてやり、後日の再会を約して海老坂峠を北へ渡り、安懸といふ田舎の村迄辿り着いた。

さうすると俄に警鐘が響く、太鼓が鳴つて来る。村人は『野添に火事がある！』と云つて、一生懸命に鳶口や竜頭水、水桶などを持つて駆出す。喜楽も村人の後から走つて野添といふ小村まで従いて往つた。併し乍ら何処にも煙は立つてゐず、火事らしきものも無かつた。されど野添の寺の鐘や太鼓が頻りに鳴つてゐる。向うから走つて来た人の話によると、深い井戸を掘つてゐた所、俄にウラが来て、井戸掘人足が埋まつて了つたから、掘り出す為に、近在の人を集める為の警鐘太鼓であつた事が分つた。

§ 2

When I went to see, about one hundred people had gathered near the edge of the well, beginning to dig from a spot four or five ken away from the well using pickaxes and hoes. If they dilly-dallied, there was a danger that breathing would cease due to the buried earth, sand, and water. Kiraku suddenly closed his eyes and sat quietly in meditation upon the earth to receive a divine oracle.

Then from within his belly, a voice came forth saying,

'It is the pine grove,'

and a divine oracle was delivered saying,

'Scatter five or six shō of rapeseed oil into the well, or if there is no oil, scatter about one to of vinegar.'

Therefore, when he reported that reason to the villagers, they immediately brought two five-shō barrels from the vinegar shop and tossed them slopingly into the well. Then a large crowd gathered and spent about two hours pulling up the buried laborer. Fortunately, his breath had not stopped. That man was a man named Kuchinaka. According to that man's story,

Kuchinaka: 'When I was suddenly struck and buried in the earth and sand, fortunately I escaped and sheltered my body beneath a large stone protruding from the side. From the waist down I was soaked in water, and although my entire body was packed with earth and sand, thanks to the protruding stone I was able to move only my neck freely. Gradually my breathing became painful, and just as I thought my life was about to end, the scent of vinegar suddenly came, and my breathing became easy. At that time, from nowhere, a pine grove... now I will save your life... a voice could be heard.'

thus he narrated while weeping with joy. The mass of people turned toward Kiraku, feeling extremely grateful, and asking him to stay for two or three days, so he stayed at Kuchinaka's house and talked about spiritual science. However, the majority of this village were believers of the Funai Mōrei Association. And because the head of that association was a religious teacher named Seiroku Sano, who happened to be Kiraku's uncle, due to religious connections, he did not listen to the villagers' attempts to stop him and returned to the Sonobe meeting place.

From then on, many people in this vicinity, on their way to worship at the Funai Mōrei Association, also visited and returned via the Kuroda meeting place. Furthermore, Mr. Sadazō Kobayashi also brought along numerous believers and visited the Kuroda meeting place time and time again.

By the way, Yosaburō Hitomi, who had been the caretaker monk of the Jizō-dō in Ebisaka, held some sort of Buddhist name, but I have forgotten it. Around the sixth year of Taishō, he came to the Ōmoto and helped with sweeping the gate and various other tasks, but once again, upon the villagers' request, he returned to his original Jizō-dō.

Hearing that an Inari-san possessed a fifty-odd-year-old granny named Min Morita in Kisinoshamura Kizaki, Funai County, and that numerous believers were coming to worship, at the end of May in the thirty-second year of Meiji, Kiraku did not wear a haori and hakama, but dressed like an ordinary peasant, thinking he would go and investigate what kind of divine possession it was, and went mixed among the believers.

About one chō above the tutelary Grand Shrine, there was a small thatched-roof house, and beside it stood a new red-ochre-dyed house measuring about six tatami mats. In front of it, a small shrine painted red was built and erected. And about four pottery foxes were enshrined, with small bells hung, and red, white, and yellow bell cords hanging down about one shaku five sun, and to them were attached names of how many years old a man or woman was, hanging down in lines of five or six. With tens of old and young male and female worshippers at his back, that granny squatted in front of the shrine, and as she shook the bells clatteringly,

Granny: 'Ah is that so, heh, is that so, ho-ho-ho-ho-ho'

just as she seemed to be talking to herself, again she said,

'Just now, a male of such-and-such age, so-and-so from what village, is troubled with illness, but how can this be cured?'

and clapping her hands said,

'Ah is that so, I understand'

and speaking again, making the next inquiry,

'Ah is that so, if so, you should decoct burdock and make him drink it, eh? Mix it with black beans and willow leaves, you say? Yes, I will tell him so.'

船井郡紀井の庄村木崎の、森田民といふ五十余りの婆アサンに稲荷サンがのり憑り、沢山な信者が参拝するのを聞き、明治三十二年の五月の末、喜楽は羽織袴をつけず、普通の百姓のやうな風をして、一ペンどんな神憑だか調べて見ようと思ひ、信者に紛れて行つて見た。

So she says. When the inquiries were once over, appearing to be a granny with a good memory, she was giving prescriptions and medical directions one by one based on the state of the illnesses. The strange thing was that, according to this granny's instructions, most illnesses were cured. Then, one funny thing was that a certain woman was inquiring with a worried face.

The circumstances were:

Woman: 'No matter how many times we take a bride, she always returns home immediately, and both parents are worried. Is this failure of brides to thrive some sort of ancestral curse, or is perhaps the house layout bad? Please inquire into this once for me.'

She says. After all the numerous believers had returned without leaving a single person, there were only three people left: the granny named Omin, a woman of about fifty who had just been inquiring, and Kiraku.

Omin promptly set up an inquiry in front of the usual shrine,

Omin: 'Yoshitsunedaimiyōjin-sama, Katsuradaimiyōjin-sama, Tamafusadaimiyōjin-sama, Tamayoshidaimiyōjin-sama!'

Repeatedly calling upon them, she was inquiring about the previous woman's wish. And from time to time she laughed as if collapsing, saying, 'Ho ho ho ho ho.' After a while, Granny Omin came over here and said,

Omin: 'Your son has taken five brides with this, hasn't he? The reason they all returned is because his essential utensil has become a straw raincoat, and that is why they all go back home; so this fellow is a bit difficult. Even if you pluck it out with tweezers, it grows back again, you see.'

Woman: 'Can't you somehow have it cured through the grace of Inari-san?'

Peering anxiously into Omin's face, she asks this. Granny Omin tilted her head,

Omin: 'Well, try having faith. Once your faith truly reaches them, since it is a matter of the gods, they will surely do something about it. Now, please go home already. From now on, I have to perform a certain spiritual practice. The gods are in a great hurry...'

Saying this, she successfully sent her away. And Granny Omin turned to Kiraku and said,

Omin: 'Sensei, you are a person from Kuroda, aren't you? Disguised as a believer, you came to investigate me, didn't you? The gods are saying that you should ask Sensei to make the arrangements so that I may receive the rank of Kyōdōshoku. Please take care of this for me.'

Kiraku: 'You are a person who understands things quite well, unlike ordinary ladies. And you were talking to the ceramic Inari-san in various ways, but do such ceramic Inari-san speak words?'

When he asked this, Granny Omin, without a moment's hesitation,

Omin: 'Heh heh, they certainly do speak! Just now, I inquired about the matter of that woman's son who just went home, and it was wondrous. When I sat still and gazed intently at the ceramic Inari-san, suddenly a ○○ appeared from between the Inari-san's legs, and it was completely covered with hair all the way to the tip, so I asked the Inari-san, "...Is this man a straw raincoat, and is that why the brides will not stay?" When I asked, he said, "Oh, that is so." Oho ho ho!'

She laughs. And

Omin: 'Sensei, you too should ask in front of that Inari-san for once.'

As she encouraged him, he crouched down in front of the Inari and tried having various questions and answers, but not a single word was answered, no matter what. Then, when he asked about the history of how Granny Omin became a spiritual medium, she narrated an interesting background as follows.

Omin: 'As my husband Takichi and I, the two of us, were making sweet potatoes and mulberry trees, every single year someone digs up and eats the sweet potatoes. They pull out the dropped straw of the privy and even eat the feces which is essential for manure, so it is without a doubt that some filthy bad fox dwelling in the mountain behind is eating the potatoes and feces, and deciding to smoke them out and catch every last one of them, Takichi, myself, and our son Tomekichi, the three of us, smoked out one hole with pine needles and dug from the other side, whereupon a pair of foxes had given birth to two kits. Infuriated that these were wretched feces-eating foxes, the three of us in the parent-child trio raised our Bicchu hoes all at once and beat all four of those foxes to death, sold their skins to Shinpei-san of Shimokizaki, and ate the parts where the meat was sweet. Then, starting from the third day, my entire body turned water-swollen, and suffering so intensely I could bear it no longer, whenever I opened the rice chest a fox's face was inside, and even if I went to the privy a fox was there. In the end, everywhere you looked without exception it became a fox's face, glaring at me with a frightening expression, so when I said to the foxes... Look here, you wretches, despite the fact that you dug up and ate the potatoes I cultivated with such care and even ate the feces I intended to use as fertilizer, what on earth are you resentful about to do this to me? The four foxes, parent and offspring, came out to that very place and said in reply, 'Both eating the potatoes and eating the feces were entirely the doing of a dog named Maru of Kizaki. Moreover, since taking the lives of us parents and children, selling our skins, and even eating our meat is going too far, we will not rest until we take the lives of you three parents and children and also the life of your younger brother Higejo,' and glaring at me with terrifying faces, they intimidated me. Thereupon, when I said to the foxes... Well now, I am truly sorry. Please resign yourselves that now things have turned out this way, there is nothing that can be done and it is our karmic bond. Even if you were to take our lives, it would not mean your lives are saved, so how about this, here is a proposal: from now on I shall enshrine you as a divine spirit, so please forgive us and grant your pardon—when I pleaded with them, they were surprisingly straightforward foxes and replied... That is true, just as you say, now that things have reached this point there is no use taking your lives, so if you are going to enshrine me I will forgive you, but in exchange I will help people, cure illnesses, and let you know about various matters, so you must become my vessel and from this day forward you must never do any ordinary work again. In return for that favor, I will give you seven rin of money every day...' And they told me to name the four foxes Yoshitsune Daimyōjin, Katsura Daimyōjin, Tamafusa Daimyōjin, and Tamafō Daimyōjin and enshrine them, which is why I have built this red shrine and am enshrining them. When I do this, many people come to visit, some offering one mon, some wrapping up five rin, and among them there are even those who put in as much as twenty mon and offer it, but strangely enough, if you leave it to accumulate for a month it invariably amounts to two yen and ten sen, coming out to the rate of seven sen a day. Since with just seven sen I can buy about seven shō of rice, I have no difficulties whatsoever just feeding myself.'

She was talking with utmost seriousness. Kiraku,

Kiraku: 'My, what a strange fox-san. If you understand everything that well, won't you please divine one thing for me as well?'

When he broached this, Omin-san retorted instantly,

Omin: 'Right now, many people from Sonobe have gathered, built a church inside the park, and are making a fuss saying they want to make you their teacher. And you seem to be thinking about whether you should become their teacher as well, but the place where you are destined to settle down is clearly decided about seven ri to the northwest from here. In about a month someone will come to welcome you. Your bride is also clearly decided, and her name is Osumi-san.'

She spoke of it as if it were nothing at all. When Kiraku had gone to Ayabe the previous autumn, he had heard that the founder had a daughter named Oryū, but he did not know there was a daughter named Osumi. Therefore, he even wondered if it might perhaps be about Shikisumi. However, Ayabe was nearly ten ri away. Saying it was seven ri was strange. He even thought that perhaps his destination was decided somewhere in the Wachi direction. Then, parting from Omin and returning to the Kuroda assembly hall, a sealed letter had arrived from Shikazō in Ayabe. When he opened and read it,

'As soon as rice planting is finished, by the command of the founder Deguchi-sama, I will come for consultation, so please wait without going anywhere...'—

such a meaning was written in it. ...I wonder if the folks from Ayabe are coming to call me again; if it is like last year, it would be better not to go...—thinking nothing of it, treating Omin's divine divination as half true and half false, he had completely forgotten about the letter and such things.

Just as he was putting bottles in place for fishing in the Sonobe River, the Shikazō family came to visit, so it was finally decided that he would go to Ayabe, and this time, settling down, he would open the Kinmei Association alongside the founder and perform divine service.
