Chapter 1: The Sanzu River [551]

第1章　三途川〔551〕

§ 1

Floating upon the vast ocean, renowned as an isle of treasures, lieth a solitary island in the Dragon Palace Sea with gems as manifold as Oceania. The six missionaries who arrived bearing the divine decree of Ural-hiko were steadfast in faith, guarded by mighty spirits who formerly served the Dragon Palace: Tayorihiko manifesting divine power, and Iiyorihiko with polished soul appearing as high priests of God. Subduing all through the Kototama of pure and beautiful words, they reduced the three years' toil of the Ural missionaries to empty foam, leaving the port of Albany without a single spoil, crossing over myriads of floating islets along the far sea-lanes from the Crane Land, and setting forth from the Crane Port,

They sailed back unto the sea of Fusa. Even then, tossed by the fierce northeast gales upon the waters of Fusa, they encountered Hinodewake, the supreme apostle of the Anai teachings, and landed at the port of Taru. Trudging wearily along the sluggish river toward the sacred Shizu Forest, illuminated by the divine radiance, they turned their hearts, and guided by the Anai priests, traversed the moor of Furu, explored the Monstrous Cavern, crossed the pass of Koshi, and slumbered at the foot of Saruyama Pass. When Otohiko awoke and gazed around, the moon was shining brightly through the boughs.

Hereupon the five missionaries having departed on their far journey, Otohiko accompanied Yajihiko and Yotahiko. Chased by the swarming foes, crossing moors and rivers, leaping over crooked barrier-gates, stripped stark naked in the muddy bogs, they sped with nimble feet toward the Forty-Eight Slopes of Mount Kojika. At that very hour, attacked from fore and aft by overwhelming legions, unable to withstand the multitude, Otohiko, together with Yajihiko and Yotahiko, leaped into the abyss of a thousand fathoms, vanishing amid the churning rapids like driftwood in the torrent, while the fierce mountain gale roared mournfully through the gorge.

Yajihiko: 'The world is wondrously ordered! With sheer precipices towering on one side and an abyss of a thousand fathoms yawning on the other, hemmed in fore and aft by countless foes, hopelessly outnumbered, when we hurled ourselves headlong into the torrent, what agony of terror we felt! Yet crying "Come what may, our lives belong to God, our fate is in His hands!", when we plunged down, lo, there lay a deep and tranquil pool! Stark naked as we were, 'twas the perfect element for swimming! Sir Missionary, burdened with voluminous and fluttering robes, had a dreadful struggle in the waters. 'Twas our nakedness alone that saved us from drowning! In the hour of dire peril, to possess naught save the bare skin of one's birth is a blessing beyond measure! That is why the Kami bid us travel light! "A naked man hath nothing to lose!" Nothing is more splendid than nakedness! Is it not so, Yota?'

'Yea, truly! When we tumbled into the mire and were stripped bare, I lamented: "How can one journey stark naked? What misery! If only I had a loincloth!"—unable to part with worldly attachment. Yet in such an extremity, to be naked as a newborn babe is the greatest blessing! 'Tis all by the grace of God! Had all three of us worn heavy garments, not one would have survived, but we had all departed straight for the Netherworld!'

Otohiko said:

''Tis true, the Way of God requireth no baggage! Yet unless we plait tree leaves to fashion some garments, we cannot proceed along our journey. To be fined by the constables for public indecency were shameful indeed! Nor can we steal, so we are in a sore dilemma. How if we weave garments of wayside grasses, friend Yaji?'

'Nay, since we were stripped naked, this too is by divine providence! I have no desire to reverse it! What care I? As the proverb saith, "The skin of the cheek is hardened by custom"—no matter how bitter the cold, who ever draped a cloak over his cheeks? Once accustomed, stark nakedness feeleth neither cold nor heat! A bare man walketh free; a naked pilgrimage is merry sport, Lord Otohiko!'

'Yet 'tis monstrous unsightly! How if I divide my robes among us—one wearing the haori coat, one the hakama trousers, and one the inner kimono?'

'Aha, that were uglier still—like a parade of goblins! Never mind, let us march as we are! What sayest thou, Yota?'

'The sun hath set, and darkness falleth thick around us. Walking in pitch dark, naked or clad is all one—neither handsome nor shameful! Let us swing our arms and stride forward as far as our legs will bear us!'

So the three trudged onward through the gloom, following wherever their feet led them. Presently Otohiko cried:

'Lo, light breaketh suddenly around us! Where under heaven are we? A majestic river floweth north and south, and upon the far bank wondrous golden palaces and jeweled pavilions appear floating amid the clouds! My heart leapeth with eager joy; I yearn to hasten thither without a moment's delay!'

Yajihiko and Yotahiko exclaimed:

'Indeed, indeed! Wondrous palaces greet our eyes; looking upon them, we feel as though we were nearing the Capital of Victory! How blessed, how wondrous! Deeming we were plunging into the valley of death with scarce one chance in ten thousand of life, lo, we have arrived near such a glorious realm! Truly 'tis said, "Unless one braveth danger, one tasteth not the ripe persimmon!" Yet what hath become of Taka, Ume, Iwa, and Koma? Are they still flogging their lean nags up the steep slopes of Saruyama Pass? One never knoweth whether the vanguard or the rearguard shall win the prize; nothing bringeth such peace as surrendering all unto the divine will of God!'

Conversing thus, the three arrived at last upon the bank of a broad, fathomless river of azure clarity. Yajihiko gazed in wonder:

'Behold, what a magnificent river! Most rivers are commonly vast beds of dry stones, with a mere ribbon of blue water trickling along the edge; yet this river is a marvel—full to the brim from bank to bank, and flowing with pure, crystal-clear water! Commonly, when a river floweth bank-full, 'tis in a deluge of red, churning mud; but this stream is wondrous beyond compare!'

Yotahiko said:

''Tis a magnificent river, yet praise it as thou wilt, there is neither bridge nor ferryboat! How are we to cross? We must devise some stratagem!'

'Why, we are fortunately stark naked! Skilled in swimming as we are, we need but plunge in and swim to the far shore! In such an hour, to be bare without a rag is marvelous convenient! The only one in trouble is Sir Missionary Otohiko! Sir Missionary, why not cast all thy robes into the stream and join us in nakedness? We will keep thee company!'

'That soundeth easy enough, yet thou art but a layman, whereas we bear the heavy burden of missionaries of the Anai teachings! I must preserve this hire-cloth with reverence, and I cannot cast aside my sacred vestments! Alas, the bearer of authority hath a grievous lot—constrained and inconvenient in the extreme!'

'Thou art bound by attachment to thy vestments and hire-cloth, and that is thy undoing! Clad in protective camouflage, thou canst not move with absolute freedom! If thou art naked, thou canst act as a naked man! Like a chameleon that turneth green amid green grass, red upon red leaves, and white upon a white bough—is not such free, unbounded versatility the very method a missionary ought to employ? Great Toshitoku-no-Kami shed his missionary vestments to become a rustic peasant, donning farmer's garb to assist in the fields, and thereby fulfilled his sacred mission with glory! To cross this river, Yajihiko deemeth that nakedness is the only way!'

Otohiko: ''Tis well said, yet at least until I worship at Mount Caucasus, Otohiko would fain keep these robes!'
