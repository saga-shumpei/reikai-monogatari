Chapter 5: 親子奇遇 [717]

親子奇遇〔717〕

§ 1

The Sendenshi of Ananai-kyō, Akihiko and Komahiko, both, by the divine command of Kotoyoriwake, seeking to test the spiritual power (shinriyoku) of the Sendenshi of Ame no Maura, disguised their forms at the foot of the mountain of Hito no O-tōge under a snowy sky.

Here the three men, having briefly rested at the office (nagatoko) of the Takeshi Shrine in Uzuyama and chanced to meet the Kamuzukasa Matsutakahiko, Akihiko and Komahiko cast Ame no Maura down from above the bank where deep snow was falling, and advanced pointing toward the east.

By the grace of Kami, traversing the Afumiji road while bathing in the Hieizanoroshi wind, riding over Ōtsu and Fushimi, they prepared small boats (kobune) and the width of the river (kahahaba) reached even to the shore of Hirakata.

Gradually mooring their boat, seeing the village of Naniha to the right, Sakai, Kishiwada, Sano, Fuke, crossing the Ki-no-kawa river, passing Wakayama before they knew it, and Hidakagawa, they arrived at last at the riverside.

The sun had grown dark by degrees. Amidst ten days of rain, muddy waters swelled in the river, and there was no means to send a ferryboat. The two men, having no other choice, turned back, and hearing that there was a waterfall deep in the mountains of Hidakayama, resolved to perform austere practices for a while until the river waters subsided, and using the moonlight as their strength, they advanced deep into the mountain recesses.

Near the waterfall there was a small shrine, where a dragon deity (ryūjin) was enshrined. Around this shrine, wondrously, splendid persimmon fruits hung heavy with branches. Neither men nor crows took them. They were praised as the persimmons most deeply cherished by the dragon deity.

In the middle of the night, tracing a path sharpened by human footsteps, the two men finally arrived beside the waterfall. Quickly stripping off their clothing, purifying their bodies in the waterfall, finishing the chanting of norito, they sat formally straight in front of the shrine and assumed the posture of chinkon (soul calming). The persimmon branches, bending to the point of breaking, were stirred by a sudden strong wind and caressed the bodies of the two men. Driven by the delicious scent, the two men finished their chinkon, plucked the fruit here and there, and ate their fill. Suddenly the shrine building began to rumble.

The sound grew mighty moment by moment, and the earth began to reverberate.

Akihiko: 'Hey, it looks like an earthquake after all.'

Komahiko: 'Nonsense, it is not an earthquake. It is rumbling and making the earth shake because the noise is so violent. Moreover, no sooner did we pick and eat these persimmons than this shrine building began to rumble, did it not? Is the Kami perhaps reluctant to part with them?'

Akihiko: 'Nonsense, with this many persimmons, even if we ate five or ten, we ourselves would not begrudge them, so how much less reason is there for Kami to be angered by human beings eating them with joy. They are made for humans to eat. There is no such thing.'

The shrine building rumbled ever more violently, and with an indescribably unpleasant voice, they felt as though they were being shouted at, so that unconsciously the two men lost their courage, and chanting 'Kannagara Tamashii Sakihaimase', they fled along the path they had originally come, tumbling and stumbling.

The night finally dawned. By the clear water of the valley stream, there was an aged crone with white hair and a strange appearance washing clothes.

Akihiko: 'Komahiko-sama, look over there. She has come out.'

Komahiko: 'Hey indeed, is there not a monstrous creature there? She seems to be washing something. Even though there are no dwellings in this mountain depth, that such an aged hag is washing clothes—this defies understanding. This fellow might be some kind of goblin. We must be on our guard.'

Akihiko: 'Since she is located at a place where the road is a single path wedged between valleys, we cannot pass without going past her somehow. Shall I pluck up my courage and go?'

Komahiko: 'It is a journey we must make in any case, but well, let us think about it for a moment before we go. Shall we go with strength, or go with weakness? Shall we not decide upon one course from that?'

Akihiko: 'At any rate, adapting to circumstances, let us make it suit the occasion.'

Thus feeling an eerie dread, their steps lacking swiftness, they advanced step by step with reluctance. When they looked, from the white hair on the hag's head, two tortoise-shell-like horns curved forward neatly without height differences, growing just like an upside-down Japanese numeral eight.

Akihiko: 'Hey Komahiko, if we go with weakness to this fellow, we will be taken advantage of. If we go with strength, she might get angry and snap at us. At any rate, let us pass by flattering the old hag's chin in a comical way. In regard to that, Akihiko and Komahiko will not do. Let us reduce our names back to the original Umago and Kakō and try negotiating.'

Komahiko: 'That would be best.'

So saying in a low voice, he drew close to the old woman. The old woman appeared to be deaf, and as if unaware of the footsteps of the two men, she was washing a blood-stained garment with all her might.

Akihiko: 'I say, Granny... Hey, Granny.'

He shouted loudly, putting all his strength into that final call. The old woman paid no heed and continued washing with all her might.

Akihiko: 'Ah, this fellow is deaf. Yet what a thoroughly disagreeable old woman. We cannot cross over to the other side without passing that spot, which is a troublesome matter indeed. Shall we turn back for a while and wait until the old woman finishes her washing and goes home?'

Komahiko: 'No, we cannot turn back even a single step now. After being shaken like that by those rumblings and shouted at in such a nasty voice, I simply cannot endure it.'

Akihiko: 'If you say that, we cannot advance either, and we are brought to a complete standstill, aren't we?'

Komahiko: 'That is precisely where we are sendenshi. We shall force our way through by the power of Kamisama. It matters little even if we are devoured by an onibaba.'

Akihiko: 'As if I could bear being eaten by such a creature! I do not mind sacrificing my life for the sake of o-michi, but if I become the prey of an onibaba, then the sendenshi are finished as well. Komahiko: 'Oh, exactly so. It is merely a single old woman, while we are two robust men. However, a sneak attack is not honorable. Let us ask her permission to pass. If by any chance she refuses to let us pass, then and there we shall fight to the death.'

So saying, they drew close to the side where the old woman was blocking the narrow mountain stream, and pushing her stooping waist as if half in fear,

Komahiko: 'Hey there, Granny, please let us pass through here.'

Waiting for the dead of night, they bound this old woman and old man to pillars, subjected the single daughter to foul abuse, stole away all the savings of the old folks, and in the end they went away after torturing the daughter to death. Akihiko: 'This is truly an outrageous thing to say. But since you have horns growing on your head, you must be the onibaba of Mount Hidaka who preys upon human beings.'

Old Woman: 'That is settled. It is because I am an onibaba that horns grow. Now, bow down before this place. This old woman will prepare a rough dish and avenge her daughter.'

Komahiko: 'Hold on, Granny, what nonsense are you spewing! I am a sendenshi named Umako of Izumo Shinpo. Don't talk about thieves... don't be a fool. And if she was your daughter, she must have been an oni daughter as well. No doubt you lost your own child as retribution for habitually eating people. That creature called Kishibojin, despite having a thousand children, snatched other people's children and devoured them. But once, when one of her children was hidden away by Kamisama, she wept incessantly from morning till night, and then... she said, "Even among a thousand children, if I lose just a single one it causes me this much grief; how much more agonizing must it be when a human being, who has three or five, or at most ten children, has even one taken away!" So repenting, she became a noble Buddha, or so the story goes. If you too understand how painful it is to have your child taken, then from now on, whether they be human children or parents, you must never devour them again.'

Baba: 'Ho ho ho ho, do you call me an ogre hag, and are you a sendenshi of Ananai-kyō? If you are a sendenshi, you should be able to tell the difference between an ogre hag and an ordinary old woman.'

Komahiko: 'Even so, isn't a creature with horns growing on her head an ogre? Though I look like this, I was originally called Umasan, born in Kii Province, went up to the capital because of circumstances, was employed in a splendid residence, gradually advanced in life, and now I am an unquestioned sendenshi-sama. How could I make such a misjudgment? Even if you say such vague things and try to deceive me, it's no use.'

Baba: 'What! You were born in Kii Province... and went to service in the capital... That is a strange thing to hear. And isn't your name Uma?'

Komahiko: 'That's right, they called me Uma. What of it?'

Baba: 'Since I have a hunch about something right here, wouldn't you come to the old woman's house?'

Akihiko: 'Hey, hey, Komahiko, pull yourself together. You'll fall into a trap.'

Baba: 'Do not doubt me. There are only two of us living together, an old man and an old woman. One older brother was carried off by a tengu when he was young, taken somewhere, and has not returned to this day. One younger sister had her life taken by a thief two or three days ago, and the two old folk are spending dreary days. It is a small house, but I will not say I want to eat much or drink much. I have something I want to ask, so please come.'

Komahiko folds his arms, tilts his head, and gazes intently at the old woman's face.

Komahiko: 'Hey, old woman, since when did those horns grow?'

Baba: 'Oho ho ho, so many thieves keep appearing, so for the sake of caution I stick deer horns on my head to make myself look like an ogre. Just like this...'

And she casually plucks off both horns and shows them.

Akihiko: 'Aha ha ha, yay, with this I'm a bit relieved. Then it seems she wasn't an ogre hag after all. Now, now, dear old woman, where is your home?'

Baba: 'If you go around that big rock sticking out abruptly over there, there is a house like a charcoal burner's hut. That is my dwelling. This village has only seven or eight houses, but even if you say you are going to the neighborhood, you have to go about a ri, so it is inconvenient. Come, please do come to the old woman's house.'

Komahiko: 'Whatever may be, Mother, let us follow you.'

The old woman goes on ahead with a smile, returning. Komahiko follows, repeatedly tilting his head to the left and right as if something is dawning on him.

From behind, Akihiko watches the two figures with suspicion, falling two or three spaces behind, advancing with reluctance.

When they round the foot of the rock protruding abruptly at the nose of the mountain, a fairly large thatched house stands seven or eight spaces ahead. The old woman turns to Komahiko,

Baba: 'That is my house. Please stay and rest leisurely tonight.'

Akihiko mutters, 'What is this, it makes no sense,' driven by a sense of anxiety, and stands waiting in the narrow path with folded arms.

The old woman flings open the half-broken door with a rattle,

Baba: 'Come, come, young men, please enter.'

Komahiko: 'Yes, thank you.'

Looking back behind him, Akihiko is standing four or five spaces away with folded arms, looking pensive.

Komahiko: 'Hey, Akihiko, won't you come quickly? What are you doing?'

Akihiko: 'I am guarding from the outside, so you be careful and go inside. It would be terrible if there were a falling ceiling and you were smashed to pieces, so go in with your wits about you. If I think it's an emergency, I'll leap right in and slay the enemy... Well, as a reserve, I'll wait outside.'

Komahiko: 'If so, I kindly rely on you.'

He stepped over the threshold and concealed his figure within the interior. The old man seemed to have poor eyesight, and as he trembled, he appeared from the inner room.

婆『疑ひなさるな。爺イと婆アと二人暮しだ。一人の兄は幼い時に天狗にさらはれて、何処かへ連れて行かれたきり今に帰つて来ず、一人の妹娘は泥棒に二三日前生命を奪られ、爺婆二人が面白からぬ月日を送つて居るのだ。小さい宅だけれど、滅多に食はうとも、呑まうとも云はぬ。尋ねたい事が有るから来て下され』

駒彦は双手を組み首を傾け、婆アの顔を熟々と眺めて居る。

駒彦『オイ婆ア、其角は何時から生えたのかい』

婆『オホヽヽヽ、あまり泥棒が出て来よるので、用心の為に鹿の角を頭にひつ付けて鬼に見せて居るのだ。それ此通り……』

と無雑作に二本の角を引むしつて見せる。

秋彦『アハヽヽヽ、ヤア是れで一寸は安心だ。さうするとヤツパリ鬼婆ではなかつたらしいな。コレコレ婆アさま、お前のお宅は何処だ』

婆『そこへニユツと突き出て居る大きな岩を、クルツと廻ると、炭焼小屋の様な家がある。そこが妾の住家だ。此村は七八軒の所だが、近所へ行くと云つても一里位行かなならぬのだから不便なものだ。サアどうぞ婆の宅まで来て下さい』

駒彦『何は兎もあれ、お婆アさま、従いて参りませう』

婆はニコニコし乍ら先に立ち帰つて行く。駒彦は何か心に当るものの如く首を頻りに左右にかたげ乍ら従いて行く。あとより秋彦は不審相に二人の姿を看守りつつ、二三間遅れて、厭相に進んで行く。山の鼻にヌツと突出た岩の麓を廻はると、七八間向うにかなり大きな草葺の家が建つて居る。婆アさまは駒彦に向ひ、

婆『あれが妾の家だ。どうぞ今晩はゆつくり泊つて往て下されや』

秋彦は『なんだ、合点がゆかぬ事だなア』と呟きつつ、不安の念に駆られ、手を組んで細路に佇立して居る。婆アさまは半破れた戸をガラリと開き、

婆『サアサアお若い衆、這入つて下さい』

駒彦『ハイ有難う』

と後を振り返り見れば、四五間あとに秋彦は手を組み思案らしく佇んで居る。

駒彦『オイ秋彦、早う来ぬか。何して居るのだ』

秋彦『俺は外から警固して居るから、貴様用心して中に這入れ。釣天井でも有つてバサンバサンとやられちや大変だから、よく気を付けて這入れ。俺はサア事だと思つたら、直に飛び込んで讎敵を討つてやるから……マア予備として、俺は外に待つて居る』

駒彦『そんなら宜しう頼む』

Jī: 'Ah, Ba, you have returned safely. I was truly lonely and troubled. Since your return was so late, I was beside myself with worry, fearing that you might have encountered a dorobau once again. However, is there not someone following right behind you? If you go about dragging careless persons back with you, you will only be subjected to such an ordeal as that of the other time. It is without even the slightest shiyōko that you seize people passing on the road, talking about zenkon and offering them lodgings, and that is why such things happen. As for myself, I am already cured of such folly, so I would never bring back some metsuta fellow. This person is said to be a man called Uma, and since our son's name is also Uma, I felt a yearning for him for no particular reason and brought him home. Jī: 'Hearing that, I somehow feel a sense of yearning. Now, now, you who are called Uma-sama, please wipe your feet and come up.'

Komahiko: 'Yes, thank you very much. I too am a hitorimono. Somehow I have come to feel a deep yearning for this grandmother-sama.'

So saying, he wipes his feet and steps up onto the zashiki. Akihiko, doing so with timid hesitation, comes as far as the kadoguchi and observes the yōsu.

Jī: 'You are said to be called Uma-sama, but where on earth were you born?'

Komahiko: 'Yes, I was extremely young and do not remember clearly, but I faintly recall that it was somehow near the banks of the Hidakagaha. Yet at the same time, I cannot tell whether it was a dream or reality. Being carried off by a tengu and left hanging upon the branch of a giant taiboku in Murasakino in the province of Yamashiro, the chieftain of that place recognized me, rescued me, and from then on I grew up as a child of that household.'

Jī: 'I am one called Tsunetsuna, and as for the old woman, she is called Oku, but do you remember the names of your parents?'

Komahiko: 'Being a mere child at the time, I do not know, but according to the words of my master, upon my protective bag there was a mark reading either Tsune or Ku, and my name was written as Umakusuna, while my master would call out "Umaku, Umaku," or so I have heard.'

Jī: 'What, Tsune and Ku, and written as Umakusuna? If so, then you are my son. How truly wonderful that you have remained safe and well.'

With these words, both of them cling to each other and break down in weeping.

Komahiko: 'Ah, hearing it thus, I somehow feel as though you are indeed my revered parents, but there is one distinctive physical feature upon my body. Do you know of it?'

Jī: 'What you call a distinctive feature—from the time you were small, your testicles were remarkably larger than those of other people. It is said to be an ailment called a testicular hernia, and we, your parents, were exceedingly worried about it. How are your testicles now?'

Komahiko: 'Yes, as you say, they are twice as large as normal. When I was dragged away at the Matsuhime Mansion on the pretext of being a wealthy man, I was truly troubled. Since our accounts match up to that extent, there can surely be no mistake that you are indeed my revered parents. Ah, how wonderful that you have remained safe and well.'

At this, Komahiko too sheds soft tears. Oku speaks:

Oku: 'If only you had returned two or three days earlier, would not your younger sister Okaru have escaped meeting such a fate... Ah, what a deplorable thing has been done. We searched everywhere for your whereabouts; while we were still young, the two of us walked hand in hand across the entire expanse of Kii Province, yet we could by no means learn where you were. Now that we have grown this old, we are no longer able to walk about, so whenever we saw anyone, we would have them stay at our house, hoping against hope for some clue, and thus we kept a zenkon'yado. And yet, we ended up sheltering a terrible dorobau and having the life of my younger sister taken from us. Ah, how pitiful... If only my younger sister were alive, she would have been able to meet her beloved older brother, and how overjoyed she would have been. Ah, ah, ah.'

The old woman sinks into weeping. Both old man Tsunetsuna and Komahiko are likewise overcome with tears, sniffling as they weep. Hearing this, Akihiko comes running in:

Akihiko: 'Oh, Komahiko, congratulations! You have always said how much you wished to meet your parents, and now you have achieved this reunion with them in a place you least expected. This is entirely the blessing of the Great Gods. Not only you, but I too am filled with joy. Ah, thank you, O Gods.'

His voice turning tearful, he brings his palms together and, sobbing in broken fragments, offers up a prayer of thanksgiving. Upon the roof, a flock of seven or eight Kumano crows alight upon the ridgepole and, with hoarse voices, sorrowfully cry out, 'Kawai, kawaii.' From the ceiling, the squeaking of mice, 'Chiu, chiu, chiu, filial piety, filial piety,' comes clearly to the ear.
