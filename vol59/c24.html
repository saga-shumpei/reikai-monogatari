Chapter 24: 隆 Light [1524]

隆光〔1524〕

§ 1

Akisu's song: 'Kinchō and Ginchō spread their wings, gliding over the waves in every direction, looking so joyful. In the Amatsumisora, the summer Taiyō shines brilliantly. The sake they drank goes to their heads, and the ship sways unsteadily, yet they are in an indescribable state of Jiyōkigen. It has become Omoshiro and Oka. They have loaded twenty ships with beasts in human skin and humans in beast skin, crying, laughing, and trembling, a Senshibantai upon the waves. The blowing wind comes, making my sleeves flutter with a sense of unease. The followers of the Brahman Kyō, in a state of Hananhanki, are coiled up in the hold of the Itahikomaru, whispering secretly and enjoying the light of Zento, yet their whispers are filled with doubt. Even if Ametsuchi were to overturn, even if the lake of purification were to dry up, even if Kairiuō were to appear and swallow the ships and all, what is there to fear in the way of Kami? The light of the sun in the Amatsumisora shines into every corner of the Namima, and with every strike, it sparkles and shines like Giyorin. Steering through such a joyous and peaceful great lake, carrying the joy of over three hundred, we return to the sacred land.'

Akisu and Kāru, the two of them, were the managers of the Bāchiru mansion. While searching for the whereabouts of their master, they spent their days crying, living in sadness and loneliness. Yet, there were times when, through the grace of the Kami of Ametsuchi, their master smiled, helped by the messengers of Kami. With the joy of his return, alongside Anchī-sama, as the years passed peacefully, we two, at the edge of the shore, holding hands and dancing the swallow dance, stood at the head of the noble party, including our master and the propaganda envoys, and returned to the Bāchiru mansion with a triumphant march.

The joy of that moment felt as if Ametsuchi were opening all at once. We opened the storehouses before the twenty houses, carried out the sake barrels we had stored, and held a great banquet for seven days and seven nights. In the midst of that, the princess of the saber gave a divine oracle, commanding us to welcome back the three hundred and thirty-three Kenzoku-sama left on the island of Shōjō. Receiving the command of our master, we followed Itahiko-tsukasa, and together with Anchī, who is accustomed to the sea, we pushed through the waves and advanced.

Such an example is a divine omen, unheard of even in dreams since the ancient times when the earth of Arakane solidified. We loaded the entire Kenzoku of Shōjō-hime onto this ship, leaving not a single one behind, and returned, parting the waves with a resounding roar. Ah, Kannagara-kamukami.

The forests of Mount Azumosu, contrary to the loneliness of the past, will surely echo with the lively cries of monkeys, 'kyatsu-kyatsu,' morning and evening, resounding in all directions. If this ship arrives at the shore, the villagers, drunk on sake, will follow Bāchiru-sama, form a human fence along the shore, and welcome our Shōjō-tai with shouts of joy that will echo to the center of the heavens.

When I think of it, when I think of it, how brave it is. The propaganda envoys, who open the path of sincerity for the sake of Kami and for the sake of the world—it goes without saying for the common people, but they even go to save the groups of Shōjō that appeared on remote islands floating in the waves, islands where no people dwell. That achievement is noble.

Though people boast that they are the children of Kami, the palace of Kami, and possess souls equal to Kami, in reality, they are dens of tigers and wolves. The party of the Shōjō-tai has become the noble Kenzoku of Kami, and each serves in the divine work, sweeping away the hundred calamities of the people of this defiled world. They are the messengers sent to protect the Ametsuchi created by Kami, keeping it peaceful and calm. When I think of it, when I think of it, how grateful I am. I humbly offer three cheers of Banzai for the Shōjō-sama. Banzai, banzai, man-banzai.'

The ancient story of the island of Shōjō, finally recorded by the brush of Kitamura. Kitamura Takamitsu. Using the light of the rising morning sun as a lamp, I tell this story.
