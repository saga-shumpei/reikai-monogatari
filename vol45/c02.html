Chapter 2: Kami Tree [1192]

神木〔1192〕

§ 1

Old Woman Otora pointed toward Matsuhiko at the bank of the Kajikagaha, indicating an old pine tree with fine branches spreading out in all directions like a long meandering serpent and jutting out boldly over the river, and said,

Otora: 'I say, O living shrine of the Great Deity of the King of Heaven of the Last Days, just look at that pine tree. Is it not a magnificent sight?'

Matsuhiko: 'Indeed, considering the scenery of the river, the shape of those branches, and the bluish green luster, it is truly an unexceptionable view. Cranes must nest there quite often.'

Otora: 'Yes, indeed! Far from mere cranes, that pine tree is the number one living pine in all the world where the Great Deity of the Sun, the Great Deity of the Moon, and the eight million Great Deities condescend to rest. That is the divine body of the Great Deity of the King of Heaven of transmutation in the Last Days.'

Matsuhiko: 'If so, then that pine tree must be a transformation of my own spirit, is it not?'

Otora: 'Absurd! Far from a mere transformation, that is your true guardian deity. The workings of time are truly formidable things. Since you, the living deity, have come to visit at last, the teachings of the divination will become the Toyosaka-nobori-hiko-no-Mikoto of the morning sun. What the founder of the Salamander-wake teachings said is not wrong by even a single minute part.'

Mankō: 'Ha ha ha ha, Matsuhiko-sama, you have a rather inconvenient body, do you not? You are always staring into the water mirror by that riverside, getting your head splattered with dung by kites, hawks, and eagles, remaining calm and collected as if you fancied yourself a riverside willow. No wonder I thought your legs felt heavy. Now that we know your true guardian deity is that big old pine tree, we cannot entirely blame Matsuhiko-sama for being so idle. Heh heh heh.'

Otora: 'Now, now, Burarihiko, you are being overly loud-mouthed again. You should not meddle in things.'

Mankō: 'Now look here, grandmother, I am no such old-man-sounding name as Saheiji. I am Mankō-san, fresh and full of life, living through all eternity. You should not underestimate me so much.'

Otora: 'O ho ho ho, what a dull-witted man you are. When I said Saheiji, I meant an interfering busybody. I called you Saheiji because you always thrust yourself forward and do nothing but get in the way. It is not your place to step forward, you yam-digging fellow, seeing you are but a big pine yourself!'

Mankō: 'As for me, I am a bit different from such a big pine. No matter how big a pine it is, a pine's lifespan is a thousand years. I am Mankō-san, who preserves a lifespan of ten thousand years. You should not undervalue me so easily.'

Otora: 'Eh, what a troublesome man you are, who cannot understand anything unless it is explained from A to Z! Calling someone a big pine is a synonym for a big-eating laborer. When you send him out to the fields, he pulls up turnips and eats them, gnaws on daikon radishes, devours carrots, and chews and eats anything raw from sweet potatoes to pumpkins; that is why he is called a big pine.'

Mankō: 'Is it not ridiculous to call someone who eats a lot a big pine? I would like you to explain the origin of that word.'

Otora: 'Eh, what a poor fellow to grasp the point! The piles for the Ran'on River all require long, big fellows, so they are called large piles and long piles. Since those large and long piles cannot be made unless they are big pines, that is why they are called big pines.'

Mankō made a curious gesture and said,

Mankō: 'Ah, is that so, hem! Matsuhiko-sama must also be a big pine then, since he has a karmic connection with pine trees.'

Otora: 'Your pine is a pine that has been turned into a pile. This person's pine is a fresh, living pine with vitality just like that. There is a vast difference between the pine of eternal life through all eternity and the big pine whose trunk has been cut, whose branches have been trimmed, whose head has been shaved year in and year out, made into an inverted tumbler, beaten on the buttocks, and driven into the ground. It would be a terrible trouble if you were to confuse the good and the bad. However, Matsuhiko-sama, because wonderful divine protection is established at the root of that pine tree, I would like you to pay your respects to the deity there for a moment before we go to the Daimon Shrine.'

Matsuhiko: 'Is there a Kamisama enshrined at the root of that pine tree?'

Otora: 'Hai hai, that is the crucial Oshigumiba. If you do not understand that Innen, you will not understand the Innen of Kogitayama. I certainly want you to come.'

Mankō: 'That being the case, are there still other Kamisamas enshrined? It is the Kamisama whom the old man at the Uketsuke said would cause Shiyokutai if shown all at once. Seeing one or seeing two is the same thing. I might as well go and Kuwanran. Hey, Isokō, Aku, Taku, Teku, how about it, do you not feel like doing some Kenbutsu too?'

Ichidō: 'Un, that should be Omoshiro. Let us go and Kenbutsu under Otora-sama's Mosahiki for the sake of Sankau. Otora-sama, please make the Mosahikichin cheap, for if we are treated to Mikake, lately all of Wareware are Funiyoi right at Temoto and that makes it troublesome, you see.'

Otora: 'Kuwanran and Kenbutsu, what Motsutai things you are Otsushiya! You are not going to look, you are going to Sanpai. Why do you not say that you are allowed to go and Sanpai?'

Mankō: 'Sanpai, far from it, after having been made to listen to such a large amount of misdirected oracle, my belly and chest are full, aha ha ha ha!'

Otora: 'Now, Matsudai-sama, I shall guide you. Please follow this hag no matter what.'

Matsuhiko reluctantly followed behind the old woman along with the company to the foot of a large pine tree with fine branches.

When he looked, an extraordinary, gigantic rock was surrounded by a boundary fence, with cut stones piled up, and it was grandly enshrined in a lofty place like an ornament. And an information stone stood nearby, on which was inscribed in Imoriwake's handwriting,

"The sacred pine tree of the prosperous divine government"

Isokō: 'I say grandmother, what on earth is this huge rock? And it is inscribed as a sacred pine tree, but is this not a tree, is it not a rock?'

Otora: 'Is it not fine not to worry about such things and not to argue about principles? It is written as "the sacred pine tree of the prosperous divine government" so that you people will treat it as a sacred tree. Since this is the boundary between good and evil, the Chibiki-no-Iwa is placed like this so that evil Kamisamas cannot crawl into the Takamanohara of the land of Kogitayama. On the surface, it is said to be the Goshintai of Miroku-sama. And sixteen small pines are planted just like this as a sign that sixteen Kamisamas are enshrined. However, this is for the outside; the truth of the matter is that Susanoo-no-Mikoto's Aramitama was sealed right in here so that he could not move, surrounded on all eight sides by stone pavements, with the Chibiki-no-Iwa placed on top, sealed away so that he could never rise for all eternity. Because of that, Susanoo-no-Mikoto of the pure soul is just like being blocked on all eight sides, unable to advance two or three steps, and is suffering. We went through a great deal of hardship even when transporting this stone here. Above all, Imoriwake-sama, Magahiko-sama, and Daishōgun-sama—the diligent efforts of us Otora and others were truly tremendous. Working without sleeping for some twenty days, night and day, we finally sealed away the evil Kamisama Susanoo-no-Mikoto. The fools of the Hinodekyō know nothing, and therefore they still think that Susanoo-no-Mikoto is appearing in this world. If we do it this way, it is Imoriwake-sama's divine strategy to pull all the believers of Hinodekyō into Kogitayama like rats dragging rice cakes. How truly grand it must be.'

Both Mankō and Isokō flew into a rage, and from both sides they firmly grabbed the old woman's hands,

Mankō: 'You filthy hag, I will not put up with this any longer. I am going to bury you in this river, so think of it that way. You utter outrageous things.'

Isokō: 'I say, Otora, a frog by its own mouth—since you have confessed by your own hands, I will no longer tolerate this. Now prepare yourself. Hey Mankō, take her legs, I too will take these legs and carry her to the deep pool of the river and throw her in.'
