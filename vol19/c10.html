Chapter 10: Horse Deer 正直 [655]

馬鹿正直〔655〕

§ 1

Soaring above the clouds, along the ridges of Takashiroyama, stands the mountain of Kami where the Shiyōjiyu grow thick. Between the trees flashes the Toeu Mon. The Mioshihe of Kuniharutachi Ōkami, of Haniyasukami, and of the Hime Mikoto, is transmitted to the four quarters; the Kamuyakata resounds as the Deyashiro of the Kototama faith. Matsuhime, morning and evening, pours her Magokoro into the Kototama of prayer for the Miyo of Miroku. The hundred Kami gather, and acknowledging her Mikokoro—which, though called the teaching of the ugly, thinks of the Mikuni and thinks of the world—they deign to protect it; how noble it is.

At the front gate of the Matsuhimeyakata, a station for the reception and the gatekeepers is established. Tatsuwaka, Kumahiko, and Torahiko, these three, are whiling away the short winter day with innocent talk.

Tatsuwaka said, 'Around this spring, the weather was fine, and as buds sprout daily, seekers of the way followed one upon another; we were indeed extremely busy with the reception and the opening and closing of the gate. But spring has departed, summer has passed, autumn has gone, and in these days when winter comes, snow flutters, the cold wind blows, and the treetops have become bare as if white, white flowers had bloomed. Has this hall of the Uranai faith, rather than knowing the autumn of the world by the falling of a single leaf, become the winter of the world where all leaves have fallen and loneliness is extreme? Even if one says that prosperity and decay are the customs of the world, the decline of the Uranai faith is truly a pitiful and fleeting state. For us to be sitting here idly like borrowed pugs, having nothing to do, is somehow lacking in spirit. I cannot help but feel sorry for Matsuhime-sama as well. Ah, has the cold and cruel winter come to the Uranai faith as well?'

Kumahiko said, 'That is the blessing of the spirit. It is because there is winter that spring comes. Does not Kami say that it is a mechanism of pulling back and forth? The waves of the sea and the wind are just the same. When the wind of seven, five, and three blows, the waves rise; around this spring, the wind of seven blew and the waves of seven rose for the Uranai faith. When summer came, it was like the wind of five and the waves of five, and from the end of autumn to the beginning of winter, the wind of three blows and the waves of three are striking. Also, because the history of the world repeats itself, the flowering spring will surely visit the Uranai faith. It is unlikely that the Uranai faith alone will be looking at the coldness of winter forever while the world is in spring; you should not be so pessimistic.'

Torahiko said, 'Kumakō, you are indeed an optimist. With a method like a spider spinning a web and waiting for insects to be caught, no matter how much time passes, spring will not visit the Uranai faith. After all, it will not do unless we expend the best effort possible. Luck is not something that comes even if you wait with folded arms. After all, must we not start our activities from this side? Moreover, lately, even Matsuhime-sama of the hall has stopped the missionary work of the propagandists, has she not? Altogether, we cannot help but struggle to understand.'

§ 2

Tatsuwaka spake: 'Since we, the Sendenshi, have failed to accord with the Goshinriyo, we are being subjected to discipline until we have sufficiently polished our Mitama during this quietude, attained the understanding of the Goshinriyo, and grasped the true Ōmikokoro of the Kamisama, such that the Kamisama may deem us fit to be sent forth for Senden. The steed of Tsukihi shall not return again, and the dawn of one Ichijitsu is hard to be regained; thus, in this Kikuwai, we must perform the Jiyūbun Tamamiga. To conduct Senden full of Datsusen as we have done heretofore serves only to increase the Konran and Kyōwaku of the world. Believing we were performing the Ritsupa Goyō of the Kamisama, we have done naught but Jiyama; it is for this reason that the Kamisama, as a warning, has stayed the Senden and kept the Kiudaushiya from us of late. If we, the Ichidō, come to comprehend the true and sincere Kamigokoro, the Kamisama shall surely permit us to perform Senden and shall draw unto us the Mitama of Inen. For the Kamisama is flawless in all things, from beginning to end.'

Kumahiko spake: 'Yet, in that regard, is not the Sangōkyō relatively flourishing? Takahime-sama and Kurohime-sama, those of high standing, exerted their vaunted divine craft and demonic wisdom to welcome Tamateruhime-sama into the Uranai-kyō, but they were outwitted by Murasakihime of the Sangōkyō and that fellow Aohiko, and thus were made fools of; truly, there is no moment for negligence or respite. Furthermore, that which I cannot fathom is the conduct of Matsuhime-sama. Osetsu, the wife of that turncoat Aohiko, has been using a fawning voice of late to ingratiate herself with Matsuhime-sama, and now she serves in the inner chambers, does she not? I fear she may play the second act of Kurohime and meet with a foolish end. However vast the heart of Matsuhime-sama may be, like the great ocean that swallows both the pure and the impure, to allow a dangerous person like Osetsu to dwell within the inner chambers is akin to sleeping while embracing a bursting bomb. Why is it that Matsuhime-sama does not perceive a principle so manifest?'

Torahiko spake: 'Be that as it may, they are of the faction of the evil gods of Susanoo-no-Mikoto, whose schemes and stratagems know no bounds, and who transform their forms in a thousand ways; since she is a monster bearing such danger that she has, with audacity, come even unto this place despite being but a woman, we must, upon discovering even a single flaw in Osetsu, seize the Kikuwai to remonstrate with the commander Matsuhime, even at the risk of our own positions, and cast Osetsu out.'

§ 3

Tatsuwaka said, 'That is indeed so. Since even women who have entered the Ananai-kyō possess such settled courage, the men are all the more unmanageable. Furthermore, one never knows when those of the Ananai-kyō might come hither and attempt to repeat the dance of the Ihaya of Makutsugahara; therefore, be ever vigilant, for if they be of the Renchiu of the Ananai-kyō, not a single step shall they be permitted to take within this Monnai. Whether by sweeping them out with a broom, or if that be unheeded, by beating them with a Rokushiyakubō until they are chastened, we must act, lest the Uranai-kyō be overturned from its very Kontei before we know it. As for Matsuhime-sama, whether it be a wolf or a tiger, she is troubled because she devours all without distinction of Rauniyakunanniyo. It is truly Kennon-senban, for she swallows Dokuyaku into her belly and remains Heiki. Henceforth, we shall Suika every person who appears, and none shall be permitted Tsūkau unless their Mitama has been examined. To permit or deny entry through this gate is the authority and great responsibility of us all; therefore, let us form a triangular alliance, strengthen our unity, and resolve to resolutely deny passage to any suspicious person of strange character. Even the Ikumiya of Hinode-no-Kami or the Ikumiya of Ryūgū-no-Otohime-sama would be subjected to such ridiculous and miserable fates, as if having their kettles stolen on a moonlit night, so we must maintain strict vigilance, for it is a matter of national emergency. This single gate is the place where the fate of life or death is determined.'

At this juncture, the two, Ma and Shika, opened the wicket door with a rattling sound and entered.

Kumahiko said, 'Halt! To enter by opening the wicket door without any response to us, the gatekeepers, is most insolent, is it not? Come, depart at once.'

Bakō said, 'Ah, this is truly a grave discourtesy. It was so silent that I was quite unaware that you were guarding the gate in such stern attire, and in my haste, I entered without responding, for which I beg your pardon. I pray you, overlook this impropriety, and by the grace of Kamunao-bi and Ōnao-bi, look upon me and listen to me anew, and grant me passage.'

Kumahiko said, 'When I say it shall not be, it is absolutely not to be. Depending on the circumstances and the nature of the matter, there are times when I might allow passage, but in your case, it is impossible. The reason is that you have just said, "Look upon me and listen to me anew by the grace of Kamunao-bi and Ōnao-bi." Those who utter such phrases are only of the two sects in this wide world: the Uranai-kyō and the Ananai-kyō. However, you are not a person of the Uranai-kyō. You are surely a piece of rubbish from the Ananai-kyō. If I were to allow a fellow like you to infiltrate this mansion, the interior would be transformed in an instant; and if that were to happen, we too would be done for in an instant. Like the autumn sky that changes in an instant, shivering as winter approaches, we would have our faces flayed and be cast out, and even a man of seven shaku would be finished in an instant.'

Umakō said, 'Yea, yea, that is truly a kind and gracious favor. That the two of us, the horse and the deer of Ananai-kyō, should come hither, and that the wise Matsuhime, as one would expect, should know of it and cause the gatekeeper to wait in readiness to welcome us—this is indeed so. The gate of the heart opens in an instant; from this time forth, it shall surely become the protection of the Hinodewake. Come, Shikakō, let us receive permission and proceed into the inner depths.'

Kumahiko replied, 'What is this? How strange; you speak of horses and deer, and indeed, you possess the countenances of fools. Hearken, this gate is the passage for the one God of absolute goodness and for mankind. It is no place for four-legged beasts to pass. Be off with you at once!'

Umakō answered, 'It is true that our names are Horse and Deer, and we are indeed four-legged, but look upon this gate; is it not also four-legged? Furthermore, do they not call your names Dragon, Bear, or Tiger? You are also four-legged, are you not? That a four-legged gate should be guarded by the four-legged—it is a most excellent contrast, ha ha ha!'

Torahiko exclaimed, 'Tiger? What are you spewing? If you desire a contrast so much, shall I strike your kettle with this club to provide such a contrast?'

No sooner had he spoken than he took up a six-foot staff from his side and struck the foreheads of the Horse and the Deer two or three times.

Shikakō cried, 'The Uranai-kyō is truly given to such rough deeds.'

Torahiko retorted, 'What? It is not the Uranai-kyō that does these rough deeds; it is your own evil heart that compels this Torahiko to strike you. This is what is meant by the tiger of the heart tormenting the body. Because you spoke foolish things and begged for passage, I have granted your order and bestowed the club upon you. Henceforth, be humble and do not commit such violent acts. The guardian deities of the Horse and the Deer, in their majesty, use the body of Torahiko-san to make fools of you, ha ha ha!'

Umakō said, 'I have been deeply in the wrong. I pray you, grant me your forgiveness.'

Kumahiko replied, 'Do you now understand that you were in the wrong? If you say you were wrong, do you think that is enough to earn forgiveness? To call this magnificent divine gate a four-legged gate, and to call the three of us four-legged—such insolence! What is this? I know not if such unreasonable and selfish logic passes in Ananai-kyō, but such sophistry shall not pass in the Uranai-kyō, which is devoted to the path of sincerity.'

Shikakō said, 'Nay, whether it passes or not is of no consequence; our purpose is served if only we pass through this gate. It was truly inexcusable to open the gate in silence, but there is a proverb that says, "The peach and the plum tree do not speak." Therefore, we passed through with a quiet and reverent demeanor.'

Torahiko exclaimed, 'Ah, thou babbling creature! If thou continuest to dally with thy words, the fern of the demon shall pay thee a visit.'

So saying, he clenched his bony fist and struck the face of Shikakō two or three times with a heavy thud.

Shikakō cried out, 'Ouch, ouch, ouch! Thy strength is indeed formidable.'

Torahiko replied, 'It is but natural; though I may appear thus, I am a master of the hundredth degree, having forged my body through the arts of Kenjiyutsu and Jiujiyutsu from morn till night. I am Torahiko, whose Zenshin is hardened with iron; my Tetsushin and Tetsuchiyō are such that thou mayest bring spears or Tetsupau and strike or thrust at me as thou wilt. It is as if a gnat were to launch a Shifugeki against a Kautetsukan, ha ha ha!'
