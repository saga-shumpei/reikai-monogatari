Chapter 21: Small 北 Mountain [1190]

小北山〔1190〕

§ 1

Matsuhiko and his party, after resting for a while, climbed the steep slope for about one chō, and carving their way up about two hundred fine steps of kizahashi, they finally arrived at the kadoguchi of the Kogitayamakamuyakata.

There, an old man with hakuhatsu sat neatly like an okimono before a desk, dressed in biyakui and shirohakama.

From the inner quarters, the murmuring voices of kinen could be heard.

Matsuhiko:

'Grandfather, I am a traveler, and having heard that a wondrous Kamisama is enshrined here, I have come to pay my sanpai; what is the name of the teaching here?'

Old man:

'I know not whence you come, but welcome indeed, you have come for gosankei.

Since I cannot see, I am doing the uketsu like this, yet it is a grateful thing; hearing a person's voice, whether man or woman, old person or young person, person of good heart or bad heart, I understand it well, so it is a grateful thing.

And being frequently asked by people, I am drawing pictures just like this.'

Matsuhiko:

'How strange indeed, show them to me for a moment, let me see.'

Old man:

'Yes, yes, please look; even so, believers are pleased to make them into kakemono or hangings, so...'

Matsuhiko:

'Indeed, for pictures drawn by a blind person, they are quite admirable.

My, a ryūjin is winding around a pine tree, daikon and turnips—even Maruyama Ōkyo would run away barefoot.

Hey, Mankō-san, you like daikon and turnips, don't you? How would it be if you were given one?'

Mankō: 'Matsuhiko-sama, you too seem to have a most wicked mitama, please look at this picture; is there not a black snake with a single horn winding around the pine named after you?'

Old man:

'I know not from what region you come, but this is the Goshintai of the Otohime of Ryūgū.

Do not utter such irreverent things as calling it a black snake.'

Mankō: 'Even so, does it not have a large mouth with a black rope drawn to it?

That is why I said it was a black kuchi-nawa.'

Old man:

'Ahahaha, it is a troubled matter indeed since you have no eyes for viewing pictures, nah.'

Mankō: 'That we have no eyes here is only natural.

Since it was drawn by a blind grandfather, why, this might well be a ryūjin of Meido.'

Old man:

'You have come to this hall to mock, have you not? Such people, please return; your attitude is wicked.'

Matsuhiko:

'Grandfather, this fellow's wits are a bit touched, so please overlook it.

The truth is, I brought him here hoping you would cure this madness; since he crawls into the rice fields, chews raw daikon and turnips, and stuffs sweet potatoes into his mouth with the mud still on them, he is a hopeless lunatic fit for an asylum.

Is there not some way you might cure him?'

Old man:

'Indeed, hearing you say so, it appears that this person's wits are touched; somehow my spirit felt that way from the very beginning.

How pitiable it is, nah.

Since this madness will not be easily cured, until his spirits calm down for a while, there is a stone prison kept, so I shall take charge of him and thrust him into a dark place for about three weeks.'

Mankō: 'Oh no, Grandfather, that is quite enough.

Since seeing your august face, my mood has gradually improved and somehow I have already become sober.

That is quite enough, thank you.'

Old man:

'Even so, it would be troublesome should it recur, so shall I put him in for two or three days?

You who are called Matsuhiko-sama, what is your thought?'

Mankō is pulling at Matsuhiko's sleeve persistently.

Matsuhiko:

'My, if it is to this degree, there should be no great matter.

Well, let us ask you after watching his condition for a while.'

Old man:

'If so, I shall leave it to your judgment.

I will take charge of him at any time.'

Matsuhiko:

'Yes, thank you very much.

I beg you to treat him kindly.'

Gosankō, pulling Mankō's sleeve gently with a twitch:

'Oh, is he not drawing only a black snake on a pine, and turnips on a daikon?

He is just like a grandfather such as Shōhō Bunzō-sama dwelling in the Miroku-dono of the twentieth-century Ansu Church, isn't he?'

'There are too many to count immediately, but whatever you say, they are the eight million Kamisama. 'Since the names of the Kamisama appear according to their functions, the Kyōso Imoriwake gave them names so that if you just hear the name, you can well understand what they will protect you from. Originally, Kamisama have no names; it is humans who give them names and praise them.'
