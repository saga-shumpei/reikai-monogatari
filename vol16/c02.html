Chapter 2: Darkness Night の邂逅 [592]

暗夜の邂逅〔592〕

§ 1

Driven by the cold blast of the Ōeoroshi, Hidekohime advances; for the Mitama entrusted unto Kami, whatever may befall, so too for Yoshikohime.

Upon the mountain path ascending on tiptoe, stumbling and falling, they keep watch over the Atari; do the Oni, the Orochi, and the Magatsukami fear these two Otome?

Beyond the valley, the cry of a fox echoes, 'kon-kon'; neither man nor Oni appears, yet even to the bottom of the Rinnaraku, must they search and seek, and shall they leave without meeting their Chichiuhe?

Their Ashinami faltering, they reach the vicinity of the port of Yura; at that very Orikara, a Sendenka with a clear voice rings out through the darkness.

In the Monokage by the roadside, the two stand close and conceal themselves, peering to see who it might be; though they cannot discern clearly in the Yome, it is the Sendenshi of deep Inshiyō, from whom they parted at Kenonkiyō.

It is the figure of a Masurao, valiant and dignified, who has come seeking the whereabouts of Kamususanō, the Kamehiko who celebrates the Yorozuyo; Hidekohime is ready to take flight, and at that moment, the two princesses, Hideko and Yoshiko, emerge from the darkness, their voices trembling as they speak.

Hidekohime: 'Pray, traveler, though it be sudden, I would ask you a question. We are two women who, for certain reasons, have journeyed from a distant land and at last arrived at the center of this Jitentō Island; we are but weak women. Are you not a Sendenshi of the Sangokyō?'

At the sound of the women's voices heard suddenly from the darkness, Kamehiko knits his brows in suspicion and stops abruptly; remaining silent for a while, he stands on the slope with his arms folded, tilting his head to the left, as if untangling a knot of thread, tracing back his ancient memories. Though he traces and traces, the knot in his heart does not easily unravel; it is as if he stands at a crossroads of a hundred, a thousand, or eight thousand paths, lost in his way forward.

From the darkness, as the voices of the two women: 'Pray, traveler, does your lack of reply mean that you are not an acquaintance of ours, or do you mistake us for female bandits? We are by no means suspicious persons; some time ago, we encountered a strange shadow at the foot of the Ryūtōmatsu and have finally escaped to this place. If it be no hindrance, pray, declare your name.' Kamehiko: 'Though the voices sound somewhat familiar, as I have a matter weighing upon my heart, my memory does not easily surface; I pray you, declare your names.' From within the darkness, a frantic voice: Kinshū: 'Hey, what is this? Standing in the middle of the road, talking of names and such—what a strange creature. Oi, Genshū, will you not wake up? A strange fellow has come, has he not?'

§ 2

Genshiu: 'Ugh, muniya muniya muniya muniya.'

Kinshiu: 'Hey Genshiu, it is a Taihen matter.'

Genshiu: 'Ugh, ugh, ugh, ugh, what is it, you noisy fellow. You, Kinshiu, have shaken me awake in the Saichiu of my enjoying a Dotsusari of Gochisō after a Dekuha at a house of Sauren; now, a Batsukin! I shall demand a Seikiu for the Songai of my Gochisō. Ah, I am sleepy, so sleepy.'

Kinshiu: 'Sleepy over there, sleepy over here; alas, it has come to pass that I cannot understand the situation at all. Even if one calls this Ukiyo a dream, it is an age where a strange fellow named Onikumohiko has appeared at Ōeyama, so perhaps a Bakemono has appeared in the Yachiu, and someone has emerged who sleeps vertically with the Ten as his pillow. Hey Genshiu, will you not wake? Fortunately, it is the Yonaka, and the Mukafu is but Hitori, while the Kataitsupau is a Zetsupeki like a wall. The other side is a Dangai, and to add to that, it is a sea where the Aranami rages; thus, if we do not perform a Renshifu of Ohihagi at such a time, there will be no time to do so. Come, wake up, wake up. Hey, hey, Name, will you not strip your belongings clean and give them to Kin-sama here?'

Kamehiko: 'Unfortunately, I am lacking in my pockets after a long journey, and I have little affinity for Kin-san. Aha ha ha ha.'

The two women: 'Oho ho ho ho, oho ho ho ho.'

Genshiu, rising up abruptly, said:

'There, that is Sasshari Gensuke; what is this, I thought it was a man's voice, but it suddenly changed into a woman's voice; the Magokami, they are playing a clever game.'

Kamehiko: 'Gen, I do not possess any gold. In its stead, shall I give you a fist-rice?'

(In this region, 'fist-rice' is a synonym for a hard-packed rice ball.)

Genshiu: 'Yea, that is thoughtful. I am grateful, I shall not hesitate, no matter how much you have. How much do you possess in total? Hey Kinshiu, how about you also receive a Dotsusari of fist-rice instead of gold?'

Kinshiu: 'Yo, that is grateful indeed. Excuse me, traveler, will you truly give them to us? It is fine for me to receive them, but your own stomach may become empty. Please leave two or three, and give us the rest.'

Kamehiko: 'Yea, my fist-rice is inexhaustible. If you desire, I shall give you a hundred, a thousand, or even ten thousand. Come, show your face, turn your cheeks, and draw near; it is somewhat dim and I cannot take the Kentō.'

(In this region, a snack is called 'kentō'.)

Genshiu: 'With this, I have finally taken the Kentō. If possible, please give them into my hand. To receive them on the cheeks is close to the mouth and seems good, but it would be a waste if they were to roll and fall.'

§ 3

Kamehiko, turning toward the direction of the voice, clenched his fists.

Kamehiko exclaimed, 'Come, you thief, eat this!' and struck out with his fists in a wild and reckless barrage.

Genshiu cried out, 'Ouch, ouch, ouch, ouch! That is a terribly hard fist indeed. In this darkness, I have become quite unable to tell where I have fallen. If you were going to give it to me, it would have been better to place it gently in my hand.'

Kamehiko replied, 'You insolent thief, if you continue to babble, shall I trample you underfoot and crush you to pieces?'

Genshiu retorted, 'Ah, what a waste! My eyes will be crushed. To trample upon or crush such fine rice balls—you should not treat so lightly the rice that the farmers have labored to produce with the sweat of their brows.'

Hidekohime laughed, 'Ho, ho, ho, ho.'

Yoshikohime laughed, 'Ho, ho, ho, ho.'

Kinshiu said, 'Nay, what is this? This fellow is a monster. He uses his voice in three different ways, becoming a man and then a woman; do not make a fool of me. Most likely, he called a Dangoishi a rice ball and threw it at me; no wonder it hurt.'

Kamehiko laughed, 'Aha, ha, ha, ha.'

The two women laughed, 'Ho, ho, ho, ho.'

Kinshiu shouted, 'Hey, this fellow is truly a madman. Hey, Genshiu, life is the seed of all things; let us flee, let us flee!'

Saying this, he lay down in the darkness and rolled away as if he were a rolling dumpling.

Kamehiko said, 'Aha, ha, ha, what a strange beggar that was. Yet, from here on is a dangerous zone; I must be careful. Excuse me, my ladies, from which direction do you come?'

Hidekohime replied, 'It cannot be helped; I shall tell you. I am Hidekohime, daughter of Susanoo-no-Mikoto. The other is my attendant, Yoshikohime.'

Kamehiko said, 'That voice is unmistakable; this is, this is—forgive my rudeness, as it is a dark night. I am Kamehiko, as you know.'

Hidekohime said, 'If that is so, you are the husband of my younger sister, Kikukohime. Meeting you in such an unexpected place gives me great strength. And what is your purpose in coming here?'

Kamehiko replied, 'Though it is difficult to speak of, your father, since the incident in Takamagahara, has been burdened with the thousand-fold offerings and has set out on a journey of wandering the world. I, at the summit of the mountain of Saien, learned of your father's whereabouts and heard that he had descended to Jitentōtō; therefore, I crossed the rough seas from afar, and having finally arrived at the port of Yura, I am on my way here to seek his location. According to the rumors, the Great Father-God has been captured by the demons of Mount Ōe, but now that I, Kamehiko, have arrived, you must surely not worry. I shall certainly rescue him and bring him before you.'

§ 4

At this moment, from within the thicket of trees nearby, twenty or thirty men appeared before the three.

"Ha! You are the scoundrels of the faction of Susanōnomikoto; we have heard every word of your whispering from the beginning, hidden in the shade of the trees. Come, we shall bind you and drag you back to the fortress of Ōeyama; prepare yourselves."

With these words, blades of ice flashed in the darkness, thrusting from all four quarters and eight directions. The three drew their double-edged tantau with a flourish,

"How insolent, you petty mushiya!"

And they defended themselves with the vigor of a lion in a frenzy. The scores of torite surged forward with a roar, only to retreat again, like the waves of the shore that advance and recede, while the sound of kengeki echoed through the atari.

Kamehiko cried, "Against such akugiyakubudau magami, it is a waste to employ the Kototama of zengenbishi; for the sake of chastisement, I shall cut them down, cut them down, and cast them aside!"

And he raged like Ashiyuraō. The enemy divided into two, fleeing like clouds and mist.

Kamehiko descended the slope and ran toward the west, ever toward the west. Meanwhile, Hidekohime and Etsukohime fought brilliantly against the attacking enemy, and even the magami could not withstand them, fleeing toward the east. The two pursued them with all their might. Ah, what shall be the outcome of this?
