Chapter 4: Dream か現か [594]

夢か現か〔594〕

§ 1

Kamehiko arrived at the entrance of the main residence, feeling as though he had worn the two gatekeepers like shoes.

Two women appeared from within, pulling open the door amidst the clamor at the gate, and exclaimed, 'Oh, are you Kamehiko-sama?'

Kamehiko replied, 'Greetings, you two; it is a wonder to meet you in such a place.'

Hidekohime said, 'Kamehiko-sama, what is it that you have caught upon your feet?'

Kamehiko answered, 'Oh, it is nothing at all; a single Ginbahe born of dung and a single Kusogame have bitten onto my feet; if you have any raw loach, pray give me one, ha ha ha ha.'

The two women laughed, 'Ho ho ho ho.'

Ginkō and Kamekō muttered, 'Tch, he makes a fool of us; he calls this Ginkōtsukasa a Ginbahe, and Kamekō a Kusogame, speaking such selfish nonsense. You, Kame, even a tiny insect has a soul of five bu; I shall surely take my revenge, so you had best be prepared for it.'

The moment Kamehiko kicked his right foot toward the mid-heaven, Ginkō soared seven or eight ken through the air, spinning and spinning, and landed decorously before his own room at the front gate, sitting there motionless.

When Kamehiko kicked his left foot, Kamekō danced through the air like a ball and sat motionless once more in his own gatekeeper's hut.

Ginkō cried, 'Ah, how wretched; I thought a great fellow had come and kicked me into the mid-heaven, but was I merely dreaming? Still, what a strange dream I have had.'

Kamekō replied, 'Hey, Ginkō, did you also have a dream? It was the same for me. I thought a fellow called Kame had come and kicked me into the mid-heaven with the tip of his foot, but it was indeed a dream. Ah, to have such dreams bodes no good; let the Baku devour them, let the Baku devour them, for all is vast and vague like a dream, ha ha ha.'

At this moment, a voice sounded before the gate, 'Excuse me, gatekeepers, I am a woman on a wandering journey; I beseech your mercy, pray open this gate. Surrounded by evil spirits, I have fled to this place with my life.'

Saying, 'Oh, a woman's voice I do not recognize,' they opened the gate with a slide, and the two women, bowing politely, hurried toward the inner depths.

Ginkō said, 'Hey, Kamekō, the two beauties we saw in our dreams have arrived. Dreams are not to be taken lightly.'

Kamekō replied, 'Yes, I saw that dream as well. They are the very image of the beauties I saw in my dream, not differing in the slightest. However, if dreams come true in this manner, the strong fellow called Kamehiko who will appear next will be a terrible matter indeed; it is best to be humble and let them pass through the gate in peace.'

§ 2

Ginkō said, 'Oh, indeed, shall I open it gently so as not to be an opponent?'

At this moment, a man's voice was heard at the gate, knocking upon it with a rhythmic sound, 'Excuse me, I am a traveler named Kamehiko; I fear I may be an intrusion, but would you be so kind as to open this gate?'

Kamekō said, 'There, there, the dream has become reality; Kame is a fine companion.'

Saying this, he opened the gate with a smooth motion.

Kamekō said, 'Welcome, you have come at a good time. Pray, proceed deep within. Now, as for tossing you into the mid-heaven—oops, steady now, this was but a dream; pray, enter gently and without violence.'

Kamehiko replied, 'I shall never commit any act of violence; pray, be at ease.'

With these words, he fixed his gaze upon the interior and advanced with a composed gait.

The official of the people of the port of Yura, Akiyamahiko, had the gate opened in a flash and entered. The dark night cleared, and Hidekohime, along with Yoshikohime, who beheld the scenery of the four quarters, received the morning sun of Koharu upon their bodies. Though it was called the beginning of winter, the Hafuguchi was still warm, and there was a tranquility where a cat slept. The gatekeeper, who slept not night nor day, performed his loyal service to his master. He slid open the middle gate, and the man, bold and without hesitation, was escorted by the gatekeeper to the entrance. As he called out, 'Is anyone there? Is anyone there?', two women answered, 'Yes,' and pushed open the sliding doors to emerge. Their faces met with a start—'Oh, is it Kamehiko? Is it the Princess?'

They had met in an unexpected place. He wished to ask how the Majin fared, but he waited a moment. This mansion was not to be trusted; one knew not what Majin might be lurking within. Like a horse passing a gap, he must be wary, for there were ears in the walls that must not hear. On this morning of autumn, the eighth day of the ninth month, the month of chrysanthemums, he brought tidings of the father. Communicating through their eyes, the two women walked quietly toward the inner chamber. Left behind, Kamehiko stood dejected, his hands clasped, wearing a look of deep contemplation. 'Ah, how suspicious, how suspicious. This mansion has an air of mystery. The look in the eyes of Princess Hideko is of no ordinary nature. Shall I push open the door and step inside? Wait, wait a moment, wait a moment.'

A small matter before a great event. If I should fail at this time, long labors would become as bubbles on water. Not meeting is often better than meeting; such examples are many in the passing days. He decided to stand here for a while to investigate the state of the house, both inside and out, in minute detail. To see and hear again with a direct heart, to reconsider—how valiant it is.

Kamehiko, standing at the entrance, pressed his palms together toward the rising morning sun, continuing to offer a silent, heartfelt prayer. At this moment, two maidens appeared, having swiftly slid open the entrance doors, and bowed respectfully toward Kamehiko.

§ 3

"Oh, oh, a guest from afar; let me guide you to the inner chambers, come, pray follow me."

Pointing toward the corridor, she proceeds ahead with a smile.

Kamehiko thought to himself:

"Ah, how grateful, how grateful I am; had I taken but one wrong turn, I would have suffered the misery of being turned away at the gate. Ah, there are no demons in this world; here lies a vast garden. Demons out, fortune in; the appearance of the house is somehow elegant, and even the sound of a single-stringed koto is audible. That voice is surely the handiwork of Hidekohime; this house is naturally a wind of peace, a god of fortune, and the manner in which those of high and low station dwell together in harmony, passing the days—could it be that in this house, the great Ōkami Kamususanō resides in concealment? Even in my heart, which is not that of a Kami, the atmosphere within and without is serene."

So he muttered to himself, following the maiden along the long corridor.

A man of about fifty, whose appearance and bearing were not lowly, and who appeared to be the master of this house, came forth to greet him with his attire properly arranged.

"Why, this is the Kamehiko-sama, the missionary of Sangokyō of whom I have heard rumors; you have done well to enter. I am Akiyama-hiko, the overseer of the children of this village. Come, come, without reserve, pray proceed deep into the inner chambers; I shall guide you."

He proceeds ahead. Kamehiko, tilting his head in suspicion, casts his eyes to the front, rear, left, and right, focusing his mind.

"Ah, this is a reception of disagreeable courtesy. If I dally, might I be ensnared and cast into a pitfall like the cavern of the ugly? Nay, nay, to doubt others is the greatest of sins; they say that if there is a cloud in one's heart, one doubts others. Ah, how shameful; it seems the subordinate guardian deity is still entrenched in a part of my body, planning to loose arrows of suspicion and exercise its ferocity. The enemy within the heart is to be feared."

He exclaimed this aloud without intending to.

Hearing this voice, Akiyama-hiko turned back and said:

"Why, Kamehiko-sama, you spoke of an enemy just now; I am by no means an enemy, so please proceed without worry."

"Nay, I am truly sorry; it was the subordinate guardian deity lurking in our hearts that whispered. They say the demon of the heart torments the body; alas, we humans who are not Kami, though we bear the fine label of missionary, are truly shameful articles."

So he spoke as they walked.

"Now, this is the inner chamber; there is one who wishes to meet you, so pray, do enter."

§ 4

She bowed her waist and, with grace, pushed open the sliding door to announce them.

Kamehiko entered, enveloped in clouds of suspicion, and upon looking toward the upper seat, behold, how strange it was; for upon the high seat in the center, the Great Ōkami Kamususanō sat in solemn majesty.

Slightly below him sat Kunitakehiko, and on the right side was Hidekohime, with Yoshikohime sitting further down, and a beautiful woman of about forty years, who appeared to be the wife of this house, sat in attendance with proper decorum.

Kamehiko, upon catching a single glimpse, was struck with astonishment.

"Ah, are you the Mikotosama?" he said, and having spoken but one word, his vision was clouded by tears; and covering his face with his sleeves, he remained for a while prostrate in silence.

The woman of about forty, as if impatient for Kamehiko to lift his head, spoke in a tone of voice:

"Are you indeed Kamehiko-sama? You have done well to come; I am Momijihime, the wife of Akiyamahiko, and as you know, this is an inconvenient land, and we can offer you little hospitality, but pray, do stay with us at your leisure."

Kamehiko replied, "This is a greeting for which I am truly humbled; I pray for your kind favor in all matters. Ah, were you the Mikotosama? I am glad indeed that you have remained safe; I am filled with joy."

Kamususanō replied, "Are you Kamehiko? It is a matter for celebration that you are safe; I hear that Hidekohime was greatly indebted to you on the way."

Kamehiko replied, "It was nothing at all."

Hidekohime said, "Kamehiko-sama, it is a joy that you too are safe; until this very moment, I have been anxious for you, but now I am at peace."

Just as they were rejoicing, the area outside the gate suddenly became noisy with many voices, and Akiyamahiko entered in haste.

"Ah, everyone, please be silent; I shall take charge of the front, for a small matter has arisen."

Kamususanō laughed, "Hahaha, handle it as you see fit."

Kamehiko said, "Akiyamahiko-dono, this matter that has arisen must be the raid of Onikumohiko; I pray you, take me with you, for ah, how interesting, how interesting; now is the time to test the power of the Kototama which I have trained in daily."

So saying, he prepared to go forth in the lead.

Kunitakehiko opened his mouth for the first time and said:

"Ah, Kamehiko, wait a moment; until you have the permission of the Mikoto, you must not move from this place even an inch."

Kamehiko, scratching his head with his right hand, replied:

"Heh, heh, heh, heh, very well, I have no choice; yes, my arms are trembling, they are humming and I cannot help it."

All of them laughed, "Hahahaha, ohohoho."
