Chapter 1: 天 Bridge 立 [591]

天橋立〔591〕

§ 1

In the land of Ashihara no Mizuho, known by name as the Kenonkiyō of Mesopotamia, Onikumohiko of the lineage of Ham, born as a descendant of Noah, made the Brahman Kyō his shield. He proclaimed the doctrine of Reishiyutaijiu, holding the present world in contempt, and taught that the Kakuriyo, the destination of the soul, was the Kakihatokiha Sumika. Yet, as his name and reality did not accord, his ugly Magagoto spread day by day like the tide. The Shinjin beneath the Ama suffered and writhed, their Murakimo hearts twisted day by day, and the world grew clouded in Tokoyami. To subdue the Yamataorochi, the Shikogitsune, and the Magaoni led by the Shikogami, and to establish the abundant Mikuni of the Kami through Kotomukeyaha, the Mizumitama of vast grace, Ōkami Kamususanō, dispatched the precious children of the Yaotome who open the teachings. He sent the messenger of the Sangokyō, Kamotama no Mikoto, to watch over the person of Onikumohiko and to calm the ugly arrogance of the Magagami. As he governed Kenonkiyō in unity of heart and strength with the Yaotome, the great leader of the Brahman Kyō, Onikumohiko and his kin, fled like clouds and mist, crossing over to the land of Fusa and advancing the power of evil while spreading their religious lines here and there.

At such a time, the Ōmikami hid within the Ama no Iwato in the Takamagahara of the Amakyōsan. Taking advantage of the sorrow of men in a world wandering in the darkness of the Nukabane, the Magagami, appearing to have gained the hour, began to rage ever more. Ōkami Kamususanō, bearing the Chikura no Okido, wandered on a long journey, not knowing where to turn. Hiding his form, he suffered ten million afflictions. In the sky of his journey, with the thought of a hototogisu crying eight thousand and eight times until it vomits blood, he drifted through the Yashima no Kuni, without distinction of far or near. The end of his person, wandering, was pitiful. Even the mighty Ryūjin, when the time is not his, hides his form.

Becoming as a newt or an earthworm, lurking in dust and refuse, the Ōkami, born as the younger brother of the Sumeru Ōmikami, whose name is high in Takamagahara, was shrouded in the mysterious name of a most shallow and wretched Tsumigami. His heart was clouded like the sky of the fifth month. The five beloved Hime-miko, separated from their parent like fledglings, were taken captive by the foul-hearted Magagami and were pitifully cast into a small boat upon the great ocean of the eight hundred paths of salt and the eight tide-paths. Drifting at the mercy of the waves, they were cast upon the island where the Majin of the Ryūgū, far across the sea, raged—the Jitentōtō, the island of Shakuran, the land of Tokoyo, and the land of Chiri.

Drifting at the mercy of the waves, the five sisters were here, their means of escape lost upon the waves. Moistened by the rain of tears, and beaten by rain, wind, and frost, their wandering was grievous. Among the eight maidens born of the spirit of Ōkami Kamususanō, Eiko-hime, of graceful form and surpassing the others, and Etsuko-hime, of fair countenance and figure, led their attendants in a small, abandoned boat, their lives in peril and decaying. As days and nights passed, and the years reached twice eight, they reached the Wakasa Bay. Leaving the Nariu no Misaki behind, regardless of where their path might lead, they gazed upon the Kyōga-misaki to their right yesterday, and now they are in the Miyazu Bay, the land of Ōkami Kami-izanagi.

Upon the waves, of which it is told that they collapsed while the bridge was sleeping, the long-floating Hashidate was crossed at the Kiritō, and while being blown by the mountain storms of Nariahi, the Kokochi at last reached the roots of the Riutōmatsu, where the waves of Yosa Sea grow gentle.

The Futari abandoned their boat and at last made Jiyōriku at the roots of the Riutōmatsu. The Retsupū of the Orikara brushed the Kaimen, and the sound of the Matsukaze blowing across the peaks gave a sense of Hitoshiho Sekireu. In the Yufugure sky where the cold pierces the body, a flock of several thousand crows, returning to seek their nests, flew toward the Haumen of Ōeyama while crying 'kawai, kawai.' The robes of the clouds were torn, and from Tokorodokoro the light of the Hoshi began to flicker. The Futari sat down by the Robau, and while worrying over their past and their Yukusue, they spoke in whispers of their Monogata.

Yoshikohime said, 'Hidekohime-sama, when I think of the time since we departed from Kenonkiyō in Mesopotamia, when we were placed upon a rotting boat by a heartless demon and drifted away, it seems like a dream. Even so, I wonder what has become of Kunikohime-sama and the other four princesses... Regarding your safe arrival here, it is hard to fathom whether the four princesses are suffering in their own circumstances, their hearts crushed by the cold winds and the heartless torments of men, like the winter sky that weighs upon my mind. However, as this place is also within the territory of Onigumohiko, if we are careless, it is impossible to tell what further misery we may be forced to endure. Shall we hide ourselves in the forest without a moment's delay, pass the night, and make our way across the mountains toward the sacred land?'

Hidekohime replied, 'Ah, that is so; a long stay is to be feared, and we must do something. Regarding that, I wonder in what far-off place my four sisters are suffering and worrying at this time.'

So saying, she tilted her head and sank into tears for a while. After a time, Hidekohime raised her head and said, 'Ah, I must not think of it, I must not think of it; all things are but a fleeting moment, and there is no path but to leave them to Kannagara. Come, Yoshikohime, let us make haste.'

Just as she was about to stand, the voices of four or five men approached; at the moment they were startled and about to flee, by some strange fate, Hidekohime collapsed flat upon that very spot. Yoshikohime groped about, scooped the water of the shore into her hands and held it in her mouth, and aiming at the face of Hidekohime, she blew upon her the mist of Ibuki; whereupon Hidekohime at last raised her face and said, 'Ah, Yoshikohime-dono, my chronic ailment of cramps has attacked my body once again; now that it has come to this, I cannot walk a single step. It would be a grave matter to be captured by the enemy; do not concern yourself with me, but flee from this place quickly. Come, quickly, quickly.'

As she urged her on from beneath her labored breath, Yoshikohime, while brushing away her tears, spoke thus:

"My Lady, what is it that you say? How could I abandon my master in such peril and flee from this place? Even should we fall into whatever fate may befall us, the words you once decreed—that master and servant shall share life and death together, and that in the future we shall surely be bound as one lotus—are deeply carved into my heart; there has not been a single moment that I have forgotten them. I beseech you, let me attend to you."

As she wept and prostrated herself, Hidekohime replied:

"Ah, Yoshikohime, you who will not listen to reason. I am a body of infirmity; even if I were to escape this place safely, it is hard to tell when this frail woman's body might be stricken by illness or captured by the Akugami. You must depart from this place as quickly as a single moment allows, and proceed toward the Seichi."

Yoshikohime shook her head and said:

"No, no, no matter what you may say, I cannot endure to leave this place."

"Ah, you who will not listen to reason, do you defy the words of your master? From this moment forth, I sever the bond of master and servant."

"My Lady, those words are heartless, to speak of severing our bond..."

Before she could finish, a double-edged Tantau flashed in the darkness. Hidekohime, forgetting the body that suffered from illness, swiftly gripped Yoshikohime's arm with all her might, and with a tearful voice said:

"Do not be headstrong, Yoshikohime. You are of a healthy constitution; you must live long, meet with my father, and convey the tidings of us sisters. Come, I pray you, compose yourself and depart from this place as quickly as a single moment allows... Look, the voices of men heard near at hand just as I said; if we are discovered, it will be a great calamity. Quickly, quickly..."

As she urged her on, Yoshikohime replied:

"Even if you say so, how could I possibly overlook this? Even if the bond of master and servant is severed, how could I abandon you and go?"

"It is the request of your master's whole life; I beseech you, depart from this place. Even as we speak, the footsteps of men—come, quickly, quickly."

She urged her on in a low voice. Yoshikohime, feeling as though her hair were being pulled from behind, could not bear to abandon this place; though her heart was divided, her body was but one, and her heart was shattered. Just then, four or five rough men advanced, and with voices like gongs, slightly intoxicated, they spoke:

"Wh-what is this? 'Depart from this place quickly'—to whom are you spewing such words? Is there any question of departing or not departing? I have received orders from the Great General of Ōeyama, and I have appeared here intending to capture the two women who were supposed to drift ashore. There is no such thing as 'departing' or 'nonsense'... Hmph, do not make a fool of me. You, Ishikuma, whenever it grows dark, you always make the eerie, unpleasant sound of a woman weeping... is that not unmanly?"

So saying, he clenched his Nigirikobushi and struck a Hitori man squarely upon the side of his face.

Ishikuma cried out, 'Ouch, hey, you Onitora, do not make a Baka of me!'

And again, Ishikuma said, 'Come, here is your Henrei,' and no sooner had he spoken than he struck Onitora repeatedly upon the side of his face, beating him with such force that his arm seemed near to breaking.

At that moment, Onitora staggered and reeled, falling with a heavy thud upon the Futari maidens, and Onitora cried out, 'Woe, how terrifying! ...Hey, hey, they have come forth, those with long hair... those of blue hue, those cold ones. Hey, all you Renjiu, will you not take me and flee?'

From the darkness, Ishikuma said, 'Ahahaha, look well, you Okubiyōmono... A Hitori man said in an Ōgoe, 'Itsutai, for what purpose do you Nanjira receive Takusan of Teate and walk about on Teisatsu? What is the matter with those legs of yours? Is it possible that at the Kanjinkaname, the Shiyōnenba, you should be unable to stand? Can you not pull yourselves together?'

§ 5

Ishikuma said, 'What are you babbling about? Do you not hold your Yakume in high regard? If two maidens were to come hither under the cover of darkness, what do you intend to do, Kisama? They say that Aitsu is a woman of skill, ill-suited to her sex. According to the Kifuhau from Torahiko of Kyōgamisaki, and the urgent Gomeirei from Onikumohiko, if you do not steady yourself, you shall be undone by Abekobe. A-tsu, e-tsu, ga, ga-a, gara-gara-gara-gara.'

Kumataka said, 'Ah, how foul it reeks! To pour a torrent of mixed sake and rice upon a man's face... it makes my chest ill... Aye, it seems I, he-he-he-he, am about to vomit. Oi, Kora, Otora... O-o, bring the bucket... strike my back, ga-a-a-a-a, gara-gara-gara-gara.'

Onitora said with a trembling voice, 'O-o-oi, why are you being Guzuguzu? Will you not come quickly? Carry me upon your shoulders and flee. Some Ketai, Bakeshiu has appeared, zo.'

Ishikuma said, 'E-e, do not babble so noisily... A Daikōzui has issued from my mouth, and the Jinka are almost entirely Riushitsu, with Shishiyō beyond counting—a scene of such Sanjiyō. Is this a place to save you, Kisama? Should you not dispatch the Hijiyōgumi to Kurida and head for Kiuen? Gara-gara-gara-gara.'

Kumataka said, 'E-e, how strange and ill-omened, it is a night that defies Gaten. Just as I thought I had come this far, my feet suddenly stopped dead, and I have become as a tree grown from the earth... Ya-i, will you not do something to make my feet move?'

From the darkness, a voice replied, 'Even if you say to make them move, I have no saw at hand to cut them from the roots. Well, it would be best to endure until winter comes, the leaves scatter, and you become a withered tree. If you do so, then—though it is not the Sangōkyō—perhaps cold flowers shall bloom upon the withered wood. Waha-ha-ha-ha.'

Kumataka said, 'E, e, everyone and Aitsu, they are all fellows with weak loins, are they not?'

Onitora said, 'Ya-i, all of you, it seems this is the pair of maidens who are our objective. Let us steady ourselves and take them alive, shall we?'

Ishikuma said, 'Na, na, what is this? Kisama, you who have been babbling that your loins were paralyzed from the start, have you been lying soundly upon the two beautiful women? You are a cunning fellow, indeed.'

Onitora said, 'My eyes are not yet paralyzed, but my loins are completely so... Is there no one whose loins are not paralyzed who will come forth and bind this one? It seems she is suffering from a spasm; if we bind her now, it will be an easy task... Kora, Ishikuma, Kumataka, come quickly and capture her.'

§ 6

Kumataka said, 'For some reason the weather is ill today, and I am displeased... Oh, it is the Meinichi of my Oba, so perhaps I shall cease this Setsushiyō... Hey, you, Ishikuma, today is your turn; I shall yield the Tegara to you.'

Ishikuma replied, 'I, too, feel no inclination today; it is the Meinichi of my Niyōbau, so perhaps I shall cease this Setsushiyō.'

Kumataka laughed, 'Ha, ha, ha, you have never even had a Niyōbau, so where would you have a Meinichi? Do not make a Baka of me.'

Ishikuma answered, 'Do you not know of my Niyōbau? In total there are Jifuhachinin, and among them, my most precious Oharu died on this day; she is the Niyōbau of my spirit. Ah, when I think of it, what a Kahaisau thing I have done; boo-hoo.'

Onitora shouted, 'What are you spouting? Look, that is the Niyōbau of Hachibee next door; even if one speaks of a Machiga, where is there a fellow who would Torichiga his own wife?'

Ishikuma said, 'I had made her my Niyōbau in my own heart as I pleased. Wah, wah, wah, when I think of it, what an Iji thing I have done; boo-hoo.'

Hidekohime said, 'Oh, Yoshikohime-dono, I have become well through your kind Kaihō. Come, let us Futari go together.'

'Ah, that is a most joyful thing; this is all entirely due to the honorable father-god Susanoo-no-Mikoto...', said Yoshikohime.

Hidekohime, in a low voice, restrained her, saying, 'Shitsu,' and placed her hand over her mouth; Yoshikohime, with immediate wit, continued:

'This is all entirely because the fermentation of the sake has become wild, so everyone is performing an entertainment for us just like that. If only the moon would rise here, it would surely be a most interesting sight... but to have only voices is not a spectacle; it is the teaching of the God to see with the ears and hear with the eyes, ho, ho, ho.'

Kumataka said, 'Hey, hey, hey, you are the daughter of Susanoo-no-Mikoto, are you not? What are you spouting? Seeing with the ears and hearing with the eyes—you are a creature spouting things like a hag-monster. Hey, woman, do not move from there!'

Yoshikohime replied, 'Oh, ho, ho, ho, everyone, even if you tell me not to move, for some reason my body moves of its own free will and I cannot help it; you yourselves probably cannot move even if you wish to, for I have placed a spiritual binding upon you, you see. Well then, please relax and drink your fill, and spend the night awake... If that is the case, everyone, I am sorry, but I shall take my leave first... Now, if you please, Himegimi-sama, come this way.'

Hidekohime spoke: 'Ho, ho, ho, Minasama, pray be at ease; though there is naught to offer, I have gathered these scraps, so pray, partake of them. That which issues from within your own body, you shall return unto your own body. It is said that if one curses another, one must dig two graves; it returns to the self from whence it came. Oh, how Arigata is the protection of the Kamisama.'

As she sought to depart, Onitora, with Itsushiyōkenmei, grasped the hem of Hidekohime’s garment and would not let go.

Hidekohime said: 'Oh, how detestable; this man grasps my hem and will not release it in the slightest.'

Yoshikohime said: 'What shall we do? Ah, yes, it must be that this man was struck by Reibaku while still clutching the hem of the Himegimisama, and thus he has become frozen in that state. Ho, ho, ho, this is a grave Buchōhafu indeed. Come now, this arm like that of an ogre, I shall release the Reibaku; come, let go.'

With a single Hitokoe of 'Un,' the clenched fist of Onitora fell open.

Hidekohime said: 'Ah, Arigata, I am released at last.'

Ishikuma said: 'Hey, hey, that fellow Onitora, just as I suspected, he was pulling at a woman’s hem; what a dull-witted fellow he is. Very well, I shall file a Kokuhatsu against your wife first thing Miyōchō; prepare yourself for that Kakugo.'

Kumataka said: 'What, do not feel Shinpai; I shall become your Tokubetsu Bengonin and rattle off a Benron with such eloquence that Ishikuma shall surely face Haiso. Once you are granted Muzai Haumen, shall we then file a claim for Songai compensation against them? Aha, ha, ha, ha.'

Hidekohime and Yoshikohime, blending into the darkness, vanished into the unknown. Behind them, the sound of the wind blowing over the sea surface resounded like a heavenly drum, booming with a thunderous beat. The five men, from the darkness, raised their voices like torn drums, crying out:

'Oi, oi, you two women, wait a while. Oi, oi, return, come back...'

Thus they shouted, mimicking the manner of Kumagai.
