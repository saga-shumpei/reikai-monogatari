Chapter 7: 空籠 [597]

空籠〔597〕

§ 1

Moved by the Sendenka into which Akiyamahiko had poured his heart, Onihiko, Onitora, Ishikuma, Kumataka, and the others repented of their past wrongs from the depths of their souls; they prostrated themselves before the party, joined their Riyōte, and, steadying their breath with the uncertain Kototama, followed behind Akiyamahiko to recite the Amatsunorito, chanting the Sendenka to manifest their intent of Kijiyun.

Onihiko spoke: 'We offer our Orei to Susanōnomikoto and all the Goichidō. You have saved us, who are but vessels of Akuma and Zainin beyond forgiveness, from our Kinan; furthermore, you have aided us, whose Shintai are infirm, through the Daiji and Daihi of the Ōkami and the Ōmikokoro. We know not how to express our gratitude, but henceforth we shall repent from the depths of our hearts. Pray, I beseech you, depart from this place with all haste. If you proceed deep into this Makutsugahara, you shall find the rocky cavern dwelling of Onikumohiko, and beyond it, his magnificent main castle. Should you venture there, many wicked ones lie in wait with arms prepared, and I fear that even you, noble and valiant deities, may suffer some hardship. I pray you, heed our words and flee from this place.'

He offered this counsel with his sincerity manifest upon his countenance. Kamehiko, half in mockery, replied:

'Ho, General Onihiko, your wit turns quite well, does it not? This is a cunning stratagem to feign kindness, repel our brave commanders, and snatch a moment of respite. Having attempted to torment our party here, you have met with immediate divine retribution, only to be thwarted by your own stones and arrows, suffering a miserable defeat and learning your lesson, it seems. However, we are by no means like the Brahmins, nor like Onikumohiko, who wear the mask of virtue to disturb the world. We shall now meet with Onikumohiko and compel him to repent from the depths of his heart, just as you have done. From this moment, bind our party hand and foot as before, and, though it be a burden, carry us in this Ajirokago.'

Onihiko replied: 'Nay, that would be disastrous. Should we welcome deities such as you into the main castle, it would bring about a cataclysm that would shake heaven and earth; the interior of the castle at Ōeyama would be reduced to chaos and ruin, a tragic scene of scattered petals and dust, as clear as an object reflected in a bright mirror. I beseech you, I beseech you, pray withdraw from this place.'

Kamehiko said: 'You are still favoring Onikumohiko, are you not? Ah, how admirable, how admirable. To show such devotion to one whom you once relied upon as your general is indeed the manifestation of human sincerity. Nevertheless, our hearts are set upon this course, and to turn back our horses midway is something a man cannot endure. If you will not listen, we shall continue our forced march from here and advance upon the main castle of Ōeyama.'

§ 2

As they began to walk slowly, Onihiko uttered a cry of lamentation.

"Alas, alas, it is a great calamity; however heroic you, noble deities, may be, I perceive the bitterness of your struggle, being but a few against a multitude," he said, restraining them from the sincerity of his heart.

The subordinates, one and all, displayed countenances of astonishment and gazed upon the face of Onihiko.

Kamehiko spoke: "What is this? Do not utter such effeminate words; are you not the man who was called the left-hand guardian of Onikumohiko? Furthermore, what is the meaning of this? To raise a ruinous cry of sorrow and fill your eyes with tears of despairing grief to stop us—I cannot fathom your intent. There must be some deep stratagem in this. We, the party, possess the right to walk through the mountains at our own free will. Come, everyone, let us advance. Even if Onikumohiko should possess a great army of a million to defend and fight, I, Kamehiko, alone am sufficient. As a strong wind stirs up clouds of dust, by the breath of my single word, I shall cause them to repent from their very foundations and transform the den of demons into a heavenly paradise. Ha, how amusing, how valiant!"

So he muttered to himself, squaring his shoulders with a spirit of ten thousand fathoms that none could withstand, stamping his feet and roaring with martial vigor.

Onihiko said: "Kamehiko-sama, you are raising your meter with a terrible intensity."

Kamehiko replied: "Oh, indeed, it is the meter of the enemy's imminent destruction; do not think that I am unaware of your attempt to hinder my advance by means of this meter. What do you take me, the Jean-Pierre, to be?"

Onihiko said: "Jean-Pierre? I know not if it be a Jean-Pierre horse, but you are truly a person who concocts such Jean-Pierre."

Kamehiko said: "Agh, it smells of Jean-Pierre; if we dally, the meter that has risen with such effort will become Pierre. Come, let us go."

And he attempted to set forth once more.

Onihiko said: "Ah, though I kindly entreat you not to go, you show every sign of wishing to proceed regardless; now that it has come to this, though I would go, I cannot go."

Kamehiko said: "Eh, is this a time for jests? Without spewing such drivel, why do you not bind me and carry me to the main castle?"

Onihiko said: "Th-th-that is the very calamity. Had I been the man I was until now, no matter how much you wished to go, I would have taken you and claimed the merit; but now that I have awakened to the causality of heaven and earth and repented of evil, how could I remain silent? The nature of man is good; I shall never speak of evil things."

§ 3

Kamehiko exclaimed, 'Ah, what a pack of helpless cowards! Onikumohiko must find it no ordinary task to sustain such a company; when I consider it, I find the heart of Onikumohiko truly pitiable.'

At this moment, five men wearing wigs with two horns protruding appeared, carrying spears and walking with a slow, heavy gait into the place.

The men spoke, 'Hail, General Onihiko, what a feat, what a feat! Come, we shall now guide you; General Onikumohiko awaits with neck outstretched to learn of the situation, for it must have been a most arduous labor for you.'

So saying, they peered one by one into the palanquin.

The men cried, 'What is this? Is it not an empty palanquin? What has become of Susanōnomikoto and the others? Was it not reported that you had successfully captured them alive?'

Onitora, Kumataka, Ishikuma, and Onihiko looked about them, and lo, how strange it was; the figure of Kamehiko, who had been boasting so loudly until now, and the shadows and forms of Susanōnomikoto, Kunitakehiko, and their party had all vanished.

Onihiko said, 'Ah, this is a mystery. Where have the divine men—nay, the prisoners—whom we brought in this wicker palanquin hidden themselves? It is a matter beyond my comprehension.'

Kumataka replied, 'Come, this is the notorious Makutsugahara; perhaps invisible demons have emerged and devoured them while we were unaware, or perhaps they have naturally vanished, fearing the might of General Onikumohiko. In any case, it is a matter beyond my comprehension. Ho, you five gentlemen, return to the main castle at once and report this matter with haste.'

One of the five, widening his eyes, said,

'What is this you say? I cannot comprehend your command to report with haste.'

Kumataka declared, 'I am not the Kumataka of yesterday; today I am a noble believer of the Sangōkyō. From here, I shall launch a counterattack upon the main castle, wring off the bare neck of Onikumohiko, and bring the spirits of the Yamata-no-Orochi to submission through Kototama, one by one; the raising of our victory cry shall be but a fleeting moment. You must depart this place at once and fail not to prepare for defense against our approaching forces.'

The five, all at once, looked suspicious and asked,

'Is... is that truly the case?'

Kumataka answered, 'The truth or falsehood shall soon be known; depart this place with haste.'

Awestruck by this vehemence, the five exchanged glances and said,

'What is this? Both General Onihiko and Brother Kumataka, and the faces of all the others, have completely lost their stern composure; their former demonic masks have suddenly transformed, falling into the countenances of radiant goddesses. How troublesome this is! Does one become such a spineless creature when one falls into the path of virtue?'

§ 4

The Gonin turned upon their heels and fled in haste, pointing toward the distance. Onihiko stood up with a start, having finished chanting the Sendenka of the Ananai-kyō, and spoke: 'Come, all you people, how is it? Do you not feel as though the wind has been taken out of your sails?'

The Ichidō replied: 'Indeed, it is so. The evil heart that we had strained to maintain until now has vanished like a fart in the water, bubbling away into nothingness. Everyone has become like Amazake from which the alcohol has evaporated. Kamehiko offered his Iken, but that too lacked any firm foundation; it was a speech neither sweet nor spicy, neither severe nor lenient, and altogether incomprehensible. It was just like drinking Amazake with the juice of Shiyōga added to it. It felt as though one were receiving money on the sly while listening to the Kowaiken of an Oyaji. Come now, as a Kinen of our Nifushin, we must hasten to the Honjiyō of Ōeyama and extract the soul of the Akushin—nay, the neck of Onikumohiko—to save him.'

The Ichidō clapped their hands to express their agreement, and with Onihiko in the lead, chanting the Sendenka, they advanced, leaping over the desolate wilderness where the winter wind raged and crossing valleys to the right and left. At that moment, a man and a woman suddenly appeared from the thicket, and catching sight of Onihiko and his party, they laughed coldly and said:

'Ha! Are you not the hero and man of valor of Ōeyama of whom we have heard? Yet, what is this appearance of yours today? Is it not utterly ruined? With that indistinct countenance, like a marble-less bottle of Ramune, have you been deceived by a fox? Or perhaps, have you been taken in by the glib tongue of the Akushin of Susanoo-no-Mikoto, and had your gall stolen and your loins weakened? In any case, it is a senile figure and a senile soul that defies understanding. To think that you, Onihiko and his company, would return so sluggishly, having been so easily toyed with by the enemy—it is truly pathetic. The great commander Onikumohiko will surely rejoice greatly. He will likely shed tears like acorns, saying that a man is fortunate to have such retainers, and rejoice. Ahahaha! Ohohoho!'

They laughed until they rolled on the ground. Onihiko, with a sullen face, replied: 'Hey, I know not who you are, but we have merely exercised our own rights of freedom. This is no place for the likes of you to interfere. If you continue to babble, shall I unleash the power of Kototama upon you? Shall I strike your cheeks with an iron fist—nay, a rice cake—like a sea snail?'

'Ahahaha! Ohohoho! Hey, everyone, come this way. Come, quickly!'

As they tore open the rock, a staircase was revealed within.

Onihiko said: 'Hey, this is a path we always take, yet I never knew until now that there was a hole in such a place. This is strange. Hey, Onitora, Kumataka, Ishikuma, and all the rest of you, shall we set out to explore this cavern for the sake of observation?'

Onitora replied: 'That should be interesting,' and went down first.

Driven by curiosity, the hundreds of rough men, without a single one remaining, tumbled into the cavern with a sound like a mountain of pond snail shells being poured out from above. Suddenly, Onitakehiko, who had appeared, shut the stone lid tight and placed a thousand-man-pulling rock upon it with a thud, saying: 'Ahahaha! Well, now we shall be at peace for a while.'
