Chapter 20: 思はぬ歓 [610]

思はぬ歓〔610〕

§ 1

The great orb of fire and light that fell to the foot of the Riutōmatsu and exploded became countless orbs of varying sizes, which, as one watched, diminished in volume until they transformed into small, luminous gems resembling gold, silver, Suishiyō, Ruri, Menau, Hisui, and Kuōgiyoku, which then linked together like a rosary to encircle the entire body of Yoshikohime, and in an instant, as if absorbed into her body, they permeated her completely.

In that very moment, a divine Shinkaku beyond expression was added to Yoshikohime, and within her gentleness, she possessed an Igen that none dared to violate; her Kotoba suddenly attained a degree of Sauchiyō, and at first glance, she appeared to be a Betsujin, having reached the state of emitting infinite Reikuō from her entire body.

All those present opened their eyes in wonder, tilted their heads, and praised her with the utmost fervor.

Yoshikohime stood up with solemn dignity and spoke: 'Ah, all of you, I have bathed my body in the divine spirit of the Hinodewake. From here, let us proceed toward Manai-ga-take. As the remnants of the evil spirits of Ōeyama are scattered here and there in our path, I pray you all exercise twelvefold caution; I shall depart a step ahead of you, farewell.'

No sooner had she spoken than she vanished from sight as swiftly as an arrow in flight.

Left behind to watch her go, the company could only exhale sighs of 'Ah, ah, ah.'

Otohiko said, 'Though we have traveled together thus far, Yoshikohime has bathed in infinite divine virtue and departed, leaving us behind. It is truly a letdown. To be left behind by Yoshikohime, the single red flower in the midst of ten thousand greens, a beauty that puts flowers to shame, is a fine humiliation; we men of seven feet are left with no face to show.'

Iwakō replied, 'It is truly so; one would think she might have taken at least Iwakō along as a companion, it is quite cold of her.'

Otohiko said, 'A simple-hearted person like you would not suffice, which is why she did not take you; even I, Otohiko, have been left behind.'

Iwakō asked, 'Why would she not take us, who are simple-hearted just as you, sir, have described, like newborn babes?'

Onitora said, 'Iwakō, you are a remarkably naive fellow; when Otohiko-sama said simple-hearted, he was euphemistically rephrasing the fact that you are a dull-witted person with fine words. To be truly simple-hearted means to be a human being like a primitive dog or cat, who has not undergone the training of society.'

Iwakō replied, 'Do not talk nonsense; Otohiko-sama is a propagandist with spurs, a refined and polished propagandist of the San-kyō, covered in the moss of an antique. He would never say such reckless things; hey, Onitora, that is your own twisted nature. Though I, Iwakō, may look this way, I interpret all things with good intentions; if one takes things with malice, everything ends up becoming evil. It seems you cannot cross the slope of repentance.'

Onitora said, 'Even so, Ihakō, consider it well; is it not a matter of Fushigisenban? Did not the balls of fire divide into countless numbers, and in the end, did they not all Senchiyaku upon the beautiful Yoshikohime? Oh, the Tenchi no Kamisama do not hear me. Father, Mother, why did you not give birth to me as a Zetsusei beauty? I resent it bitterly; on, on, on.'

Onihiko said, 'Hahaha, what are you spouting, you fellow Onitora? With a face like the Iurei of an o-iwa, it is certain that not only the Kamisama, but even the monsters of Ōeyama would hesitate and retreat if they were to behold your Gomensau. Onitora said, 'Every time I speak, I am struck down; could you not try to place yourself in my position? I have received a Hijidetsupau from the Kame next door, been spurned by O-hachi, and abandoned by my wife; what an Inguwa birth I have had. The only reason I became a Shinjiya of the Ananai-kyō was in the hope of becoming a beautiful man, to be a Zetsusei beauty of the world, and to draw the gaze of all upon this Onitora. Ah, ah, can the Kamisama do nothing about one's face and figure? It is pathetic; if this is all there is, it would be better to die.'

All together said, 'Hahaha.'

Onihiko said, 'The right to sorrow and agony is your own freedom; we shall neither suppress nor interfere. It would be best to open the curtain of your lamentations to the fullest and offer it for us all to behold; whether we watch or not is, in turn, our own freedom and right. Hahaha.'

Otohiko said, 'Ya, the night has cleared completely; come, with the sun upon our backs, shall we continue our journey?'

Kamehiko said, 'Come, from the Ryūtō-matsu as our starting point, to Iwataki, it is a marathon race. Tighten your belly bands firmly, tie your straw sandals securely, and run so as not to fall behind midway. One, two, three.'

§ 3

The company, aiming for Ihataki, whipped their legs and ran with all their might.

Ihakō, from the rear, cried out, 'Hey, wait for me! Will you leave me behind alone? Is it right to treat a precious Ningensama, whom the Kamisama created with such care, so poorly as to leave him cast aside on the road? Hey, hey, put this one Ningen into your sleeve and run with me! It seems some part of my Kautsūkikuwan has suffered Sonshiyō, for I cannot walk well.'

'Be silent, you noisy one! If we dally, others will take the Ketsushiyōten,' they replied, and with Itsushiyōkenmei, they dashed off, turning into clouds and mist without looking back.

Ihakō muttered, 'Ah, what fools. They endure such unnecessary Shinrau. From here, I shall board a boat, cross the Ama no Hashidate, and arrive at Ihataki before them. Shall I give those fellows a fright and startle them out of their wits?'

So saying, Ihakō untied the rope of a boat moored beneath a pine tree, and while maneuvering the oar, he glided leisurely toward Ihataki; the boat soon arrived at Ihataki.

Ihakō said, 'Ah, how pitiful are those who lack wisdom. Thanks to my having been a boatman in the past, I am well-versed in geography. No matter how much they run on a path as different as a bow is from its string, how could they ever hope to catch up? Well, I shall leisurely climb Mount Nariai and perhaps perform a mata-nozoki.'

At this moment, the party of Oto-hiko arrived, breathless and panting.

Oto-hiko said, 'Come, come, everyone, let us rest for a moment; we have run quite a distance.'

Oni-hiko replied, 'I have sweated profusely. Even so, that fellow Ihakō must be lagging behind on the road, complaining and crying, "Hey, hey, are you leaving me behind?" It is a nuisance to have a weakling in tow; as he is a cripple by nature, he is unfit for a marathon.'

Oni-hiko continued, 'Because one of Ihakō's legs is short, when he runs, he looks exactly like an octopus fleeing from a potato field; it is truly a strange and bizarre sight, ha ha ha.'

Ihakō, thrusting only his head out from within the thicket of trees, said,

Ihakō said, 'It is not that one of Ihakō's legs is short; it is that one of them is long.'

Oni-hiko exclaimed, 'Ha, how uncanny! Is that not the voice of Ihakō? When did you arrive? You are like a specter.'

Ihakō replied, 'Hmph, do not mock me. Because one of my legs is long, my march is that much faster than yours. Furthermore, because Princess Etsuko secretly placed a jewel into my bosom, I have arrived here safely as if walking on air. Ha ha ha.'

Onitora said, 'Ihakonsu at the lead? We may be entirely and thoroughly at ease. Ha, ha, ha, ha!'

Ihakō said, 'A faint light may be seen a little way ahead; if we go there, there is a large thatched house. Let us knock at the door and ask to be lent lodging for the night. Come, but one more effort.'

Leading the way, they ran forth with alacrity, and the Ichidō at last arrived before a certain house.

Ihakō stood at the gate and cried out, 'Pray, good grandfather and grandmother, I am one who makes pilgrimage to the deities of Hinu, Manai, and Hijiyama; I have trudged hither from Riutōmatsu, but the sun has set entirely, and our knees no longer obey our command. I beseech you, even a corner of your garden shall suffice, that we may find shelter from the dew and rain for a single night.'

The grandfather said, 'Hearken, O Nara, it seems there is a human voice at the gate; go and open it to see who is there.'

O-Nara replied, 'Heisuke, do you still bid me go buy more, even after you have drunk so much sake? It is unbearable for me to go out now that it has grown so dark. If I were to encounter Onitora, a retainer of Onikumohiko of Ōeyama, as we did last year, there is no telling what manner of fate might befall me; has not our own daughter been snatched away by that very Onitora? Woe, woe, woe!'

Heisuke sighed, 'Ah, it is a trial to have a wife who has grown old and deaf. Ah, there is no help for it; I shall go and open it myself. Heave-ho, ouch, my hip bones are stiff, and it is no small matter for me to walk even through the garden.'

So saying, he took up his staff from the side, tottered out to the front, and slid the door open with a clatter.

'What business have you here in this darkness?'

Otohiko answered, 'Indeed, we are those who make pilgrimage to the deity of Hijiyama; as you see, the day has closed, and we beseech you to let us stay for but one night, even in a corner of your garden. We have brought our own provisions; we ask only for shelter.'

Heisuke replied, 'I see a great company of you, and I perceive you have come here counting upon this solitary house in the field. It is absolutely impossible for me to lodge you; come, come, depart at once. We are but an old man and an old woman living alone, and it would be most inconvenient; I must humbly decline.'

Otohiko said, 'That may be so, yet I implore you earnestly; we are surely not suspicious persons.'

Heisuke continued, 'It was about this time last year that two travelers appeared at our gate after the sun had set, just as you have, and begged to stay even in a corner of the garden. § 6

With a sharp sound, he shuts the door.

Otohiko said, 'Ah, what a predicament; what ought I to do? I have no choice but to spend the night in the open field.'

Ihakō said, 'Excuse me, Otohiko-sama, though it is not the scene of the sushi shop at Senbonzakura, there is a saying that a lack of hospitality can become a form of hospitality. The old man here has once suffered greatly at the hands of you, Onihiko and Onitora, and thus he fears everyone he sees. He is swayed by the preconceived notion that anyone who asks for lodging at nightfall is a kidnapper, and that is why he speaks in such a manner. Since it is said that the power of truth saves the world, let us ask once more.'

He knocks on the door again, and Ihakō said, 'Excuse me, grandfather, we are men of pure sincerity who have come accompanying the Sendenshi of Ananai-kyō. Please, I beseech you, let us stay for just one night.'

Heisuke said, 'What? Ananai-kyō? I have never heard of such a teaching. Are you searching about like a snake after the equinox, having no hole to hide in? For such people, I refuse all the more to provide lodging. I cannot bear to have my only daughter, Onara, kidnapped and taken away. Do not prattle on, and go home.'

Ihakō said, 'Ah, there is no help for it. Even though I explain the matter so clearly and ask of you, you will not listen. There are demons in this world. It is a world of demons and devils, filled only with those who know neither mercy nor compassion. Hey, Onihiko and Onitora, you two are strangely silent. It is your old scars that speak and cause this hardship tonight. Will you not offer an apology?'

Onihiko said, 'Excuse me, Otohiko-sama, is it not fine even if we do not sleep for a night? Shall we consider this a discipline and spend the night in the open?'

Onitora said, 'Ah, that is right. If one were to perish from spending a night or two in the open, one could not maintain the faith of Ananai-kyō. Tell me, Sendenshi-sama, what do you think?'

Otohiko said, 'If that is so, then let us do that.'

Ihakō said, 'Hmph, you speak conveniently. You must have no face to show the old man and woman. Since you fear your past evils will be exposed, it is no wonder you act this way, but how about you make a clean and thorough apology to the old man and woman? As long as you keep your evil hidden, your sins will not be removed. To sin is to conceal. I beseech you, as proof of your repentance, offer an apology to the old man and let him strike your head with a coal shovel to your heart's content; perhaps then your sins will be somewhat diminished.'

§ 7

Onihiko and Onitora sat with their hands clasped and heads inclined, exhaling deep breaths. At this moment, two women came running from the direction of the front. The group widened their eyes in surprise, and upon looking closely, they beheld Yoshikohime and a young maiden.

The maiden said, 'Oh, my Princess, I am deeply indebted to you for your care. This is the house of my grandfather and grandmother. Pray, do enter; how they shall rejoice to see you.'

Yoshikohime replied, 'Nay, having escorted you thus far, I shall now depart with a tranquil heart.'

The maiden pleaded, 'I beseech you, do not say such things. Though it be but a wretched hovel, I would fain offer you a cup of bitter tea. Even for a brief moment, I pray you, do enter.'

From within the darkness, a voice spoke:

'Is that not the Yoshikohime?'

Yoshikohime answered, 'That voice—is it Otohiko-sama? What are you doing in this darkness?'

Otohiko replied, 'The night has grown so dark that I am beseeching them for a night's lodging, yet the grandfather will by no means grant it.'

Yoshikohime said, 'I have heard the particulars of the situation from this maiden. Though it is past, I must say that Onihiko-sama and Onitora-sama have committed a grievous sin. It is no wonder the grandfather refuses to grant you shelter. I shall now entrust this maiden to her grandfather and intercede on your behalf.'

The maiden called out, 'Grandfather, Grandmother, it is Setsu. I have been saved by the Gods and have returned in safety. I beseech you, open the door.'

Heisuke, startled by this voice, exclaimed, 'What? Setsu has returned? Oi, oi, Onara, Setsu has returned!'

Onara replied, 'Grandfather, my ears do not hear clearly, but did you say Setsu has returned? How strange; I cannot comprehend it. It is likely a stratagem of the kin of the evil spirits of Ōeyama, who have assumed Setsu's voice to enter this house and, taking advantage of our solitude, intend to drag us, the aged couple, away. Do not be careless and open the door.'

From the threshold, Osetsu said in a gentle voice, 'Grandfather, Grandmother, I beseech you, open the door. It is Setsu.'

Heisuke cried, 'What are you babbling? I shall not be deceived by such tricks. You mimic her voice, saying, "Grandfather, Grandmother, it is Setsu"... How could a maiden who was captured by the demons of Ōeyama and made their prey return alive? Hear me, you wretches at the door, Heisuke is not one to be taken in by such schemes. If you wish to enter, then try it. I have prepared a pitfall with nails planted throughout; if you do not value your lives, then force your way in.'

The maiden forcibly pushed open the door and leapt inside. Seeing this, the grandfather exclaimed, 'Ah, it is truly my granddaughter Osetsu! You have returned safely. Oi, stay still, stay perfectly still! It is dangerous to move. If you take one false step, you shall fall into the pitfall. I prepared it in anticipation of the demons of Ōeyama coming here. I shall now guide you, so you must not walk anywhere else.'

So saying, he drew a line in the garden with his staff. Osetsu walked along the line and advanced into the grandfather's living room.

Onara cried, 'Ah, you are Osetsu! You have returned safely!' and she fell down, weeping with joy.

Heisuke also clung to Osetsu's body and asked, 'Have you truly returned? How have you fared?'

Osetsu replied, 'Grandfather, Grandmother, how I have longed to see you!'

The three of them broke into tears together and collapsed in each other's arms.
