Chapter 8: 明志丸 [8]

明志丸〔8〕

§ 1

Mountains and rivers resound and the land shakes; To clear away the clouds of eternal darkness Where evil deities rage, to establish a beautiful Age of the gods, and to form and solidify A golden world, The missionaries of Ananaikyō Who have appeared on Mount Ōgon: Shinonomewake and Aokumowake-no-Mikoto, Gradually enduring A hundred tribulations; By the divine plan, on the plain of Kusu, Six male and female divine ministers Met together on the plain of Areno; Going ever westward to the northern forest, Sheltering through a night of dew, Revering the divine teachings, On their way, in the village on this side Of Lake Akashi, They parted from one another and went their ways, While the brocade-like leaves of the trees had entirely fallen.

Under the winter sky with its cold north wind, The ground a single expanse of silver, Upon the snowy road where she stumbled and fell, The missionary, admirable with the fragrance Of Umegaka that waits not even for spring, Arrived trudging along alone At the shore of Lake Akashi.

The Akashimaru, carrying several dozen passengers, was now on the point of setting sail. Umegaka-hime hurriedly became a passenger aboard the vessel. A bone-rending cold wind blew whistling like a flute, sweeping the surface of the sea. Tossed by the waves, the pitching and rolling of the ship grew increasingly violent moment by moment. Most of the passengers trembled in fear and cold, curling up small and clinging to the bottom of the hold. Among them, four or five men pulled the stoppers from the gourds at their waists and slowly began to drink sake.

A: "The sky is somehow gloomy and the sun cannot properly be seen, with white clouds stretched across like a ceiling. The ground is pure white as far as the eye can see; if one speaks of anything blue, it is only this Lake Akashi and your face. Why don't you gulp down a cup and cheer up?"

B: "No, I cannot drink... You should just drink by yourself."

A: "Hey, Hachikō, you are a drinker, so I will keep you company."

He thrust out a ladle. Hachikō took the ladle, and pouring from the gourd, drank and drank. Gradually the intoxication took hold, and—

Hachikō: "Hey, Katsukō, why do you have such an uncharacteristically crestfallen face? Won't you show a little spirit? Having been tied up by that missionary in the northern forest, your complexion has been terribly pale ever since."

Katsukō: "Shut up, don't talk! I am in the middle of planning a strategy right now. Having received an order from the deity Uralhiko of Armenia, who said that missionaries were sure to come to the northern forest, to tie them up and bring them, I had been staking out day and night every single day. But since, absurdly enough, six of them showed up all at once, even someone as powerful as me was momentarily taken aback. Every single fellow in the village was a coward, trembling in fright, and they all ran away. No matter how rigid and strained I became all by myself, there was simply nothing to be done. From now on, along with making my apologies, I am going to report to the deity of Ural about the worthlessness of the villagers. Hey, you fellows are among the cowards too!"

Yakkō: "Do not speak so haughtily! You were struck with that thing called spiritual binding, turned like a fossil under the cold sky for a whole day and night, merely rolling your eyes and dripping shameful tears in drops, were you not? Even if you speak arrogantly thinking no villagers are here, a witness is right here! If you intend to speak ill of the villagers to Uralhiko, say whatever you please; I am the representative of the whole village, and thus the four of us are going. Since I will report all of your flaws, do not think you will return safely!"

Katsukō: "Do not speak nonsense! No matter how unrivaled in mighty strength Katchan may be, how can one fight when the common foot soldiers are shivering and trembling? Hey, Kamokō, what about you? The sight of you when you panicked, rushed first before the missionary, and showed your back to flee—it was truly an unsightly figure not fit for any picture. Well, without bickering noisily, whether you like it or not, why not drink some sake, cheer up, and make peace here together? Look again, hear again, and decree anew!"

Yakkō: "Hey, Katsukō, if you say such things, you will be done in! Unbeknownst to you, your soul has been captured by the Anaicurami teachings, and are you not spouting things like propagation songs? That thing called the Anaicurami is a teaching that babbles absurd nonsense, about the moon shining or running, snow piling up or not piling up, or the sea overturning without tide. What an easily infected fellow you are—a fellow just like the offspring of lice!"

Katsukō: "What, offspring of lice? Do not make a fool of me! The true head family, the very origin of lice, is Katchan! Just take a look at my head; even a single handful nurtures about one gō of them. They say the creature called the louse laments having no birthplace, but that birthplace is indeed Katchan's head! Everyone and anyone breeds lice, yet instead of saying they bred on their own heads, they blabber that they caught them from someone else; that is why the lice weep, saying they have no birthplace. From this alone, one can well understand this deceitful world."

Kamo, in a low voice:

Kamokō: "Hey, that fellow riding over in that corner wearing a straw raincoat and hat—he seems to be the missionary."

Yakkō: "Yes, that is the fellow who spouts, 'Give me water.'"

Kamokō: "Even if he asks for water, this kind of salt water is not something fit to drink. How about giving him a cup of rice water to drink, and making him dance to relieve our boredom—would that not be amusing?"

Katsukō, saying "Yo," stared with drunken, bleary eyes toward the woman:

Katsukō: "Aha, a lucky catch! Ah, with this, we can make amends for our failure in the northern forest. We are inside the ship; when that fellow goes ashore, let us seize her hands and take her feet from front, back, left, and right, bind her hands behind her back, and take her to Armenia. At any rate, making her drink sake and getting her drunk is the best course."

Kamokō: "That will not do. Those fellows of Anaikyō are ones who proclaim, 'Do not drink or partake of sake!'"

Hachikō: "That is merely on the surface. Where could there be anyone who does not consume sake? They say there is no god who does not partake of sacred sake, so even the gods drink sake. For an emissary of that god to babble that she dislikes sake—that is sheer hypocrisy. Let us waft a delicious aroma before her, and drink, drink, and carouse. Then the missionary will keep flicking her tongue and start licking her lips; at that point, we will thrust it out, saying, 'Hey, sister, have a cup!'"

Kamokō: "I cannot hold liquor, so I do not know the nature of sake, but is it really such a thing?"

The woman missionary abruptly stood up and began to sing a propagation song:

§ 4

Umegakahime said, 'Ho-ho-ho-ho-ho, master Katsu, please listen to the end.'

Katsukō said, 'No, I will not listen! In the midst of this large crowd, spreading shame about whether Lord Katsu won or lost, lowering my dignity as a man! Since it has come to this, it is a matter of pride. Whether I won or lost against you, right here—though not this lake—I must make an akashi (proof) and clear my name. Which is good and which is evil, bright or dark, will soon become clear!'

Saying this, he raised his fist and was about to strike Umegakahime. At that moment, there was a man who grabbed him firmly by the collar hair and held him dangling about two or three feet, as if pinching up a cat.

The man said, 'Ahahaha! Now then, victory or defeat, this is the Lake of Akashi. The moment I release this hand, Katsu will be food for the skipjack tuna.'

Katsukō said, 'Hold on, hold on, wait, wait! When I say wait, you had better wait! It is a life of which there is only one; do you not hold it dear? Even I am a divided spirit of the deity. I am a man who would not flinch even if killed by you, but if you harm this divine shrine of mine, punishment will fall upon you. If you are going to kill me, kill me; I will have my revenge in Hell...'

The man said, 'Do not talk back insolently. For one thing, you have been drinking sake and are quite feverish from drunkenness; so that harmony may be restored, shall I plunge you into the water like a pickled eggplant once?'

Katsukō said, 'Hold on, hold on, please wait! We are the precious people of the sovereign under the same heaven. All within the four seas are brethren!'

The man said, 'This is called the Demon Sea, the Dead Sea; this is a sea where people die. Into this Dead Sea, exactly as ordered, I shall give you a Dead Sea plunge.'

Katsukō said, 'Hey, Hachi, Kamo, why are you dawdling? Can you not trip this fellow's legs? Send this fellow plunging into the Dead Sea!'

Kamokō said, 'Serve you right! Siding with the stronger is the way of the world today. Thinking you were strong, we always said "yes, yes, right away" to your face, yet without your realizing that we were sticking our tongues out behind your back, you acted high and mighty and rampaged through the village—this is your retribution! The sun deity is honest. If you are plunged in with a splash, the northern village will pound rice cakes and celebrate!'

Katsukō said, 'Seeing a person in distress, are you going to leave me to die?'

Hachikō said, 'Leave you to die or whatever, what nonsense!... Excuse me, sir, I do not know who or from where you are, but holding him dangling like that indefinitely must tire your hands, so please go ahead with that Dead Sea plunge you spoke of.'

Katsukō said, 'Hey, even you chiming in with him—what a worthless friend you are!'

The man said, 'Ahahaha! What a weak fellow. In that case, shall I postpone it for a bit as entertainment for later?'

Katsukō said, 'Later is later, now is now. An inch ahead is a dark night, but after the darkness the moon will rise.'

Hachikō said, 'The moon is the moon, but it is the end of your luck; I too have reached the end of my patience with you.'

The man gently lowered Katsukō down into the boat.

Katsukō said, 'Ah, thank you very much. Thanks to you, my life was saved.'

The man said, 'Well, it is merely postponed for a while.'

Hachikō said, 'He is spoken to just like a lapdog.'

The man said, 'Oh, my, my, Princess Umegakahime, to meet you in such a strange place! What has become of everyone else?'

Umegakahime said, 'Ah, was it you, Lord Toki?'
